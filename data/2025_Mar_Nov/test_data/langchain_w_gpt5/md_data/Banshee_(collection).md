# Banshee (collection)

Banshee is the autumn/winter 1994 runway collection by British fashion designer Alexander McQueen, shown during London Fashion Week at the Café de Paris in February 1994. It is widely documented as one of McQueen’s early formative collections and is known for its stark mood, experimental construction, and provocative styling, including molded plaster bodices, military-influenced tailoring, and transgressive knitwear developed with collaborator Julien Macdonald. A highly publicized show piece—a heavy plaster-of-Paris corset worn over a sheer white dress—became emblematic of the collection’s raw theatricality and the designer’s emerging themes of vulnerability and power. Elements from Banshee have since appeared in major museum exhibitions, including Alexander McQueen: Savage Beauty, and in notable public and private archives; the collection is frequently referenced in critical retrospectives of McQueen’s early work and influence on 1990s British fashion [1][2][3][5][6][7][11].

## Scope and medium

The subject referred to by “Banshee (collection)” is a fashion collection: Alexander McQueen’s autumn/winter 1994 ready-to-wear collection presented on the runway in London. It is not a short-story, poetry, art, comics, or music compilation. The exact title of the collection is Banshee, used by McQueen to frame the show and its thematic references [1][2].

## Background and development

Banshee followed the buzz around McQueen’s Central Saint Martins graduation and the early patronage of Isabella Blow, who famously acquired his MA collection, helping to draw attention to his subsequent runway presentations [1]. The period around Banshee was marked by extreme resourcefulness in a small studio and an informal network of collaborators. Former studio director Ruti Danan has described the early years (1994–1996) as cash-strapped but intensely experimental, with garments hand-sewn by McQueen and significant material improvisation; many pieces from these years, including items from Banshee, later became prized archival objects and were included in major museum exhibitions [4].

Julien Macdonald, then a student, produced inventive knitwear for McQueen at this time. One widely cited knit associated with the 1994–1995 Banshee season was a turtleneck with transparent fishing-line panels that revealed the bust; it was devised to shock and to play with constraints and visibility—an early indicator of McQueen’s willingness to subvert craft and social norms through design [9]. Hair stylist Eugene Souleiman worked the show, slicking model hair flat and stenciling “McQUEEN” in silver spray across the head, aligning presentation with the collection’s austere, confrontational mood [1].

## Show and presentation

- Date and venue: February 1994, Café de Paris, London (London Fashion Week) [1][2].
- Mood and casting: The atmosphere was deliberately somber; models were told not to smile. Casting and styling embraced extreme contrasts, including a heavily pregnant model with a shaved head appearing in sheer black mousseline and an Elizabethan neckline, emphasizing McQueen’s early use of provocation and social commentary on bodies and beauty [1].
- Hair and makeup: Hair was slicked close to the head and stenciled with the designer’s name in metallic spray, contributing to a uniform, branded aesthetic across diverse looks [1].

Participants and later commentators have highlighted the show’s intensity and audience engagement; the presence of Blow and the unconventional looks (such as the heavy plaster corset) drew audible reactions and helped cement McQueen’s reputation among London’s fashion insiders [1][2].

## Design, materials, and selected pieces

Banshee synthesized McQueen’s Savile Row-informed tailoring with collision-course materials and historicist references:

- Molded plaster bodices: A signature of the show was a high-necked, hip-length plaster-of-Paris corset formed over chicken wire, worn over a diaphanous white dress. The piece was exceptionally heavy, required assistance backstage, and left welts—illustrating McQueen’s willingness to test the limits of comfort and structure for effect [1][2].
- Military-inflected tailoring: A one-of-a-kind grey military cropped jacket (Look 18) with an extra-high collar and gold braiding, empire-line sleeves with sliced armpits, and peaked accents on black wool cuffs demonstrates McQueen’s technical manipulation of traditional forms. The jacket was hand-sketched, patterned, and stitched by McQueen himself and later featured in Savage Beauty at the Met and V&A [3].
- Knitwear and visibility: The “Get Your Tits Out” turtleneck knit, designed by Julien Macdonald with translucent fishing-line panels, played with exposure and concealment, challenging conventions of propriety and the function of knitwear [9].
- Fabric experimentation: Museum collection records include Banshee pieces executed in wool blends with plastic thread, indicating hybrid material use and an embrace of unusual fiber combinations to achieve sheen, tension, or structural effects [5].

Selected documented pieces
- Plaster-of-Paris corset over sheer white dress [1][2].
- Grey military cropped jacket with high collar and gold braid (Look 18) [3].
- Transparent-panel turtleneck knit by Julien Macdonald [9].
- Wool-blend dress with plastic thread (National Gallery of Victoria, accession 2022.128) [5].

## Themes and influences

Contemporary reporting and later retrospectives describe Banshee as drawing upon Celtic culture and folklore, evoking the banshee’s wail and a gothic romanticism that McQueen would continue to develop. The juxtaposition of military elements with sheer, vulnerable fabrics underscored tensions between protection and exposure, constraint and freedom, cruelty and care—themes often observed across McQueen’s oeuvre. The show’s somber mood, sprayed branding on hair, and confrontational silhouettes further aligned with McQueen’s interest in spectacle, identity, and the politics of display [2][9][11].

## Collaborators and credits

- Designer: Alexander McQueen.
- Knitwear: Julien Macdonald contributed key knitwear pieces to the Banshee season, notably the transparent-panel turtleneck [9].
- Hair: Eugene Souleiman styled the show, including the metallic stencil branding [1].
- Archives and later editorial documentation: Banshee pieces have been photographed and styled in major fashion editorials (e.g., Nick Knight and Katy England’s 2015 Another magazine shoot), reflecting continued critical engagement with the collection’s imagery and construction [3].

## Distribution, availability, and archival holdings

- Museum holdings: In addition to NGV’s Banshee dress, early McQueen pieces from 1994–1996 have been conserved in private archives (e.g., Ruti Danan’s collection), displayed publicly (e.g., pop-up installation at the New-York Historical Society), and auctioned; the Banshee grey military cropped jacket sold for $35,750, reflecting strong collector interest in this formative period [4][3].
- Exhibitions: Banshee pieces appeared in Alexander McQueen: Savage Beauty at the Metropolitan Museum of Art (2011), with the catalogue serving as a principal scholarly reference. The exhibition received extended hours and exceptional attendance, underscoring the public and critical appetite for McQueen’s work and its early collections [6][11].

## Reception

Contemporaneous mainstream reviews of Banshee are limited in accessible archives, but participant accounts and later media retrospectives emphasize the show’s intensity, audacity, and technical inventiveness. ELLE’s Rebecca Lowthorpe recalled the audience’s vocal reactions, the physical demands of the plaster corset, and the stark presentation choices (no smiling; stenciled hair), framing Banshee as an event that set McQueen apart and foreshadowed his trajectory toward theatrical, concept-driven fashion [1]. The Guardian’s visual retrospective has highlighted Banshee’s Celtic inspiration and iconic molded bodices within the context of McQueen’s broader genius across the 1990s and 2000s [2].

The continuing inclusion of Banshee pieces in museum exhibitions and major editorials signals enduring critical recognition of the collection’s role in establishing McQueen’s distinctive vocabulary—melding historicism, technical disruption, and provocations regarding the body and social codes [3][6][11].

## Influence and legacy

- Museum canonization: The appearance of Banshee pieces in Savage Beauty at the Met and V&A placed the collection within the institutional canon of late-20th/early-21st-century fashion innovation [6][3][11].
- Archival and market value: Early McQueen garments from Banshee have become highly collectible, with documented provenance and technical detail enhancing their scholarly and market significance [4][3].
- House continuity: Alexander McQueen’s creative leadership has continued to evoke banshee imagery in later seasons. In spring/summer 2025, creative director Seán McGirr introduced a “banshee hair” embroidery technique—hand-teasing organza with a comb into wisplike textures—explicitly linking banshee folklore to the house’s history and contemporary storytelling [16]. Such revisitations attest to the thematic and narrative potency of the banshee motif within the brand’s identity.

## Disambiguation: other collections titled “Banshee”

Banshee (collection) in this article refers specifically to Alexander McQueen’s A/W 1994 runway collection. Other similarly titled compilations exist but are unrelated in medium and scope:

- Banshee (anthology, 2026): A hardback/ebook/audio Irish mythology short-story anthology edited by Ailbhe Malone, acquired by Renegade/John Murray Press and scheduled for publication on 26 February 2026. Contributors include Jane Casey, Naoise Dolan, Salma El-Wardany, Wendy Erskine, Nikita Gill, Anne Griffin, Sarah Maria Griffin, Jess Kidd, Megan Nolan, and Sheila O’Flanagan [13][12].
- Banshees (comic-book limited series): A five-issue series published by Scout Comics (with ashcan previews and variant covers) about four murdered college students returning as ghosts to uncover their killer. Products include single issues, incentives, webstore exclusives, and printer plate collectibles [14].
- Banshee (music compilation): The soundtrack album “Banshee (Music From the Cinemax® Original Series)” released by Relativity Music Group in 2014 (17 tracks, 56 minutes), tied to the Cinemax television series—not to McQueen’s fashion collection [15].

These distinct uses have their own notability and coverage; the present article addresses the fashion collection by McQueen.
