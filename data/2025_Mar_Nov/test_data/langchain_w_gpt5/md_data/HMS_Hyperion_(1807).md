# HMS Hyperion (1807)

HMS Hyperion was a 32‑gun fifth‑rate frigate of the Royal Navy, built by contract at Kingston upon Hull and launched on 3 November 1807 to a design by Surveyor of the Navy Sir John Henslow [1]. She served during the Napoleonic Wars and in the postwar period on multiple stations, including the Mediterranean, Jamaica/North American and West Indies Stations, and the South America and West Indies Stations. Hyperion’s recorded operations include convoy protection, anti‑piracy patrols, and prize actions, notably the recapture of the Portuguese ship Providencia (11 December 1813) and the capture of the American privateer brig Rattlesnake (June 1814) [2][4][5]. She was paid off in 1831 and broken up in 1833 [1][2]. The vessel is distinct from later Royal Navy ships named Hyperion, including the H‑class destroyer H97 that served in 1939–1940 [6].

## Design and construction

Hyperion was a fifth‑rate, ship‑rigged frigate of nominally 32 guns, built under contract at Kingston upon Hull (Yorkshire) and launched on 3 November 1807 [1]. Her design is credited to Sir John Henslow, with William Gibson listed as the constructor responsible for her building at Hull [1][8]. The hull was ordered on 13 June 1805 and her keel was laid in February 1806, marking a typical mid‑Napoleonic War timeline for frigate construction [1].

Commissioning details, tonnage (bm), dimensions, draught, and complement figures are not specified in the sources cited here; contemporary technical particulars for frigates of this rating and period are documented in standard reference works but are not independently verified in the accessible records used for this article.

## Armament and modifications

- Nominal armament: 32 guns (fifth‑rate frigate classification) [1].
- Equipment modification: A Plymouth Dockyard plan records “Captain Chasman’s method of stoppering chain cables” as fitted to Hyperion, signed by Edward Churchill, Master Shipwright, indicating the fitting of chain‑cable stoppering arrangements during her service life [7].

No detailed refit armament changes are specified in the cited sources; armament arrangements typical of the class and period are not included here unless directly sourced.

## Commanders

The following commanding officers and dates are recorded in compiled service lists and contemporary notices:

- Captain Thomas C. Brodie: in command by mid‑1808; died at Jamaica in March 1811 [2].
- Captain William Pryce Cumby: from September 1811; in command through 1814 [2].
- Captain James Lillicrap: from January 1815 (temporary/acting) [2].
- Captain Thomas Searle: from September 1818; South America Station [2].
- Captain James Lillicrap: from April 1821; Cape of Good Hope and West Indies [2].
- Captain George Rich: from October 1823; Jamaica Station [2].
- Captain William J. Mingaye: from January 1825; Coast Blockade (Newhaven) [2].

Subordinate officers named in action reports include First Lieutenant James Morgan and Lieutenant George Bissett during the Gonaïves incident (1811) [2].

## Service history

### Early deployment and Jamaica Station

- 1808: Fitted out and sailed for the Mediterranean on 30 June under Captain T. C. Brodie [2].
- 1810–1811: On the Jamaica Station; an incident at Gonaïves, Saint‑Domingue (Haiti), saw shore batteries fire on Hyperion’s boats, resulting in casualties and the temporary detention ashore of senior officers, resolved the next morning by bringing the frigate close inshore and issuing an ultimatum for their release (see Notable actions) [2].
- 1811: Command passed to Captain William Pryce Cumby in September; subsequent service confirms Hyperion’s stationing on the North American and West Indies Station (logs for 1811–1813 are catalogued by the National Archives) [3].

### North American and convoy service

- 1812–1813: Reported on the banks of Newfoundland and engaged in convoy duties between Newfoundland, Barbados, and British ports; weather‑dispersed convoys and damaged ships forced arrivals at Plymouth and Portsmouth at various dates late in 1813 [2].
- 11 December 1813: Hyperion re‑captured the Portuguese ship Providencia and cargo; the London Gazette recorded the admiralty‑prize and salvage accounting, naming Captain William Pryce Cumby, with proceeds to be deposited in the Registry of the High Court of Admiralty and Greenwich Hospital [4].

### 1814 operations and War of 1812 prize

- March–May 1814: Movements recorded include preparations for troop convoys towards the Garonne and carriage of specie to Bordeaux [2].
- June 1814: Captured the American privateer brig Rattlesnake. Secondary sources report differing dates—3 June and 14 June 1814—and record that the prize was taken into Plymouth in July; contemporary American and British notices attribute 16 nominal guns to Rattlesnake, with later tallies noting 18 guns and about 50 men at capture [2][5]. The capture is widely listed among Hyperion’s wartime prizes [2][5].

Hyperion continued patrols into early 1815, after which she was paid off and laid up in ordinary at Portsmouth on 31 August 1815 [2].

### Postwar deployments

- 1819–1821 (South America Station): Arrived at Rio de Janeiro from Plymouth (24 October 1819) and was at Valparaíso by August 1820. Admiralty orders in October 1820 authorized detentions under anti‑slave trade treaties on the station [2]. Hyperion paid off on 25 April 1821 and was recommissioned the same month under Captain James Lillicrap [2].
- 1821–1823 (Cape of Good Hope and West Indies): Conveyed Lord Charles Somerset, the Cape Governor, arriving via Madeira; recorded rescue of the Honourable East India Company’s extra ship Albion on 10 June 1822, with subsequent awards to Hyperion’s officers and crew, and further anti‑piracy cruises off Cuba, including delivering suspected pirates to Kingston, Jamaica, in December 1822 [2].
- 1823–1824 (Jamaica Station): Under Captain George Rich, with continued West Indies service; the ship subsequently returned to Britain for repair and reassessment [2].
- 1825–1831 (Coast Blockade): Stationed at Newhaven, Sussex, for anti‑smuggling duties under Captain William J. Mingaye as part of the Coast Blockade arrangements [2].

## Notable actions

- Gonaïves incident (Saint‑Domingue/Haiti, 1811): Contemporary accounts in service compilations record that Hyperion’s boats were fired upon by shore batteries at Gonaïves, killing three seamen and resulting in the detention ashore of Captain Brodie and others. At dawn, First Lieutenant James Morgan brought Hyperion through an intricate channel and anchored in four fathoms, close to shore; Lieutenant George Bissett was sent ashore to inform the commandant that unless the officers were set at liberty within fifteen minutes, the town would be destroyed. The detained officers were released following the ultimatum [2].
- Recapture of Providencia (11 December 1813): The London Gazette recorded the recapture of the Portuguese ship Providencia by Hyperion, naming Captain William Pryce Cumby and the prize agents, and directed that salvage proceeds and Greenwich Hospital shares be accounted in the Registry of the High Court of Admiralty [4].
- Capture of Rattlesnake (June 1814): Hyperion captured the American privateer brig Rattlesnake in June 1814; sources differ on the exact date (3 or 14 June). Rattlesnake was brought into Plymouth in July and was later sold into mercantile service; the Three Decks entry notes 18 guns and 50 men at capture in British reports [2][5].

## Incidents and records

- Prize court and salvage: The London Gazette provides the official admiralty accounting for Hyperion’s recapture of Providencia, including the allocation to Greenwich Hospital, demonstrating prize‑court procedure and distribution in late 1813/early 1814 [4].
- Logs and archival holdings: The National Archives Discovery catalogue records a Hyperion logbook covering 1811–1813 (North American and West Indies Station), held by the Royal Military College of Canada (Massey Library), with guidance to R. Morriss’s Guide to British Naval Papers in North America [3].

No courts martial or formal inquiries are directly cited in the accessible records used here.

## Fate

Hyperion was paid off and laid up in ordinary at Portsmouth in May–August 1831 after completing Coast Blockade service, and was broken up in June 1833 [1][2].

## Legacy

- Ship name: Hyperion is among several Royal Navy vessels to bear the name. A later ship of the same name was HMS Hyperion (H97), an H‑class destroyer that served in 1939–1940 and was lost off Cape Bon; the destroyer’s record is distinct and should not be conflated with the 1807 frigate [6].
- Material culture: A Plymouth Dockyard plan for Hyperion records a chain‑cable stoppering method fitted to the ship; the plan is signed by Edward Churchill, Master Shipwright, and survives as a technical illustration of equipment fitted to the frigate [7].
- Archival footprint: Hyperion’s logs for 1811–1813 survive in an overseas repository noted by the National Archives Discovery service, evidencing her North American and West Indies service [3].
