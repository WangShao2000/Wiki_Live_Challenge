# Natural Dis-tinction, Un-Natural Selection

Natural Dis-tinction, Un-Natural Selection is the Spring/Summer 2009 womenswear ready-to-wear collection by British fashion designer Alexander McQueen. It was presented on 3 October 2008 at Centquatre (Le 104) in Paris and framed around Darwinian evolution and the human impact of industrialization on the natural world [1][9][10]. The runway was staged amid antique taxidermy and a rotating projection of the Earth, with an invitation featuring a lenticular portrait of McQueen morphing into a skull designed by Gary James McQueen [1]. The collection drew broad critical coverage from major fashion media and museums, and has been noted for pioneering digitally engineered prints whose placement aligned precisely to garment pattern pieces; pieces from the show have since featured in major museum exhibitions, including the Victoria and Albert Museum’s Savage Beauty and LACMA/NGV’s Mind, Mythos, Muse [1][3][11].

## Background

Natural Dis-tinction, Un-Natural Selection extends McQueen’s longstanding engagement with nature—its forms, mechanics, and symbolism—and situates those interests within a contemporary environmental discourse. McQueen’s collection notes and subsequent curatorial writing anchor the work in reflections on Charles Darwin, the Industrial Revolution, and the destructive potential of human greed, while balancing an avowed refusal “to preach” with a clear ecological concern [1]. Museum scholarship has frequently linked McQueen’s practice to broader historical dialogues between fashion and the natural world; the Museum at FIT, for instance, explicitly references the Spring 2009 show in its treatment of evolution and fashion’s changing attitudes toward nature [8].

## Development and concept

According to curatorial accounts, the collection was conceived in two sequential parts that juxtaposed an organic, romantic vision of nature with a synthetic, industrial reinterpretation of natural forms [1]. Program notes and reviews framed McQueen’s thinking around Darwin’s theory of natural selection and the “deleterious results of industrialization on the natural world,” with a contemporary emphasis on print and color [4]. 

- The first sequence emphasizes organic shapes, soft colors, and natural fibers, using digitally engineered prints of wood grain and meadow flowers tailored to specific garment pattern pieces [1].  
- The second sequence hardens the aesthetic: natural motifs are reimagined with a synthetic edge through angular prints of crushed crystal, references to industrial structures (including the Eiffel Tower), and a granite mountain image derived from photographer Dan Holdsworth [1]. 

McQueen’s adoption of digitally engineered prints at a couture level—carefully placing motifs to idealize silhouette and visual effect—was highlighted by museum curators as pioneering and influential in the wider fashion industry [3].

## Design and content

The design vocabulary spans McQueen’s signature silhouettes and experimental materiality, articulated through the collection’s two-part structure [1][2][4]:

- Organic sequence:  
  - Digitally engineered prints of wood grain and flowers integrated with historical simplicity in single-piece dresses [1].  
  - Silk flowers trapped beneath transparent tulle referencing Victorian specimen jars [1].  
  - Whitework embroidery and embellishments such as beetle-wing sequins; metallic buttercups finished with acid-yellow enamel [1].  
  - Tailoring softened via wood-print suiting, with draping and fringing described in contemporary reviews as among the season’s most notable [4].

- Synthetic/industrial sequence:  
  - Angular, kaleidoscopic prints evoking crushed crystal and architectural structures (e.g., Eiffel Tower), alongside geological imagery (a Dan Holdsworth granite mountain) [1].  
  - A diamond print morphing into a human skeleton motif [1].  
  - Short “bell-jar” dresses and bodysuits densely encrusted with jet, gold, and silver Swarovski crystals to produce a sharp silhouette; materials include Lycra and bonded leather, with a palette of black and white punctuated by vivid brights [1][2].

The “Bell Jar” dress has drawn particular curatorial attention. With its specimen-jar silhouette, it references Victorian scientific collecting and Darwinian taxonomy, and demonstrates McQueen’s drive to push material limits, rendering the garment “more crystal than textile” through heavy encrustation [2].

## Runway show and staging

The show took place at Centquatre (Le 104), a large Parisian cultural venue in the 19th arrondissement, on 3 October 2008 [1][9][10]. The catwalk was flanked by antique taxidermy—elephant, giraffe, tiger, zebra, polar bear—creating a tableau that underscored the environmental theme [1][5]. A metal globe at the runway’s end carried projections of the sun, moon, and revolving Earth, visually connecting the set to the collection’s conceptual frame [1]. 

The show’s invitation featured a lenticular image created by Gary James McQueen that transformed Alexander McQueen’s portrait into a skull, emphasizing memento mori and the fragility of life [1]. Contemporary reporting also noted McQueen’s playful closing appearance dressed in a bunny suit, which he used to briefly “shake his tail” before exiting [7].

## Release and exhibition history

As a Paris Spring/Summer 2009 ready-to-wear presentation, the collection was part of the broader fashion-market cycle and reached retailers in spring 2009. Contemporary commentators observed that, alongside its conceptual framework, the collection offered eased-up tailoring and commercially viable pieces, particularly in the softer first sequence [4].

Since its debut, Natural Dis-tinction, Un-Natural Selection has figured in prominent museum displays and scholarship:

- Victoria and Albert Museum: elements of the collection—including the Bell Jar dress—were integral to interpretive materials associated with Savage Beauty (London, 2015), and are presented within the museum’s online Museum of Savage Beauty [1][2].  
- Los Angeles County Museum of Art / National Gallery of Victoria: the LACMA-curated exhibition Lee Alexander McQueen: Mind, Mythos, Muse (Los Angeles, 2022; restaged at NGV, Melbourne, 2022–23) featured looks and accessories from Spring/Summer 2009 and emphasized the collection’s role in advancing digitally engineered print practice [3][11].  
- Museum at FIT: the exhibition Force of Nature (2017) contextualized the collection within a historical narrative connecting fashion and nature, including evolution and environmental themes [8].

## Reception

Natural Dis-tinction, Un-Natural Selection received coverage from major fashion media and general-interest outlets, with reviewers highlighting both its ecological concept and material innovation.

- Vogue Runway (Sarah Mower) framed the show around Darwinian and environmental concerns, praising the collection’s opening “world untouched by man,” the delicacy and romance of the first half, and the season-defining fringing. The review observed an extended passage of bright, engineered prints—“new on the Paris runway”—and assessed the crystal-encrusted finale as avoiding melodrama. McQueen’s notes were quoted to the effect that he did “not want to preach” about such a serious subject [4].  
- The Guardian emphasized the set’s “parade of stuffed wild animals” hired from a Paris taxidermist, describing McQueen’s “latest obsession: Charles Darwin,” and situating the show’s theatricality within the designer’s environmental message at Paris Fashion Week [5].  
- WWD characterized the presentation as a “powerful” show of “unexpected restraint,” calling it “among the most intriguing in a lackluster season,” and drawing attention to its disciplined execution [6].  
- The New York Times (T Magazine blog, Cathy Horyn) described the collection as “drivingly contemporary,” with a “blitz” of varied prints—animals, wood grains, crushed crystals, skeletons, and mechanical forms like the Eiffel Tower—each look distinct and intense in color and pattern; the closing bunny-suit cameo was noted as part of the runway narrative [7].

## Legacy and influence

Curators and scholars have credited Natural Dis-tinction, Un-Natural Selection with the widespread adoption of digitally engineered prints in fashion, highlighting the technical achievement of aligning motifs to garment pattern pieces to sculpt silhouette and produce “multidimensional effects” [3]. The collection’s Darwinian/ecological framing has been referenced in museum exhibitions and scholarship addressing fashion’s relationship to nature, evolution, and environmental change [8]. Its design signatures—specimen-jarlike forms, crystalline encrustation, and the dialogue between organic and synthetic aesthetics—have also featured prominently in retrospective interpretations and displays at the V&A and other institutions [1][2][3][11].

## Title and stylization

Authoritative museum sources consistently present the title of the collection as “Natural Dis-tinction, Un-Natural Selection,” with a hyphen in “Dis-tinction,” a comma after “Dis-tinction,” and capitalization of “Un-Natural” [1][3]. Variant renderings appear in some media and social posts, but “Natural Dis-tinction, Un-Natural Selection” is the stylization used by the Victoria and Albert Museum and the Los Angeles County Museum of Art [1][3].
