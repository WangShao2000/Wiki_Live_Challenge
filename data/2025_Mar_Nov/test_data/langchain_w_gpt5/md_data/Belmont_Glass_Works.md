# Belmont Glass Works

Belmont Glass Works was a 19th‑century glass factory complex in Bellaire, Belmont County, Ohio, United States. Contemporary newspapers, trade references, and local histories identify the works as part of the Ohio River valley’s post–Civil War glassmaking expansion centered on Bellaire and the Wheeling area. The plant is documented as operating in the late 1860s through the late 19th century and was one of several Bellaire glasshouses that manufactured utilitarian and table glass during the period when the city emerged as a regional glassmaking hub, benefiting from river and rail transport, coal and natural gas fuel, and access to markets in the Midwest and East Coast [1][2][3]. Historic mapping and preservation records place the glass industry among the key industrial drivers of Bellaire’s development; later historic district documentation notes the importance of these plants to the city’s built environment and economy [3][4]. The name “Belmont Glass Works” also appears in scattered 19th‑century references to other localities, including Philadelphia, reflecting separate and unrelated uses of the same works name in that era’s glass industry [5].

## Identification and scope

- Common names: Belmont Glass Works; Belmont Glass Company (varied across period references). “Works” in 19th‑century usage typically denoted the factory site and plant, while “company” indicated the corporate entity operating the works [1][2].
- Industry: Manufacture of glass, within the Ohio River valley cluster of window, bottle, lamp, and pressed tableware producers [1][2].
- Location: Bellaire, Belmont County, Ohio, on the Ohio River opposite Wheeling, West Virginia. Industrial mapping and local histories place Bellaire’s glasshouses along the riverfront and adjacent to rail corridors developed in the 1870s–1890s [1][3].
- Period of operation: Late 1860s through the late 19th century, according to regional histories and period newspapers that report on works construction, routine operations, and periodic incidents such as fires and stoppages [1][2].

## Historical background

Bellaire’s growth as a glassmaking center followed mid‑19th‑century advances in American flint glass and pressed glass and the proliferation of fuel‑rich river towns that could supply skilled labor and distribution links. Regional histories compiled in 1890 described the industrialization of the Upper Ohio Valley and name Bellaire’s glasshouses among the city’s principal manufacturers as rail links expanded and coal and natural gas became readily available [1]. Period newspapers in Wheeling, Bellaire, and other Ohio River towns regularly reported on the “Belmont” works with notices about hiring, expansions, stoppages, and fires—coverage typical for glasshouses in the era, which were vulnerable to furnace accidents and fuel disruptions [2].

While incorporation files for specific companies are fragmented in accessible 19th‑century directories, the works model common to the region involved investors forming a company that would build and operate a “works” with one or more furnaces and multiple pots, adding capacity as markets allowed. Bellaire’s plants engaged in this pattern, with furnaces heated by coal or, in some later cases, supplemented by natural gas as supply permitted [1][2].

## Facilities and site

Sanborn Fire Insurance maps for Bellaire in the late 19th century depict glass factories concentrated near the riverfront and the parallel rail alignments, with typical site features including furnace houses (lehrs), blowing rooms, cutting rooms, storage, and tank or pot furnaces, as well as coal sheds and rail sidings. Although the Sanborn atlases label some works by company name and others generically as “glass works,” they provide corroborating evidence of plant layouts, fuel stores, and transport integration in the period when the Belmont‑named works was active [3].

National Register of Historic Places documentation for the Bellaire Historic District summarizes the city’s industrial landscape, noting glass manufacturing as a formative element in the growth and character of the district. The nomination materials cite the clustering of industrial buildings along the river and the railroads and describe the glass industry’s role in shaping Bellaire’s economy and built environment [4].

## Products and technology

The Ohio River valley’s glasshouses in this period typically produced a mix of:

- Pressed and flint tableware such as tumblers, goblets, dishes, and lamps, using cast‑iron molds in mechanically pressed processes that had become widespread by mid‑century.
- Bottles and utilitarian wares, often blown into molds and annealed in lehrs.
- Lamp chimneys and other hollowware where markets existed.

Trade notices and regional industry accounts place Bellaire’s works within these categories of production. The shift from exclusively coal‑fired pot furnaces toward tank furnaces and, where available, supplemental natural gas occurred unevenly across the valley in the 1880s–1890s; Sanborn mapping and trade references record these transitions at different plants in Bellaire and neighboring towns [1][2][3].

## Workforce and labor

The glass industry in Bellaire and across the Ohio River valley relied on a skilled workforce of blowers, pressers, cutters, and finishers, often organized within craft unions by the late 19th century. Period press accounts report routine labor matters—seasonal layoffs, wage negotiations, and strikes—among Bellaire’s glasshouses. These patterns align with statewide reports by Ohio’s factory inspectors and labor statisticians, which tracked working conditions, hours, wages, and industrial accidents in glass plants across Ohio during the 1880s–1890s [2][6]. The specialized nature of glassmaking helped Bellaire attract and retain skilled workers from established centers such as Pittsburgh and Wheeling, contributing to a regional labor pool that moved among valley firms [1][2].

## Transportation, markets, and economic role

Bellaire’s position on the Ohio River with ready access to the Baltimore & Ohio and other railroads provided multimodal distribution for glass shipments. Local histories emphasize these transport links as a principal reason for the city’s ability to sustain multiple glasshouses, with goods moving by barge and rail to Midwestern and Eastern markets. The works model allowed companies to flex production according to fuel supply and market demand, and newspapers reported on orders and shipments indicative of wider distribution beyond Belmont County [1][2][3]. Preservation documentation for the Bellaire Historic District recognizes the industrial corridor shaped by these logistics as a defining factor in Bellaire’s development [4].

## Notability and legacy

- Regional significance: Contemporary histories identify glass manufacturing as a key element of Bellaire’s late‑19th‑century industrial base, with the Belmont‑named works among the factories that established the city’s reputation in the trade [1].
- Urban and architectural imprint: Historic maps and preservation records document the siting and form of glass plants and related worker housing and commercial blocks, now part of the city’s recognized historic fabric [3][4].
- Collecting and scholarship: The prominent role of Bellaire and nearby Wheeling in pressed glass and tableware markets has attracted continuing interest from collectors and researchers; trade journals and library research guides treat Bellaire’s factories within the broader context of American glassmaking in the period [2][7].

## Safety, accidents, and other challenges

Industrial glassmaking carried risks of furnace accidents, fires, and heat exposure. Period reporting in Ohio River valley newspapers includes accounts of fires at glass works, including Bellaire plants, temporary stoppages, and rebuilds—episodes that were common across the industry and sometimes led to extended closures pending capital repairs [2]. Ohio’s late‑19th‑century factory inspection reports tracked compliance with emerging safety standards and recorded accidents and deaths across industrial sectors, including glass [6].

## Context within the glass industry

The decades after the Civil War saw U.S. glass production expand and diversify, with clusters in Pennsylvania, West Virginia, and Ohio competing on fuel, labor, and transport advantages. Bellaire’s works were part of this system, relating closely to the larger Wheeling market and to Pittsburgh’s well‑developed supply chains for silica, soda, and lime and for specialized molds and tools. National trade journals and state industrial reports from the era track the rise, consolidation, and decline of individual works as technology and market demand shifted toward larger tank furnaces and, eventually, to 20th‑century consolidation in fewer, larger plants [1][2][6][7].

## Other uses of the name

The term “Belmont Glass Works” also appears in unrelated 19th‑century references outside Bellaire, notably in period Philadelphia notices that use “Belmont” as a geographic identifier for works in the city’s western districts. These reflect separate companies that adopted the Belmont name for local branding and should not be conflated with the Bellaire works described above [5]. Given the generic character of “Belmont” as a place‑name and brand in the 19th century, similarly named works occasionally appear in trade and city directories without any corporate linkage among them [2][5].

## Status of the site

As with many 19th‑century factory sites in river towns, individual buildings associated with Bellaire’s glasshouses were altered, demolished, or repurposed over time. While specific structures associated exclusively with Belmont Glass Works have not been individually listed, the city’s industrial history—including glass manufacturing—forms part of the significance of the Bellaire Historic District recognized in the National Register of Historic Places [4]. Fire insurance mapping provides the most detailed surviving contemporaneous depiction of plant footprints and materials storage for glass works in Bellaire [3].
