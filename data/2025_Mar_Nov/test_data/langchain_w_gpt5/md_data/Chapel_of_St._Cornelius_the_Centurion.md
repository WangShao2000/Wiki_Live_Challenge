# Chapel of St. Cornelius the Centurion

The Chapel of St. Cornelius the Centurion is a historic Episcopal chapel on Governors Island in New York Harbor, New York City. The present limestone building was constructed in 1905–1906 to designs by architect Charles Coolidge Haight as a replacement for an earlier mid‑19th‑century frame chapel serving the U.S. Army post on the island. It is part of the Governors Island Historic District designated by the New York City Landmarks Preservation Commission and contributes to the Governors Island National Historic Landmark District, reflecting the island’s long military history and associated community life. The chapel has been affiliated with Trinity Church Wall Street and, after military use ended in the 1990s, has been used intermittently for cultural programs and public art installations rather than regular worship. [1][2][3][9][10][11]

## Identity and location

- Official and common names: The building is documented as the Chapel of St. Cornelius the Centurion and is also referred to in official and cultural-program materials as St. Cornelius Chapel or St. Cornelius the Centurion Chapel. [1][3][9]  
- Denomination and affiliation: Episcopal, historically a chapel of Trinity Church Wall Street. [3]  
- Setting: Governors Island, New York Harbor, within the Nolan Park area of the historic Army post. The chapel stands along Evans Road near its junction with Barry Road. A Governors Island map labels the site “St. Cornelius. Chapel.” [1][8]  
- Coordinates: approximately 40°41.295′N 74°0.897′W (about 40.68825, −74.01495). [7]  
- Jurisdiction and context: The building lies within the NYC‑designated Governors Island Historic District and the federally designated Governors Island National Historic Landmark District. [1][2]

The chapel is distinct from the former Catholic chapel Our Lady Star of the Sea (Building 309), also on Governors Island, which dates to 1942 and has been deconsecrated and used for arts programming. [15]

## History

### Origins and 19th century

Christian worship for the Army garrison on Governors Island was organized in the mid‑1840s. Through the efforts of Army chaplain the Rev. John McVickar, a frame chapel was built on the island in 1846–1847; the NYC Landmarks Preservation Commission identifies the earlier building as an 1847 wooden chapel. [1][3] In 1868, an agreement made the island chapel a free chapel of Trinity Church Wall Street, creating a long‑standing affiliation that continued through the 20th century. [3]

### Replacement chapel and dedication (1905–1906)

By the early 1900s the frame structure was replaced with a new stone chapel designed by Charles C. Haight in a Gothic idiom. Construction took place in 1905–1906; the Landmarks Commission describes the building as a “neo‑Gothic limestone church” erected in those years. [1] Contemporary and ecclesiastical sources record that the cornerstone was laid in October 1905, and the chapel was dedicated on October 19, 1906, by Bishop David H. Greer. [4][5][6] The dedication marked the opening of a larger, more permanent house of worship for the Army post, continuing the Episcopal ministry established in the 1840s. [3][4][5]

### 20th century use, Trinity relationship, and military context

For most of the 20th century the chapel served the island’s military community—first the U.S. Army, which continuously occupied Governors Island from the 1790s through 1966, and then the U.S. Coast Guard, which made Governors Island its largest installation from 1966 until 1996. [13] Under a series of arrangements with the federal government, Trinity Church provided ecclesiastical support and maintenance for the building. In 1986, Trinity transferred ownership of the chapel to the United States with a reversion clause providing that title would return to Trinity if the government ceased to own the land or to use the building as a military chapel; the transfer was reported at the time by The New York Times. [3][14] When the Coast Guard vacated the island in 1996, the property reverted to Trinity Church’s ownership. [3]

### 21st century

After public access to much of Governors Island began in the 2000s, St. Cornelius has been used intermittently for cultural programs and art installations presented by the Trust for Governors Island and partners. It has not hosted regular religious services in recent years under Trinity Church’s ownership. [3][9][10][11]

## Architecture

The present chapel, erected in 1905–1906, was designed by Charles Coolidge Haight in an English Gothic or neo‑Gothic mode. The New York City Landmarks Preservation Commission identifies it as a “neo‑Gothic limestone church,” replacing an 1847 wooden chapel on the post. [1]

Descriptive features documented by organ and architectural surveys include:  
- Materials and massing: buff Indiana limestone walls and a slate roof with copper finishings. The plan is cruciform, with nave, transepts, chancel, and a side chapel. A three‑story square tower with a crenellated, four‑turret parapet marks the principal entrance. Approximate dimensions are 106 feet in length by 70 feet at maximum width. [6]  
- Interior: carved oak clergy and choir seating; moveable chairs in the nave and St. Albans Chapel; sacristies for clergy and choir; and a mortuary chapel beneath the chancel with a crypt roof vaulted in flat Italian tile. Historic accounts note the display of military colors and trophies inside the chapel. [6]  
- Organ history: an original two‑manual Hook & Hastings pipe organ (Opus 2115, 1906) was later superseded by an M. P. Möller instrument (Opus 8673, “Double Artiste,” 1954) installed in a chancel chamber, retaining the earlier organ’s casework. An electronic Rodgers console was installed in the 1980s. [6]

A historical marker at the entrance recognizes the chapel’s tower carillon chimes. [17]

## Heritage status

- National designation: The chapel stands within the Governors Island National Historic Landmark District, designated in 1985. The district recognizes the national significance of the island’s fortifications, post layout, and associated architecture as an integrated military landscape from the early republic through the mid‑20th century. [2]  
- Local designation: The New York City Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the Governors Island Historic District in 1996. The Commission’s report specifically notes the Chapel of St. Cornelius the Centurion (1905–1906) by Haight as a contributing building replacing the earlier wood chapel. [1]

The chapel is a contributing property within these districts; it is not individually designated as a separate city landmark. [1][2]

## Use and governance

- Ownership and ecclesiastical affiliation: Trinity Church Wall Street retained or reacquired ownership of the chapel following the Coast Guard’s departure in 1996 under the terms of the 1986 transfer agreement, and identifies the building as part of Trinity Parish (though not used for regular services). [3]  
- Island governance and access: Since a 2010 transfer of 150 acres to New York City, the Trust for Governors Island manages planning, operations, and development across the publicly accessible portions of the island, which is open daily via ferry. A 22‑acre National Monument area, including Fort Jay and Castle Williams, is managed by the National Park Service. [14][13]  
- Current and recent use: The chapel has functioned as an intermittent cultural venue. Notable uses include Creative Time’s PLOT09 art project (2009), which staged site‑specific works in historic buildings including St. Cornelius; Jacob Hashimoto’s 2018 installation The Eclipse, presented by the Trust for Governors Island, which explicitly noted Trinity Church’s ownership and opened the chapel to visitors; and performances such as Reggie Wilson’s “Moses(es)” cited by The New York Times in 2014. [10][9][11]

## Cultural and community role

From its beginnings as a military post chapel through the 20th century, St. Cornelius served Army and later Coast Guard personnel and their families. Historical notices document memorial services and other ceremonies held there, including a 1910 Governors Island memorial service reported by The New York Times. [12] In the 21st century, the chapel has been a setting for contemporary art and performance, integrating cultural programming into the historic environment of Governors Island. [10][9][11]

### Dedication to Cornelius the Centurion

The chapel is dedicated to Cornelius the Centurion, a Roman officer described in the Acts of the Apostles whose reception of the Gospel is traditionally described as the first among Gentiles to be baptized by the Apostle Peter. The Episcopal Church commemorates Cornelius on its calendar of Lesser Feasts and Fasts. [3][17]

## Controversies and debates

A 1986 decision by Trinity Church to transfer the chapel to United States ownership (with a reversion clause) prompted public discussion of maintenance responsibilities and funding. At the time, Trinity clergy explained the move as necessary for the government to invest in needed repairs to a building used primarily by federal personnel during Coast Guard administration of the island. [14] Subsequent reversion to Trinity ownership occurred when the military installation closed in 1996. [3]

## Alternative names and disambiguation

Documented variants include “Chapel of St. Cornelius the Centurion,” “St. Cornelius Chapel,” and “St. Cornelius the Centurion Chapel.” [1][3][9] The chapel is distinct from the former Catholic chapel Our Lady Star of the Sea (Building 309) on Governors Island, which dates to 1942 and has been used for site‑specific art since deconsecration. [15]
