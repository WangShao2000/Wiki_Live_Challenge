# Kaleigh Trace

Kaleigh Trace is a Canadian author, sex educator, and couple and family therapist, known for her memoir Hot, Wet & Shaking: How I Learned to Talk About Sex, first published by Invisible Publishing in 2014 and reissued in a revised, expanded tenth-anniversary edition in 2024. The book won the Evelyn Richardson Non-Fiction Award in 2015. Trace has been recognized for her public education on sexuality and disability, has appeared on CBC programs, and practices as a registered psychotherapist in Toronto. [5][23][24][27][25][7]

## Early life and education

Trace has spoken publicly about living with a physical disability stemming from a spinal injury sustained in a car accident before the age of nine, an experience that has informed her approach to sexuality, self-acceptance, and advocacy. She discussed this background in a CBC Tapestry episode focused on disability. [27]

She holds a Master of Science in Couple & Family Therapy from the University of Guelph and is a Registered Psychotherapist with the College of Registered Psychotherapists of Ontario. [26]

## Career

Trace’s early career centered on sex education in Halifax, Nova Scotia. She worked with Venus Envy, a sexual health and education retailer, where she coordinated education programs and wrote online sex advice, and she maintained a personal blog called The Fucking Facts. She produced a small manual/zine titled Every Body’s Doing It: Talking About Sex and Disability; her writings also appeared in local media such as The Coast. [3][13][22]

In 2013, Trace co-wrote and released the consent-focused parody video “Ask First” with J. Mary Burnet in response to Robin Thicke’s “Blurred Lines.” The project was covered by the Halifax Media Co-op and is hosted on Vimeo. Trace emphasized consent, boundary-respect, and critique of mainstream representations of sexuality in coverage and commentary surrounding the video. [16][17][15]

Her public education and advocacy work includes appearances on CBC platforms. In 2017, she spoke to The Current about inclusive sex education and desirability of disabled bodies. In 2018, she was a guest on CBC Radio’s Tapestry in an episode guest-hosted by Christa Couture titled “Rethinking Disability,” alongside disability writer Eli Clare and engineer Luke Anderson. [1][27][28]

Trace’s writing career is anchored by Hot, Wet & Shaking: How I Learned to Talk About Sex, originally published in 2014, followed by a tenth-anniversary edition in 2024 that added new essays. She continued to participate in readings and events, including a 2014 appearance with author Amber Dawn in Halifax reported by The Coast. [5][8][20]

In addition to writing and speaking, Trace trained and works as a therapist. Her Toronto practice focuses on couples, families, and individuals, with attention to shame, desire, pleasure, pain, and relational healing. A 2024 Toronto Star profile noted her book’s relaunch and reported that after an intensive medical leave in 2023, she returned to her therapy practice. [25][26][7]

In 2025, Trace co-hosted Little Deaths Salon, a performance series in Toronto and Halifax that engages themes of sexuality, grief, and disability; an event occurred on July 17, 2025, at It’s Ok Studios in Toronto, with plans for additional events in fall 2025. [29]

## Writing and works

Trace’s published work spans memoir, essays, and sex education:

- Hot, Wet & Shaking: How I Learned to Talk About Sex (Invisible Publishing, 2014) is a candid memoir-in-essays that addresses solo and partnered sex, consent, and the “scripts” and expectations surrounding sexuality. CBC Books summarized the work as a frank, accessible narrative that “shamelessly” discusses sexual experiences and bodily negotiations. [8][10]

- Hot, Wet & Shaking: 10th Anniversary Edition (Invisible Publishing, 2024) is a revised and expanded edition with new essays; the publisher describes added material and an afterword by Christa Couture. The edition was distributed by Raincoast, with a publication date of June 18, 2024. [5][9]

Trace’s other writing and media include:

- The Coast articles and opinion pieces (e.g., an op-ed addressing ableist street harassment). [18][19]

- Education and advice writing for Venus Envy and personal blogging at The Fucking Facts. [3][13]

- The zine Every Body’s Doing It: Talking About Sex and Disability, distributed by Venus Envy and other retailers. [3]

- The parody video “Ask First,” which engages public discourse on consent, with documentary coverage and commentary in local media. [16][17][15]

## Reception

Hot, Wet & Shaking received coverage and reviews in Canadian media:

- The National Post praised the memoir’s honesty and inclusivity, highlighting Trace’s rejection of shame and centering of disabled embodiment in sexuality discourse. [12]

- Broken Pencil commended the book’s tone and usefulness, calling it “a much needed lighthouse” guiding readers “with love and laughter.” [4]

- The Coast characterized the work as “part coming-of-age memoir and part sex-positive feminist commentary,” and concluded that “Trace has learned to talk about sex and she has learned to do it extremely well.” [18]

In local cultural recognition, Hot, Wet & Shaking was voted Best Book (Gold) in The Coast’s “Best of Halifax” 2014 awards. [21]

## Awards and honors

- Evelyn Richardson Non-Fiction Award (Winner, 2015), presented as part of Nova Scotia’s East Coast Literary Awards administered by the Writers’ Federation of Nova Scotia. The award and winner are confirmed by both the organization and Atlantic Books Today coverage of the ceremony. [23][24]

- Best of Halifax (Gold) – Best Book (2014), community-voted recognition by The Coast. [21]

## Advocacy and activism

Trace’s advocacy focuses on sexuality education, consent, and disability:

- Workshops and talks: She has been profiled for traveling and speaking across Canada and North America about sex education, emphasizing intersections of disability, desirability, pain, and pleasure. [2][3]

- Consent education: “Ask First” used humor and music to promote consent culture; Trace framed consent as central to “annihilating rape culture,” calling for education that respects boundaries and bodies. [16][15][17]

- Media advocacy: On CBC’s The Current (2017), she addressed misconceptions about desirability and disabled bodies and argued for inclusive sex education. [1]

- Youth sexual health: In Teen Health Source’s “5 Questions” feature, Trace emphasized body diversity, listening, and kindness in sexual health education. [14]

## Controversies

Coverage of the “Ask First” video noted debate over its explicit content and language, including a self-defense lyric, which Trace and collaborators contextualized as reflecting self-protection and the anger felt by women and queer communities experiencing harassment and assault. The piece aimed to challenge harmful narratives while affirming consent and pleasure. [16][15]

## Personal life

Public sources describe Trace’s identities and professional location:

- Identity: Trace has been described as a queer, disabled writer and sex educator; artistic and organizational profiles note her as a “disabled, white femme.” [2][13]

- Location and profession: She is based in Toronto and practices as a registered psychotherapist, holding an MSc in Couple & Family Therapy. [25][26]

- Health: A Toronto Star profile reported that Trace is living with a terminal cancer diagnosis and took intensive medical leave in 2023 before returning to practice; the 2025 Little Deaths Salon event description also notes that she is a writer living with terminal cancer. [7][29]
