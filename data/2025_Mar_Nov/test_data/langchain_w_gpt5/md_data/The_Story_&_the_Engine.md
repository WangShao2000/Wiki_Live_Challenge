# The Story & the Engine

The Story & the Engine (also styled as The Story and the Engine in UK listings) is the fifth episode of the British science fiction television series Doctor Who’s Series 15 (marketed internationally as Season 2). Written by Nigerian-British poet and playwright Inua Ellams and directed by Makalla McPherson, the 47‑minute episode was first released in the United Kingdom on BBC iPlayer on 10 May 2025 and broadcast later that day on BBC One; it debuted internationally on Disney+ on the same date, in markets where the service is available [1][3][17]. Marketing and international press materials use an ampersand in the title (“The Story & the Engine”), while BBC iPlayer and UK programme listings render it as “The Story and the Engine” [1][2].

Set primarily in a barbershop in Lagos, Nigeria, the episode revolves around a domain where stories possess literal power, ruled by a figure called “the Barber,” and a cosmic “Story Engine” fed by human narratives. Reviewers noted the episode’s thematic interest in storytelling, memory, identity, and cultural authorship, as well as its use of West African folklore motifs [7][8][11][12][15]. The release was widely covered by mainstream media and specialist outlets; critical reception was mixed-to-positive, praising ambition and cultural specificity while faulting structural compression and didactic tendencies. In the United Kingdom, seven-day consolidated ratings reached 2.704 million viewers across BBC One and BBC iPlayer, ranking 35th for the week on Barb’s chart [5][6].

## Background and development

Doctor Who showrunner Russell T Davies invited Inua Ellams—an acclaimed poet and playwright whose stage works include Barber Shop Chronicles—to contribute an episode built around a single primary location. That suggestion led Ellams to the barbershop concept and to exploring the social and cultural functions of such spaces within African and diasporic communities [4]. Ellams has said the episode’s central idea was sparked by frustration during the COVID-19 lockdown about uncredited artistic labour, which evolved into a story about the power of stories, ownership, and giving credit where it is due [4][13].

Ahead of transmission, Ellams wrote an official short-story prequel, What I Did On My Holidays By Omo Esosa, published on DoctorWho.tv. The prequel introduces Omo Esosa in 1960s Lagos and contextualizes his connection to the Doctor and the barbershop setting that anchors the episode [13][14]. In a post‑broadcast interview, Ellams described the hour as being in conversation with Dot and Bubble (2024), inviting viewers to watch the two episodes back-to-back to trace the Doctor’s emotional arc and the Lagos episode’s importance to his sense of belonging [4].

McPherson emphasized a theatrical, dialogue-forward staging over spectacle, consistent with the contained setting and Ellams’s background in theatre. A longer sequence showing the Doctor and Belinda inside the Story Engine—encountering icons and talismans from global storytelling traditions—was filmed but cut for time [4][9].

## Synopsis and themes

In Lagos, the Doctor (Ncuti Gatwa) and companion Belinda Chandra (Varada Sethu) enter a barbershop where a mysterious figure known as the Barber (Ariyon Bakare) “reigns supreme.” The space is revealed to be a cosmically displaced domain powered by a “Story Engine,” with a Spider weaving a web of revenge; patrons are compelled to tell stories that fuel the Engine and move the domain through a realm of narrative possibility sometimes referred to as a “Nexus” [1][8][13]. Omo Esosa (Sule Rimi), a local man with a history linked to the Doctor, has helped lure the Time Lord to the shop in the hope that he can free those trapped within [12][13].

As stories are told and exchanged, the Barber’s plan emerges: to sever gods from the human narratives that sustain them—erasing those deities by cutting them out of cultural memory. The Doctor counters the Engine’s logic by feeding it his own “never-ending” six-word story: “I’m born, I die, I am reborn,” a recursive formulation grounded in the Time Lord’s cycles of regeneration that overloads the system and resolves the crisis without conventional sabotage [8][12]. Thematically, reviewers connected the episode to questions of authorship, credit, and cultural appropriation; several critics also read it as commentary on the extraction of creative labour by powerful systems and as a meditation on identity and belonging for this incarnation of the Doctor in a Black body [8][11][15].

## Reception

Critical response was mixed to positive, with consensus notes praising ambition, cultural specificity, and thematic reach, alongside critiques of structure and pacing:

- The Guardian’s episode recap highlighted the attempt to weave West African folklore with wider myths within the Lagos setting, noting the story’s conceptual scope [7].
- Den of Geek commended the episode’s exploration of the Doctor’s Blackness and its thematic focus on stories as power, while acknowledging areas where execution divided audiences [8].
- Critical Popcorn lauded the “theatrical” approach, calling it “the most interesting episode of Season 2 so far,” while cautioning that the dialogue-heavy format may be “too talky” for some viewers [9].
- DoctorWhoTV published a strongly critical opinion piece describing the instalment as slow-moving and structurally weak, arguing it delivered “a parade of monologues” without sufficient narrative drive [10].
- Eruditorum Press emphasised the value of the episode’s ambition and conceptual boldness, situating it within a tradition of Doctor Who entries that “take big swings,” while acknowledging compression issues in a 47‑minute slot [11].
- Mashable contextualised the episode’s significance as a first for the modern series in terms of an African-set story and near all‑BIPOC cast, describing the six‑word story resolution and Lagos barbershop milieu as central to its identity [12].

## Ratings and audience metrics

In the United Kingdom, The Story and the Engine recorded an overnight audience of approximately 1.59 million. The seven-day consolidated figure across BBC One and BBC iPlayer rose to 2.704 million, ranking 35th for the week on Barb’s national chart; Radio Times noted it was the lowest consolidated total among the first five episodes of the 2025 run, while TV Zone reported it as the BBC’s top show on that Saturday night, narrowly ahead of Casualty [5][6]. No verified Disney+ viewership rankings specific to the episode were reported in the cited sources.

## Awards and recognition

As of December 2025, there have been no major awards or nominations reported specifically for The Story & the Engine in the cited sources.

## Criticisms and debates

Several critics argued that the story’s compact runtime left ideas underdeveloped, producing an overstuffed or expository feel [10][11]. A recurring debate concerned the Lagos setting and the absence of explicit engagement with Nigeria’s legal and social restrictions on LGBTQ+ people. DoctorWhoTV and CounterCultureUK each criticized this omission, particularly given the Doctor’s same‑sex attractions in the current era and Ellams’s prior works addressing related themes; other reviewers focused on the episode’s broader thematic intentions around storytelling and community rather than national politics [10][11][15].

## Legacy and influence

Multiple outlets noted the episode’s production and creative milestones. Blogtor Who and other commentators described it as the first episode of Doctor Who written by a Black man; the episode’s Lagos setting and cultural lens were also widely remarked upon as overdue territory for the franchise [8][15][17]. Official materials positioned the instalment as thematically paired with Dot and Bubble (2024), and the production commissioned an official prequel short story by Ellams to deepen its character backstory and Lagos milieu [4][13][14].

## Geographic and cultural context

The episode is set in Lagos, Nigeria, and integrates elements drawn from West African folklore and communal storytelling traditions associated with barbershops. Coverage by mainstream and culture-focused outlets highlighted the distinctiveness of this perspective within Doctor Who, connecting it to Ellams’s wider body of work and to representational milestones in the series [7][12][15][16].
