# Joy Laking

Joy Snihur Wyatt Laking (born 1950) is a Canadian painter and author based in Colchester County, Nova Scotia. Working primarily in watercolour, and also in oil and acrylic, she is known for representational, light-infused views of Nova Scotia—particularly the Bay of Fundy—and for plein‑air practice across the province and abroad. Laking has held solo exhibitions at public institutions including the Art Gallery of Nova Scotia, Mount Saint Vincent University Art Gallery, Owens Art Gallery (Mount Allison University), and the Royal Botanical Gardens (Burlington, Ontario). Her watercolour Brigitte’s Window (1984) is in the Art Gallery of Nova Scotia’s permanent collection. She is the author of The Painted Province: Nova Scotia Through an Artist’s Eyes (2020) and received the Queen Elizabeth II Diamond Jubilee Medal in 2012. Laking operates the Joy Laking Gallery near Bass River/Portapique, Nova Scotia. [1][2][4][6][7][8][5][16][14][9][19]

## Early life and education

Laking was born in Owen Sound, Ontario in 1950. She grew up in a creative household—her mother painted, and her father made marionettes and later painted—which shaped her close attention to observation. She has described a formative moment in Grade 2 when new eyeglasses revealed fine detail in the world, a sensibility that later informed her meticulous watercolour technique. [2]

She completed a Fine Art degree at the University of Guelph in 1972 and moved to Nova Scotia shortly thereafter, settling along the Bay of Fundy. The early death of her mother from breast cancer around the time of her graduation reinforced her decision to pursue art as a full‑time practice. [2][4]

## Career

### Overview and mediums

Since the early 1970s Laking has sustained a full-time practice in watercolour, expanding later into oil and acrylic. Her work is grounded in outdoor painting and careful observation; she often develops studio canvases from on-location studies, photographs, and direct looking at subjects such as flowers and domestic spaces. [1][4]

A self-taught watercolourist, she developed an approach that places darks first in single layers to maximize luminosity, reserving the white of the paper for light. She has emphasized strong composition and tonal range across media. [2]

### Subjects and practice

Her subjects include Nova Scotia shorelines and coves, tidal marshes, clapboard houses and porches, winter “white-on-white” scenes, and window/reflection studies. She frequently paints on location, completing works in several hours to capture particular light and tidal conditions, and has pursued annual painting trips in unfamiliar places (e.g., Ghana, Europe) to refresh perspective on home environments. [1][4][12]

### Career development and projects

Selected milestones documented by public institutions and organizations include:
- Flowers of Nova Scotia (solo), Mount Saint Vincent University Art Gallery, Halifax (13 August–12 September, 1982), accompanied by a small brochure, with in-gallery painting events reported in campus media. [7][8]
- Winter Work (solo), Art Gallery of Nova Scotia, Halifax (1989), with related presence at Owens Art Gallery, Mount Allison University (Sackville, NB) in 1989; the AGNS exhibition was followed by touring presentations. [6]
- Solo exhibitions at Owens Art Gallery beyond 1989 are noted in curated profiles; Laking’s work has also been presented in solo exhibitions at the Royal Botanical Gardens in Burlington, Ontario. [2][4]
- Fraser Art Gallery, Tatamagouche (2021): winter paintings created at the Elizabeth Bishop House in Great Village, presented alongside a solo show tied to her book The Painted Province. [10]

Her watercolour Brigitte’s Window (1984) entered the Art Gallery of Nova Scotia’s permanent collection; the image was also reproduced publicly the following year on a Maritime Tel & Tel telephone directory cover. [5][2][15]

## Teaching and community engagement

Laking has taught workshops (including annual watercolour sessions through Parrsboro Creative) and engaged in arts outreach and local initiatives. She has served on boards of organizations such as the Robert Pope Foundation, the Art Gallery of Nova Scotia (Board of Governors), the Elizabeth Bishop Society, Ship’s Company Theatre, Third Place Transition House, and the Ivar Mendez International Foundation, as reported by Visual Arts Nova Scotia. Her work has also been included in community art projects such as the Pugwash Open Air Gallery. [15][4][13]

## Style, technique, and reception

Curated essays describe Laking’s practice as translating intent observation into luminous, realistic watercolours and note her distinctive technique of laying in darks first to preserve brilliance, while reserving white paper for light. She frames painting as an iterative problem-solving process requiring resilience and self-critique. [2]

Regional coverage has highlighted the precision of her winter scenes and window studies and contrasted her process with other realist painters, while Visual Arts Nova Scotia has detailed her long-term plein‑air routine, annual travel, and evolution into oil using R&F oil sticks in the studio. [10][4]

Reviews of The Painted Province emphasize the book’s accessible design (glove-compartment size) and practical mapping for readers to visit painting sites, reinforcing her public engagement with Nova Scotia’s landscapes. [16][17][18]

## Business and operations: Joy Laking Gallery

The Joy Laking Gallery is located along Highway 2 between Great Village and Bass River, overlooking a Bay of Fundy salt marsh. It features original watercolour, oil, and acrylic paintings, limited serigraphs and prints, cards, and Laking’s books. The gallery operates seasonally and otherwise by chance or appointment. Institutionally curated material situates its opening in the late 1980s; local coverage documents longstanding open-house exhibitions held over multiple decades. Contact details are provided on the official site. [9][1][2][11][19][21][23]

## Impact of the 2020 Nova Scotia mass shooting

The April 2020 mass shooting, which began near Portapique, affected Laking personally. In media coverage about memorial planning, she advocated for contemplative commemorative spaces (e.g., oceanside park and tree planting) rather than a large monument, reflecting local sensitivities and long-term community healing. [7][8]
