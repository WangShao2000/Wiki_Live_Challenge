# Trichy assault rifle

The Trichy assault rifle (abbreviated TAR; also rendered as Tiruchirappalli Assault Rifle) is an Indian 7.62×39 mm assault rifle developed and produced at the Ordnance Factory Tiruchirappalli (OFT), Tamil Nadu, which since the Government of India’s 2021 corporatisation of the Ordnance Factory Board operates under Advanced Weapons and Equipment India Limited (AWEIL), a Ministry of Defence public sector enterprise [1][2][3]. Conceived as a domestically produced AK-pattern rifle for internal security and law-enforcement use, TAR development began in the mid‑2010s with user trials by Central Armed Police Forces (CAPFs) in 2017–2018; the first consignment to the Central Reserve Police Force (CRPF) was handed over in March 2020 [6][5]. A down‑folding buttstock variant was publicly launched by OFT on 25 August 2021 to meet requirements from CAPFs, state police forces, and the Railway Protection Force (RPF) [4]. State-level and CAPF procurements have included orders by the Government of Kerala and limited-quantity tenders by the Border Security Force (BSF), among others [10][11][8][9][7]. As of December 2025, the TAR and a dedicated 40×46 mm under‑barrel grenade launcher (UBGL) for TAR/AK‑pattern rifles remain listed in AWEIL’s official small arms product catalogue [1][14].

## Identity and naming

- Official designation on the manufacturer’s catalogue: “7.62×39mm Trichy Assault Rifle (TAR)” [1].  
- Common alternate renderings: “Tiruchirappalli Assault Rifle” and “Trichy assault rifle” (Trichy being the common English short form of Tiruchirappalli) [5][6].  
- Internal/standardization document codes referenced in Government e‑Marketplace (GeM) bid attachments include “TAR‑K‑7043” (approved 20 August 2018) and “TAR‑K‑9024,” indicating internal product/documentation identifiers used for procurement specifications [17][18].

## Origin and manufacturer

The TAR was designed and is manufactured at Ordnance Factory Tiruchirappalli (OFT), a small‑arms production unit inaugurated in 1966. Following the corporatisation of India’s Ordnance Factory Board, the Government of India transferred the management, control, and operations of OFT to AWEIL with effect from 1 October 2021 [2][3]. AWEIL identifies OFT as one of its production units and notes that the Ordnance Factory Project, Korwa (Amethi, Uttar Pradesh) produces critical components of the 7.62×39 mm TAR in addition to hosting the Indo‑Russian Rifles Pvt. Ltd. joint venture that manufactures the AK‑203 rifle for the Indian Army [2]. OFT is described as an Integrated Management System (IMS) certified facility with a National Accreditation Board for Testing and Calibration Laboratories (NABL) accredited lab for mechanical and chemical testing [2].

## Type and role

The TAR is a 7.62×39 mm assault rifle intended as an individual automatic weapon for single and burst firing modes. It has been developed and marketed as an indigenous AK‑pattern alternative for internal security roles, emphasizing use by Central Armed Police Forces (e.g., CRPF, BSF, SSB, ITBP, CISF), state police forces, and other government security agencies [4][1][16][7]. A Press Information Bureau (PIB) release issued by the Government of India described the TAR as designed to engage single and group targets up to 500 m in burst mode, with a stated “destructive effect” out to 1,350 m, language reflecting the manufacturer’s performance description for its intended role [4].

## Development history

Open‑source reporting indicates the following timeline for the TAR’s development and early fielding:

- 2015: OFT began development of the rifle [6].  
- 2017: A production line for TAR was established at OFT [6].  
- November 2017: The CRPF conducted functionality and reliability testing, including a reported 15,000‑round life cycle test in a single run [6].  
- February 2018: Field trials were conducted at multiple locations including Jagdalpur, Raipur, Guwahati, and Srinagar [6].  
- 2018: Media and procurement reports described a CRPF order in the range of approximately 6,100 rifles, with figures reported as 6,137 by The Hindu and 6,167 by The New Indian Express; both accounts attribute the follow‑on order to results of the 2017–2018 evaluations [5][6].  
- March 2020: OFT handed over the first consignment of 500 rifles to the CRPF at a public event reported by national and regional media [6][5].  
- August 2021: OFT launched a down‑folding buttstock variant of the TAR to meet specific mobility requirements of CAPFs, state police, and the RPF [4].

## Design and technical characteristics

- Operating concept and pattern: The TAR is consistently described in official and media sources as an indigenous 7.62×39 mm rifle matching AK‑pattern specifications; SP’s MAI reported that OFT designed the weapon to meet AK‑47‑type specifications with a stated effective range of about 400 m, after field trials [13].  
- Caliber and firing modes: The rifle is chambered in 7.62×39 mm and provides single and burst firing modes, as stated by the PIB at the time of the down‑folding butt launch [4].  
- Dimensions (down‑folding buttstock variant): Overall length of 900 mm with the butt extended and 650 mm with the butt folded, according to the official PIB launch release [4].  
- Sights, rails, barrel length, weight, rate of fire, and muzzle velocity: The manufacturer’s publicly accessible product listings do not provide detailed technical specifications covering these parameters. As of December 2025, AWEIL’s product pages list the TAR but do not publish a formal specification sheet with barrel length, weight, rate of fire, or muzzle velocity; these data are therefore not publicly reported by the manufacturer [1].  
- Accessory compatibility: AWEIL lists a 40×46 mm under‑barrel grenade launcher “for TAR/AK‑47,” indicating compatibility of the TAR with UBGLs intended for AK‑pattern rifles [1]. Media reporting has also cited compatibility with common AK‑pattern UBGLs (e.g., GP‑25/GP‑30 and UBGL M‑6) during the TAR’s early marketing and trials [6][7].

Because TAR is produced by a defence public sector enterprise, it falls under the Ministry of Defence’s quality assurance regime; the PIB notes that defence products manufactured by DPSUs undergo Final Acceptance Inspection (FAI) by the Directorate General of Quality Assurance (DGQA), DGAQA, or DGNAI as applicable. However, product‑specific certification details for TAR are not published by the manufacturer [15].

## Caliber and ammunition

- Chambering: 7.62×39 mm, the standard intermediate cartridge used by AK‑pattern rifles [1][4].  
- Feeding system: While media articles and imagery depict the TAR with curved detachable magazines typical of AK‑pattern rifles, the manufacturer’s publicly available materials do not state magazine capacity or specific magazine model compatibility; those details are not officially published in AWEIL’s product listing [1].

## Variants and evolution

- Fixed and folding stocks: Early reporting indicated fixed‑stock and folding‑stock configurations, with a foldable version under development in 2018 [6].  
- Down‑folding butt variant: On 25 August 2021, OFT formally launched a down‑folding butt version to meet user mobility requirements; the official release provided overall lengths of 900 mm (open) and 650 mm (folded) [4].  
- Compact carbine derivative (TriCa): OFT additionally developed the TriCa compact carbine chambered in 7.62×39 mm, described by The Hindu as a shorter TAR‑family weapon intended for close‑quarters roles; deliveries to specialized units, including an order for the National Security Guard (NSG), were reported in 2021 [12][9]. The TriCa is marketed separately from the full‑length TAR in AWEIL’s portfolio [1][9].

## Procurement and users

Publicly reported TAR procurements and users include the following:

- CRPF: The first consignment of 500 rifles from an order reported as 6,137–6,167 units was handed over in March 2020 after trials conducted in 2017–2018 [5][6].  
- CAPFs and state police (aggregate): Trade and defence media summarised that OFT/AWEIL supplied TARs to multiple CAPFs (including BSF, SSB, ITBP, CISF) and various state police forces following initial CRPF induction; these accounts reflect aggregate deliveries across several years but do not provide a consolidated official tally [7].  
- Kerala Police: The Government of Kerala issued an order sanctioning procurement of 250 7.62×39 mm TARs from OFT, with related budget approval; the New Indian Express reported the sanction in July 2021 [10][11].  
- BSF: In January 2024, the BSF issued a limited‑quantity tender for TARs with bayonets and accessories; secondary reporting stated a planned acquisition of 125 rifles. The tender listing confirms the procurement action and the closure of the bid window [9][8].  

Statements by OFT officials during 2020–2021 public events also provided indicative production and order figures. In March 2020, The Hindu quoted OFT’s General Manager as stating that the factory was “on course to reaching” a productivity level of 2,000 weapons per month and had executed orders for about 11,000 TARs with additional orders on hand; in late 2021, another OFT official was quoted as saying that deliveries for approximately 30,000 TARs had been completed and that 42,000–45,000 units were expected to be delivered during the fiscal year out of collectively placed orders of around 78,000 rifles by CAPFs and state police. These figures were contemporaneous statements reported by The Hindu and are not consolidated in a single official manufacturer release [5][12].

## Field performance and evaluations

Multiple outlets reported that during CRPF testing in November 2017 the TAR underwent a 15,000‑round life cycle firing sequence, which was cited by OFT and media as a reliability demonstration preceding field trials in early 2018 [6]. At the CRPF handover in March 2020, The Hindu reported comments from OFT and CRPF officials: OFT’s General Manager characterised the TAR’s reliability as on par with international standards, and a CRPF officer urged iterative improvement based on user feedback. These remarks were published as quotations and reflect the speakers’ assessments during the induction event [5].

## Comparisons and context

The TAR is positioned within India’s broader small‑arms modernization landscape as an indigenous AK‑pattern rifle primarily aimed at internal security forces, alongside parallel efforts such as the Indo‑Russian AK‑203 program for the Army. AWEIL’s units information notes that OF Project Korwa hosts the AK‑203 joint venture facility, while also producing critical components for TAR, illustrating the co‑existence of domestic production for different AK‑pattern systems with distinct user communities [2]. Defence media commentary has situated TAR among a set of Indian small‑arms programs (including INSAS derivatives, Excalibur, and the Army’s AK‑203 and SIG‑Sauer procurements), describing its role as meeting CAPF and police requirements with a domestically produced 7.62×39 mm platform [16]. Such comparative assessments are analytical in nature and not official specifications.

## Production and current status

- Corporate and catalogue status: As of December 2025, AWEIL lists the 7.62×39 mm Trichy Assault Rifle and a 40×46 mm under‑barrel grenade launcher “for TAR/AK‑47” among its small‑arms products, indicating ongoing availability in the manufacturer’s line‑up [1][14].  
- Manufacturing footprint: AWEIL identifies OFT as the production unit for TAR and notes that OF Project Korwa manufactures critical components for the rifle [2].  
- Quality systems: OFT is described as IMS‑certified with NABL‑accredited testing labs; product‑specific certifications for TAR are not separately published [2].  
- Exports: No authoritative public reporting from AWEIL or the Government of India confirms export customers for the TAR; available open sources focus on Indian CAPF and state police procurements [1][7][5][6].

## Notability

The TAR is notable as a domestically designed and produced AK‑pattern 7.62×39 mm rifle that has been fielded by Indian CAPFs and state police forces, representing an Atmanirbhar Bharat (self‑reliance) approach to small arms for internal security roles. Its development timeline, user trials, initial fielding to the CRPF, introduction of a down‑folding butt variant, and documented state and CAPF procurements have been covered by official Government of India releases and national media [4][5][6][10][11][8][9][1].

### Notes on data availability

- The manufacturer’s public catalogue does not provide a detailed specification sheet for TAR covering barrel length, weight, rate of fire, muzzle velocity, or standard magazine capacity; such parameters are not officially published in open sources by AWEIL as of December 2025 [1].  
- Reported aggregate production and order figures cited in media reflect statements by OFT officials at specific points in time and may not represent consolidated totals across all users; AWEIL has not issued a single public summary of cumulative TAR production/deliveries [12][5].
