# Tony Hawk's Pro Skater 2 (Game Boy Advance)

Tony Hawk's Pro Skater 2 is a skateboarding video game for the Game Boy Advance (GBA) developed by Vicarious Visions and published by Activision. Released in North America on May 30, 2001 and in Europe on June 22, 2001, with a Japanese release by Success under the title SK8: Tony Hawk's Pro Skater 2 on December 14, 2001, it adapts the second main entry in the Tony Hawk’s Pro Skater series to a handheld format using a fixed isometric perspective and a real-time rendered skater model [10][1][11]. The port retains the series’ trick-combo scoring and goal structure while altering level geometry and presentation for the hardware, and it omits certain console features such as multiplayer and create-a-skater/park editors [4][7]. Critics praised its technical achievement and faithful feel relative to the console originals, with outlets including IGN, Eurogamer, and Nintendo World Report highlighting both its ambitious visuals and the learning curve imposed by the viewpoint and controls; aggregator rankings place it among the highest-rated GBA titles [7][6][8][13].

## Overview and Context

- Developer: Vicarious Visions (U.S.) [1]
- Publishers: Activision (North America, Europe); Success (Japan, localized as SK8: Tony Hawk’s Pro Skater 2) [1][11]
- Series: Tony Hawk’s Pro Skater (handheld adaptation of Tony Hawk’s Pro Skater 2) [1]
- Platform: Game Boy Advance [1]
- North American release: May 30, 2001 (launch-window title for GBA) [10][1]
- European release: June 22, 2001 [10]
- Japanese release: December 14, 2001 (title: SK8: Tony Hawk’s Pro Skater 2) [11]
- Rating: ESRB E (Everyone) [9]
- Pricing context: GBA software commonly retailed around US$39.99 at launch; the GBA hardware launched at US$99.99 [12][1][10]

Activision’s pre-release communications positioned the GBA edition as a re-creation built for the handheld, with new level designs and an adapted control scheme allowing play as Tony Hawk or a roster of professional skaters [3].

## Gameplay

Tony Hawk’s Pro Skater 2 on GBA presents levels in a fixed isometric perspective. The player controls one of 13 professional skateboarders, including Tony Hawk, and performs tricks to build score and complete goals within time limits, mirroring the structure of the console game while accommodating the handheld’s display and input constraints [1][2][3].

- Perspective and Controls:
  - Levels are shown from a fixed isometric/overhead viewpoint using pre-rendered backgrounds and a realtime-rendered skater model [2][6].
  - A and B handle jump and grind, while L and R trigger trick categories (flip/grab), reflecting an adapted mapping for the GBA’s four-button layout [6][2].
- Trick System and Scoring:
  - Players chain tricks into combos to maximize points, with objectives including high score tiers and collecting letters (S-K-A-T-E), as in the console version [2].
  - Physics and objectives are designed to “mirror the console versions,” with familiar goals (e.g., grinding helicopter blades in Hangar) adapted to the isometric presentation [2].
- Modes and Progression:
  - Career (Tour) mode tasks players with meeting per-level goal checklists and earning cash to unlock stages and upgrade stats; competition levels focus on judged runs rather than currency [8][2].
  - Free Skate is available; multiplayer modes are not included on GBA [4][7].
- Differences from Console/PC Versions:
  - The GBA port does not include multiplayer, create-a-player, or create-a-park features present in console editions [4][7].
  - Some moving obstacles and complex geometry are simplified or removed, and the fixed camera/perspective affects approach and visibility for ramps and objects [7][2].
- Levels:
  - Sources indicate the GBA version adapts a subset of Tony Hawk’s Pro Skater 2’s parks and incorporates stages suited to the handheld’s viewpoint. Reported lineups include Hangar, School II, Marseille, New York City, and Skatestreet, with Warehouse (from Tony Hawk’s Pro Skater) and a Rooftops stage appearing in the GBA version [7][5][2].
  - Level layouts and secrets (e.g., hidden areas in School II and Hangar) are present with adjustments for the isometric view and sprite/background composition [7][2].
- Roster and Unlockables:
  - The game supports a roster of 13 pro skaters; money earned in Career can be spent on boards and stat upgrades. Additional unlocks (cheats/characters) are awarded for completing Career and competition goals, as per handheld adaptations [1][8].

## Audio, Visuals, and Technology

Vicarious Visions built a custom software-driven rendering approach for GBA to preserve the series’ animation and physics within cartridge storage and processor constraints.

- Rendering and Performance:
  - The skater is rendered as a realtime polygonal model (approximately a few hundred polygons) composited over pre-rendered isometric backgrounds; objects can become transparent when occluding the skater [2].
  - IGN reported that the game runs at a “constant 60 frames per second” with responsive animation and fluid trick transitions relative to the handheld’s capabilities [2].
- Physics and Code:
  - The development team implemented 3D math for physics on the GBA’s processor, with extensive assembly-level optimization; the port strategically reused and adapted PlayStation code to preserve gameplay feel [4][5].
  - Time Extension’s developer feature describes using PlayStation tooling to produce bytecode executed on the GBA and combining C++ with low-level routines to meet performance targets [5].
- Audio:
  - Licensed console soundtracks are replaced by original music composed for GBA by Shin’en contributors Manfred Linzner and Toni Linzner, with the game using Shin’en’s GAX sound engine [7][14].
  - Sound effects emulate console feedback within GBA audio constraints [7].

## Development

- Timeline and Team:
  - Development began in August 2000, with final approval on April 6, 2001 (roughly eight months of development), led by Vicarious Visions in collaboration with Activision [4].
  - The team’s stated goal was to translate the console game’s feel to GBA without rewriting the entire experience, balancing storage, performance, and input challenges [5][3].
- Technical Goals and Constraints:
  - Storing thousands of animation frames at multiple angles was infeasible on GBA cartridges; the team opted for a realtime 3D skater to fit animation and directional movement within memory limits [4].
  - Physics and collision were computed in 3D while presented in a 2D isometric rendering context; the approach required heavy optimization due to the GBA’s limited instruction set (e.g., lack of hardware division) [4].
- Collaboration and Iteration:
  - The team engaged closely with Neversoft/Activision to align level design with series conventions; developer accounts describe iterative feedback on early builds and adjustments to the handheld-appropriate level geometry [5][3].

Activision announced the title as part of its GBA lineup targeting summer 2001, describing new level designs and controls adapted to the handheld while preserving the trick set and real-world locations [3].

## Release and Distribution

- North America: May 30, 2001 (Activision; product codes documented in retail databases) [10].
- Europe: June 22, 2001 (Activision) [10].
- Japan: December 14, 2001 (Success; localized as SK8: Tony Hawk’s Pro Skater 2; priced at ¥5,800, excluding tax) [11].
- Launch Window: GameSpot reported THPS2 GBA as a title “available for the June launch” of the system; the GBA hardware launched in the U.S. in June 2001 at US$99.99, with early coverage describing GBA software pricing around US$39.95–US$39.99 [1][12].
- Ratings: ESRB E (Everyone) in North America [9].
- Reprints and Later Availability: No Player’s Choice or Classics reprint has been documented from reliable sources; later distribution occurred through standard retail and resale channels.

## Reception

Contemporary reviews from major outlets praised the technical ambition and fidelity of the handheld adaptation, while noting a learning curve due to the fixed camera and control mapping.

- IGN:
  - Hands-on preview characterized the port’s performance and control as closely matching console play at a reported 60 fps, with familiar objectives preserved [2].
  - The review described the GBA version as playing “nearly identical to the console” within handheld limits, while acknowledging the absence of console extras and the initial challenge posed by perspective and shoulder-button mapping [7].
- Eurogamer:
  - Eurogamer emphasized that only skaters are true 3D while levels are pre-rendered, praised the feel of half-pipe airs and level design, and noted the adapted control layout for jump, grind, and trick buttons [6].
- Nintendo World Report:
  - NWR concluded that Vicarious Visions’ port “closely mirrors” the PlayStation/Dreamcast experience, assigning a 9/10 score and citing demanding difficulty and missing features as caveats [8].
- Aggregators:
  - Metacritic’s “Best Games on Game Boy Advance” list ranks Tony Hawk’s Pro Skater 2 among the platform’s highest-rated titles, displaying a metascore of 95 for the GBA entry [13].
  - MobyGames aggregates an average critic score near 88% for the GBA version based on multiple reviews, reflecting broadly positive reception [14].

Common praise centered on the preservation of the trick-combo system and overall feel relative to console versions; recurring criticisms included visibility and angle-judgment challenges inherent to the isometric perspective, and the absence of multiplayer and customization editors [6][7][4].

## Commercial Performance and Legacy

Formal shipment/sales figures specific to the GBA version were not widely published by Activision in contemporaneous trade releases. Trade coverage identified Tony Hawk’s Pro Skater 2 as a standout among early GBA titles, and subsequent retrospectives and developer features have cited the game’s technical approach as influential in Vicarious Visions’ later handheld projects (including subsequent Tony Hawk GBA entries) and in the studio’s application of similar rendering and optimization techniques to other GBA adaptations [1][12][5][7][6].

## Controversies and Notable Issues

No major content controversies were reported by reliable sources. Critic commentary highlighted:
- The fixed isometric camera can impede depth/angle judgment, increasing the learning curve relative to console play [2][7].
- The port excludes multiplayer and user-generated content features from console versions due to development constraints [4][7].

## Notes on Differences in the Japanese Release

The Japanese release, SK8: Tony Hawk’s Pro Skater 2, was published and distributed by Success under license from Activision. Success’s official product page confirms the localized title, release date and price, and provides product materials describing game modes and skater information; reliable English-language sources primarily reference the retitling and publisher change without detailing mechanical changes beyond localization [11].
