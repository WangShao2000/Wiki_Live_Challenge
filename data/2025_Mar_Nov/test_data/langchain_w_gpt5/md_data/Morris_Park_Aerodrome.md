# Morris Park Aerodrome

Morris Park Aerodrome was a short‑lived early flying field in New York City operated by the Aeronautical Society of New York in 1908–1909 on the grounds of the former Morris Park Racecourse in the Morris Park neighborhood of the Bronx. The Society leased the racetrack in late 1908 to provide convenient experimental grounds for building and testing aircraft and for staging public exhibitions, including a large Election Day meet on November 3, 1908 and flights by Glenn H. Curtiss in June 1909 [1][6][4]. The site’s use for aviation ended after a major fire destroyed much of the racecourse in April 1910 and the property was later subdivided for housing; by early 1910 the Society had also secured a second flying ground at Mineola on Long Island [7][1]. Some accounts have described Morris Park as the first established airfield in the United States, though such characterizations depend on how “airfield” is defined given earlier flying grounds elsewhere [15].

## Identity and Location

Morris Park Aerodrome occupied the infield and associated grounds of the former Morris Park Racecourse (opened 1889, closed 1904) in the present‑day Morris Park section of the Bronx [1]. Contemporary and later mapping places the racetrack and its associated roads in the blocks around Morris Park Avenue and Bronxdale Avenue; NYPL atlas plates and photographs document the racetrack footprint and streets in the area [11][12]. In the Aeronautical Society’s own program and archival description, the field was “formerly Morris Park Racecourse” and at the time “just north of New York City (then Westchester County; later part of the Bronx)” [1].

The aerodrome was not a purpose‑built airport but rather the repurposed racetrack grounds used as a flying field, workshop area, and exhibition venue [1]. New York Public Library cartographic sources confirm the racetrack’s location in Morris Park (e.g., Bromley atlas plates covering Morris Park Avenue/Bronxdale Avenue corridors) and show the local street grid that later replaced the course after subdivision [11][12].

## Establishment, Management, and Purpose

The Aeronautical Society of New York (also styled “Aeronautic Society”) was founded in mid‑1908 to promote aeronautics through experiments, facilities, and public engagement. In September 1908 the Society leased the defunct Morris Park Racecourse from the City of New York for two years, and styled the grounds “Morris Park Aerodrome” [1]. The Society’s stated purpose was to provide experimental grounds close to the city, organize public events, and support the construction and testing of flying machines [1]. By March 1909, the Society’s association with Curtiss and Augustus M. Herring was noted in the press as they prepared a new aeroplane for trials at Morris Park [6].

Period of operation at Morris Park was essentially late 1908 through 1909, anchored by the November 3, 1908 exhibition and the June 1909 trials, with aviation activity at the site ending after the April 1910 fire and subsequent subdivision; the Society also began establishing a second flying ground at Mineola in January 1910 [1][7].

## Operations and Events

Election Day exhibition, November 3, 1908. The Society’s first major public event at Morris Park was staged on Election Day, 3 November 1908, in cooperation with the Federation of American Motorcyclists. The official program survives in the National Air and Space Museum Archives and describes alternating aeronautic demonstrations and motorcycle races; the museum’s finding aid notes a turnout of “over 20,000 people” [1]. To support lighter‑than‑air demonstrations, a hydrogen gas plant was installed at the aerodrome by early November 1908 [13].

Curtiss flights, June 1909. The New York Times reported the arrival of Glenn H. Curtiss and a new, light aeroplane at the “old Morris Park track” for trials in mid‑June 1909 [2][3]. On June 26–27 the Society’s meet featured Curtiss making straightaway flights and then circling the one‑mile track, showing control of the machine over rough ground and around trees. Contemporary accounts describe a straight flight of about 300 yards at roughly 30 feet altitude, followed by a lap of the track in 1 minute 45.4 seconds, with altitude varying from about 20 to 60 feet [4]. Press coverage emphasized that the Society had procured a machine from Herring‑Curtiss for these New York trials and public exhibition flights [6].

Other activities. The 1908 program and press coverage make clear that Morris Park’s early aviation involved both heavier‑than‑air and lighter‑than‑air demonstrations, glider towing, and public shows interspersed with motorcycle races [1]. Curtiss’s use of ground “wind‑wagon” devices (propeller‑driven test cars) to assess motor and propeller performance was widely discussed in this era; Curtiss himself described them as experimental devices to test propulsion rather than practical vehicles [9]. In 1909, lighter‑than‑air development included work by Joel T. Rice and financier John Andrew Riggs: after building the Arkansas Traveler, their company began constructing a large steel‑framed airship, the American Eagle, at Morris Park, though planned tours did not materialize after early mishaps [10].

## Notable Individuals and Aircraft

- Glenn H. Curtiss. Curtiss led the June 1909 trials at Morris Park and demonstrated flights over the track using his Herring‑Curtiss biplane; the machine and flights were reported by the New York Times on June 13, 14, and 27, 1909 [2][3][4].
- Augustus M. Herring. Partnered with Curtiss in supplying an aeroplane to the Society for the Morris Park trials, as reported March 13, 1909 [6].
- Henry W. Walden. An early Society member and designer who subsequently became known for monoplane work; biographical materials document his association with the Society in 1908 and his subsequent flights on Long Island in late 1909–1910 [17].
- Joel T. Rice and John Andrew Riggs. Their Hot Springs Airship Company undertook construction of the American Eagle dirigible at Morris Park in 1909, noted by the Encyclopedia of Arkansas entry on Riggs [10].
- Alexander V. Wilson. A New England inventor whose apparatus was exhibited at the Society’s 1908 aeronautic exhibition at Morris Park, as noted by the Bar Harbor Historical Society [14].

## Physical Infrastructure and Practices

Morris Park’s physical plant was that of a large horse‑racing venue, with grandstand, infield, and ancillary stables and sheds. The Aeronautical Society used this existing infrastructure for aeronautic workshops and for exhibition logistics, while the infield and track served as flying grounds and demonstration areas [1]. The New York Times reporting on the June 1909 trials emphasized the roughness of the ground and the presence of trees, which the flying program had to accommodate [4]. For lighter‑than‑air work, a hydrogen gas plant was installed at the aerodrome by November 1908 [13]. Public exhibitions interleaved testing and demonstrations with motorcycle races to attract spectators, as shown in the November 1908 program [1].

## Relationship to Other New York–Area Aviation Sites and Relocation

The Aeronautical Society sought grounds close to the city for accessibility but also sought larger, more open fields. The Society secured a second flying ground at Mineola on the Hempstead Plains in January 1910, and subsequent Society activities and training increasingly took place there [1]. Curtiss’s narratives of early flights and training of Society pilots place the next phase of activity on Long Island in mid‑1909–1910 [9]. The relocation reflected the advantages of a wider, smoother field for extended flight tests compared to the constrained and rough ground at the racetrack [4][1].

## Closure, Subsequent Land Use, and Commemoration

On April 11, 1910, a wind‑driven fire swept the old Morris Park track, destroying stables and threatening nearby buildings; press reports describe widespread damage, effectively ending the site’s viability as a racing and exhibition ground [7]. The Aeronautical Society’s archival chronology notes that the property was later sold and subdivided, which matches the neighborhood’s subsequent street grid and residential development documented by NYPL atlases and photographs [1][11][12].

Commemoration of Morris Park’s aviation history has been limited. Local journalism has highlighted efforts by Bronx residents and archivists to mark the neighborhood’s overlooked role in early New York aviation, including proposals for a plaque recognizing the 1908–1909 aerodrome [16].

## Legacy and Historical Significance

Morris Park Aerodrome figures in the broader story of how aviation moved from private fields and remote sites to public exhibitions near major cities. The Society’s Election Day program put aeronautics before a large urban audience, and the June 1909 flights by Curtiss at the racetrack drew sustained coverage emphasizing control and practical flight over difficult ground [1][2][3][4]. Some accounts—most prominently in the New York Times’s historical retrospectives—have described Morris Park as “the world’s first established airfield,” a claim tied to the formal lease and public, organized use of a dedicated ground near the city [15]. The Morris Park episode also connects directly to the rise of Long Island as a center of New York–area aviation, with Society operations shifting to Mineola/ Hempstead Plains in 1910 [1].

## Disputes and Differing Accounts

Descriptions of Morris Park as the “first established airfield” reflect one definition of an airfield as a formally leased, organized site for public exhibitions and routine experimental operations close to a major city. Other early flying grounds existed elsewhere in the United States before 1908, although they were not necessarily leased venues with formal programs and public gates. The New York Times has used the “first established airfield” characterization in retrospective coverage [15]. Given the variety of early sites and uses, modern accounts present such “first” claims with caution, distinguishing between informal flying grounds and formally established aerodromes; the sources cited here present Morris Park’s significance without asserting uncontested primacy.

## Subsequent Documentation and Visual Sources

Period mapping and imagery corroborate the site’s identity and transformation:
- NYPL Bromley atlas plates show Morris Park’s street network and former racetrack area, including Morris Park Avenue and adjacent roads [11].
- NYPL photographs document the Morris Park Race Track area and nearby streets in the years around subdivision [12].
- Aeronautics magazine featured Curtiss’s flights at Morris Park on its July 1909 cover; a digitized copy is available via HathiTrust [8].
