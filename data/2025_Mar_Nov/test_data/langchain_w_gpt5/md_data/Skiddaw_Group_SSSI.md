# Skiddaw Group SSSI

Skiddaw Group SSSI is a Site of Special Scientific Interest (SSSI) in Cumbria, England, designated to protect nationally important geology and associated upland environments in the northern Lake District, including classic exposures of the Ordovician Skiddaw Group and late Caledonian structural and igneous features. The site is notified by Natural England under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, with official details provided via Natural England’s Designated Sites View and citation; mapping of its boundary is available through the DEFRA MAGIC map and Natural England’s open data services. The SSSI encompasses the Skiddaw massif and adjoining fells such as Blencathra, Uldale and Caldbeck Fells, with notable graptolitic stratigraphy, slaty cleavage and contact metamorphism around the Skiddaw granite, and mineralized localities around Carrock Fell and the Caldew Valley. The area lies within the Lake District National Park and coincides with the English Lake District UNESCO World Heritage Site boundary designated in 2017. [1][2][3][4][11]

## Location and extent

Skiddaw Group SSSI lies in the northern Lake District, County of Cumbria, around the Skiddaw massif (north of Keswick), Blencathra, and the Caldbeck/Uldale commons. Natural England provides the official site record (site code S1003421) with citation and unit-level information; statutory boundary mapping is accessible via the MAGIC interactive map and Natural England’s open data feature service for SSSIs. The SSSI is divided into management “units” used for condition monitoring and consenting; unit inventories and attributes are published in Natural England’s SSSI Units (England) dataset. [1][2][3][4][18]

Precise area (hectares), grid references and centroid coordinates are recorded in Natural England’s datasets and map services; the SSSI boundary can be identified and interrogated using MAGIC’s SSSI layers and the “Identify” tool. [3][4]

## Geological context

### The Skiddaw Group

The Skiddaw Group forms the oldest exposed rocks in the English Lake District and comprises a thick succession of deep-marine turbidites: mudstones and siltstones with subordinate wacke-type sandstones, deposited during the Tremadoc to Llanvirn stages of the Ordovician (with the lowermost beds possibly late Cambrian). The group is classically affected by low-grade regional metamorphism and an Acadian (early Devonian) slaty cleavage that overprints earlier soft-sediment deformation; later granitic intrusion produced contact aureoles and localized greisenization. The base of the group is not exposed; the top is overlain unconformably by Ordovician volcanic rocks of the Borrowdale Volcanic Group (BVG) and Eycott Volcanic Group (EVG). [6][7]

In the northern Lake District inlier (north of the Causey Pike Fault), the Skiddaw Group comprises, in ascending order, the Bitter Beck, Watch Hill, Hope Beck, Loweswater and Kirk Stile formations, with transitional boundaries. These dominantly muddy turbidites are interrupted by major sandstone incursions (Watch Hill and Loweswater formations). South of the Causey Pike Fault, the Buttermere Formation represents a large olistostrome (mass movement deposit) emplaced in late Arenig time, overlain by the Tarn Moor Formation of late Arenig to early Llanvirn mudstones and siltstones; the Tarn Moor Formation is truncated by the unconformable base of the BVG. The depositional setting is interpreted as a deep-marine basin on the Avalonian margin of the Iapetus Ocean, with palaeocurrent indicators demonstrating consistent flow from the east. [6][7]

The group’s graptolite fauna and acritarch assemblages underpin a robust biostratigraphy that has made Skiddaw Group localities a standard for the British Arenig succession. A regional slaty cleavage with NE Caledonide trend developed in early Devonian time during the Acadian Orogeny; late Caledonian granites (e.g., Skiddaw granite) intruded around 395–400 Ma, overlapping the cleavage-forming episode and producing contact metamorphic aureoles. [6][7][8][15]

### Classic exposures and biostratigraphy

Graptolite-bearing sections within the Skiddaw Group around the SSSI area are of national to international importance and are documented in the Joint Nature Conservation Committee’s Geological Conservation Review (GCR) Series. Key localities include: [5]

- Trusmadoor (near Great Cockup): the only British section showing the Tremadoc–Arenig transition in graptolitic facies, with assemblages including Araneograptus murrayi and early Arenig Didymograptus taxa; acritarch floras provide additional biostratigraphic control. The locality is described as of international importance for marking this epochal boundary. [5]
- Barf (on the NW shore of Bassenthwaite): best locality for the simulans Zone (upper Loweswater Formation), with diverse Didymograptus and associated taxa; overlying finer Kirk Stile beds yield lower gibberulus Zone assemblages. [5]
- Randel Crag (Kirk Stile Formation): prolific upper gibberulus Zone locality (type for Isograptus caduceus gibberulus) with Gondwanan-affinity trilobites; slates are altered by the Skiddaw granite aureole. [5]
- Outerside (upper Kirk Stile Formation): diverse hirundo Zone fauna (uppermost Arenig) and basal Llanvirn artus Zone markers (e.g., Didymograptus spinulosus, Undulograptus taxa), illustrating the Arenig–Llanvirn transition. [5]

These localities collectively demonstrate the Skiddaw Group’s role as a biostratigraphic standard and provide correlation ties to Scandinavian and Australasian Ordovician zones. [5]

### Structure and metamorphism

Late Caledonian (Acadian) deformation produced upright folds and a slaty cleavage (S1) across Skiddaw Group rocks; earlier “D0” soft-sediment deformation structures occur within turbiditic successions. The Lake District Caledonian structural regime includes sinistral strike-slip faulting and NE-trending shear zones; in places such as the River Caldew and Raven Crags (Mungrisdale), multiple deformation phases are recognized, with disharmonic folding and anomalous fold trends recorded in Skiddaw Group lithologies. The main cleavage-forming deformation and granite emplacement are constrained to early Devononian ages (~394–392 Ma), linking British Caledonian events to the Acadian Orogeny. [8]

Raven Crags provides one of the clearest displays of Caledonian folds in Skiddaw Group rocks (Loweswater Formation turbidites), with four deformation phases (D0 and D1–D3) recognized; the dominant D1 upright folds with steep cleavage show trends that differ from much of the region, illustrating local reorientation. The River Caldew also shows steeply plunging disharmonic folds documented in structural studies. [8]

### Igneous intrusion and mineralization

At Grainsgill in the Caldew Valley, the northernmost of three outcrops of the Skiddaw granite is exposed, adjacent to the Carrock Fell Complex. The site demonstrates relationships among late Caledonian granite intrusion, hydrothermal metasomatism (greisen development) and tungsten–arsenic mineralization. K–Ar dating indicates that granite cooling, greisen formation and tungsten mineralization occurred within a short interval (minimum granite age 392 ± 4 Ma; mean mineralization alteration age 385 ± 4 Ma). Tungsten-bearing veins (NNE-trending, steep W-dipping) transect Skiddaw Group country rock; Carrock Mine worked major quartz–wolframite veins (Smith, Harding, Emerson) with accessory scheelite, arsenopyrite and other sulphides and carbonates. Contact metamorphism produced a classic concentric aureole, altering Skiddaw slates to biotite–cordierite hornfels. [9][15]

The Carrock Fell Complex comprises intrusive sheets and dyke-like bodies along the Skiddaw–Eycott boundary, including gabbro and microdiorite; these igneous features are part of the broader Ordovician igneous history of the Lake District. [9][15]

## Designation and management

Skiddaw Group is notified by Natural England as a Site of Special Scientific Interest under Section 28 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981; the official record and citation are published on the Designated Sites View. Natural England’s site datasets and map services provide boundary, feature and unit information, and condition assessments are reported at unit level. The SSSI boundary, features and consented operations are also accessible via DEFRA’s MAGIC interactive map. [1][2][3]

Natural England categorizes SSSI feature condition using standardized classes (Favourable; Unfavourable recovering; Unfavourable no change; Unfavourable declining; Part destroyed; Destroyed) and undertakes monitoring on a cycle (commonly within six years). Operations that may damage the special interest require prior written consent; enforcement provisions apply where damaging operations are undertaken without consent. [12]

The SSSI comprises multiple management units, each with identified habitats or geological features and recorded condition. Unit inventories and attributes (including OS grid references, area, latest condition and assessment dates) are published via the Natural England Open Data “SSSI Units (England)” feature layer. [18]

## Relationship to wider geology and designations

The Skiddaw Group sits beneath and structurally relates to the Ordovician Borrowdale Volcanic Group (BVG) and Eycott Volcanic Group (EVG), with a significant pre-volcanic unconformity marking uplift and basin reorganization. Post-Ordovician successions in the Lake District include the Windermere Supergroup; together these units record Avalonia’s evolution along the Iapetus margin and subsequent Caledonian orogenesis. The Skiddaw Group’s fossil biostratigraphy provides national zonal standards and international correlation for Tremadoc–Arenig–Llanvirn stages. [6][7][8][14]

The SSSI lies within the Lake District National Park. The English Lake District UNESCO World Heritage Site boundary mirrors the National Park boundary designated at the 41st UNESCO Committee Convention on 12 July 2017; overlapping the SSSI boundary with the WHS/National Park boundary using MAGIC and LDNPA datasets demonstrates the SSSI’s inclusion within the designated landscape. [4][11]

Within the SSSI, several localities are documented as Geological Conservation Review (GCR) sites spanning GCR blocks including British Cambrian to Ordovician Stratigraphy (e.g., Trusmadoor, Barf, Outerside, Randel Crag), Caledonian Structures (e.g., Raven Crags; River Caldew), Caledonian Igneous Rocks (e.g., Carrock Fell), and Mineralization of England and Wales (e.g., Grainsgill/Carrock area, with mineral sites in the Caldbeck Fells). The GCR series provides the scientific justification and conservation significance of these sites and is the national framework underpinning geological SSSI selection. [5][8][13]

## Conservation, threats and condition

Natural England uses unit-level monitoring to assess condition against favourable condition targets. Upland SSSIs in the Lake District typically face pressures from grazing levels, visitor trampling and erosion, invasive species (e.g., bracken), wildfires, and (at geological localities) unregulated specimen collection; the Lake District High Fells Special Area of Conservation (SAC) documentation and broader assessments highlight the need to manage grazing, maintain hydrology for blanket bogs, control invasive species and disease, prevent fire, and avoid erosion/nutrient enrichment. Skiddaw Group SSSI is within the Lake District High Fells landscape where these issues arise. [12][11]

Mineralogical localities associated with late Caledonian mineralization (e.g., around Carrock Fell and the Caldbeck Fells) are particularly sensitive to specimen collecting. JNCC guidance in the Mineralization volume supports responsible collecting only where it does not harm the scientific integrity of sites and opposes over-collecting or destructive methods on SSSIs. [13]

Conservation actions in parts of the SSSI include peatland restoration and woodland creation consistent with maintaining and enhancing designated features. Cumbria Wildlife Trust’s Skiddaw Forest nature reserve has a long-term plan to restore blanket bog and re-establish native temperate rainforest woodland mosaics across ~1,200 ha within the SSSI, undertaken with Natural England consents and Forestry Commission regulatory processes. The reserve remains a high-altitude, exposed site where access needs hillwalking skills; works are constrained by SSSI and SAC oversight to safeguard features of special interest. [10][17]

## Access and land ownership

SSSI designation does not itself confer public access rights; access depends on landowner permissions, public rights of way, or open access land provisions. Operations likely to damage SSSI features require Natural England consent even for owners or occupiers. Natural England provides site-specific lists of operations requiring consent via the Designated Sites system. Visitors using the SSSI’s public paths and open fell land should follow safety guidance appropriate to high-altitude, exposed terrain. [12][1][3]

Within Skiddaw Group SSSI, land ownership is mixed; notably, Cumbria Wildlife Trust owns and manages the Skiddaw Forest nature reserve within the site, which includes the summits of Skiddaw, Great Calva and Little Calva. The Trust states that access arrangements remain unchanged, but emphasizes that the area is remote and exposed, requiring suitable hillwalking and navigation skills. [10][17]

## History of study

The Skiddaw Group has a long history in British geology. Early 20th-century workers commented on the difficulty of interpreting the homogenous slaty successions. A comprehensive resurvey by the British Geological Survey (BGS) between 1982 and 1997 led to major advances in understanding stratigraphy, biostratigraphy, depositional environment, deformation and metamorphism, summarized in the 2004 BGS memoir “The Skiddaw Group of the English Lake District.” That work clarified the lithostratigraphical framework (including the identification of distinct formations in the northern and southern domains), established robust graptolite/aacritarch zonations, and tied regional structure and cleavage development to Acadian orogenesis with late Caledonian granite emplacement around 395–400 Ma. These insights underpin the selection and documentation of GCR sites in the area (stratigraphic, structural, igneous and mineralization themes). [6][7][5][8][9]

## Data quality notes

“Skiddaw Group” is both the name of a geological rock group and the name used by Natural England for this SSSI; the SSSI boundary and features are defined by statutory notification and may not coincide exactly with the entire outcrop of the Skiddaw Group. Official details for the SSSI (site code S1003421)—including formal name, notification dates, interest types, features cited for protection, area, grid references and unit condition—are published by Natural England via the Designated Sites View and the citation PDF. Where specific numeric attributes (e.g., area in hectares, centroid coordinates) are not reproduced here, they can be obtained directly from Natural England’s Designated Sites View, official citation, and open data feature services, and confirmed via the MAGIC map.
