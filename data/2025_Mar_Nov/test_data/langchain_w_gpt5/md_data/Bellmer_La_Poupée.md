# La Poupée (Hans Bellmer)

La Poupée is a small French photobook by the German-born artist Hans Bellmer, published in Paris by GLM (Guy Lévis-Mano) in 1936. It assembles ten mounted gelatin silver photographs of Bellmer’s articulated “doll” sculptures, accompanied by French-language text translated by Robert Valençay [1][2][3][4]. The book is a central publication within Bellmer’s broader photographic project The Doll (c. 1933–1938), which explored fragmentation, recombination, and the erotics of the mannequin in dialogue with Surrealist interests in the object and the uncanny. Contemporary and later accounts situate La Poupée in relation to earlier and subsequent iterations: the German portfolio Die Puppe (1934), which appeared alongside reproductions in the Surrealist journal Minotaure, and the postwar artist-book Les Jeux de la poupée (1949) with prose-poem texts by Paul Éluard and hand-colored photographs [4][5][6][7][8][9]. The book’s imagery and methods have attracted substantial scholarly interpretation, including psychoanalytic, Surrealist, and gender-political readings, and its photographs are held in major museum collections [4][10][11][12][13][14].

## Titles, publication, and format

- Title and language: La poupée (French; commonly translated into English as The Doll) [3].
- Publisher: GLM (Guy Lévis-Mano), Paris [3][15].
- Year of publication: 1936 [3][4][5].
- Format and contents: A small-format artist’s book containing ten original gelatin silver prints mounted to boards and bound, with French text translated by Robert Valençay [1][4][5]. The book has been described by museums and bibliographic notes as pocket-sized; dealer descriptions give overall dimensions around 159 × 123 mm (6¼ × 4¾ in) and individual print mounts around 162 × 124 mm [1][2].
- Edition and printing: Dealer and bibliographic notes indicate a first edition printed in a total of around 100 numbered copies plus several hors commerce copies; some copies are on “papier rose” with waxed printed wrappers, and include publisher subscription inserts for a planned book, Les Jeux de la poupée [1][2]. These details are consistent with references in GLM bibliographies and photobook histories, though the surviving catalog records do not uniformly list the print run [15].
- Text contributors: The book includes an introductory text by Bellmer translated into French by Robert Valençay, as noted in museum and dealer records [1][10][11].
- Distribution and cataloging: Library and museum holdings include plates and books cataloged at the Museum of Modern Art (New York), the J. Paul Getty Museum, the Centre Pompidou, Princeton University Library, and the Bibliothèque nationale de France (BnF) [3][5][10][11][12][14][16].

## Artist and historical context

Bellmer began the doll project in 1933 in Germany, in the context of the Nazi consolidation of power and its promotion of an idealized “body culture.” The artist withdrew from commercial employment in 1933 as a personal protest and worked effectively in secrecy; he was largely unknown in Germany but found recognition among Parisian Surrealists [4][13]. Surrealist networks reproduced Bellmer’s photographs in Minotaure no. 6 (Winter 1934–35), and he later relocated to France, where Surrealists welcomed him and his work [4]. Museums and scholarship frequently note Bellmer’s mixture of desire and loathing directed at the female body and the use of dolls as Surrealist objects, alongside political readings that understand aspects of the project as a critique of Nazi body ideology [4][13].

## Development and production

- The doll(s): Bellmer constructed life-size mannequin-dolls with articulated ball joints, modular limbs, and interchangeable components that allowed reassembly into contorted postures. He built a first doll in 1934, and a second, more flexible doll in 1935 [4][5].
- Photographic techniques: The project employed studio techniques—posed staging, lighting, use of props—and extensive manual interventions on prints, including coloring, retouching, and overpainting. Variants include solarized prints and hand-colored gelatin silver prints produced between 1934 and 1937, with some hand-colored prints produced or published later (e.g., for Les Jeux de la poupée in 1949) [10][13][17].
- Settings and staging: The photographs depict the doll in domestic and staged environments, fragmented, dismembered, or recombined, often close-up and cropped to emphasize joints, limbs, and torsos [4][13].
- Sequencing and chronology: La Poupée assembles ten photographs from the ongoing project. Certain reproduced sequences in Minotaure and later Les Jeux document stages or variations of construction and pose [4][5][6][7][9].

## Content and imagery

The plates in La Poupée present motifs of fragmentation, dismemberment and reassembly, ball joints, and hybrid bodily forms. Bellmer repeatedly posed the doll to produce disorienting anatomies—torsos doubled, limbs displaced, and joints emphasized—with the camera often framing tightly to isolate parts and deepen ambiguities between animate and inanimate forms [4][10][13]. Distinctive images include configurations of doubled pelvises and bodies propped in corners or on furniture, with cuticular surface textures further intensified by retouching and, in some variants, hand-coloring [10][17].

## Relationship to related publications

- Die Puppe (1934): Bellmer self-published with Thomas Eckstein a German portfolio of ten gelatin silver prints in Karlsruhe in 1934. A larger set of eighteen images from the project was disseminated through Minotaure no. 6 in Winter 1934–35 under the title “Poupée, variations sur le montage d’une mineure articulée” (pages 30–31) [4][5][7][9].
- Les Jeux de la poupée (1949): Published in Paris by Éditions Premières (Heinz Berggruen) with fourteen prose poems by Paul Éluard (written in 1938), the book included fifteen hand-colored gelatin silver prints mounted to boards, plus two cut-out photomontages on the cover and title page. The edition is recorded at 136 copies, reflecting war-delayed publication of material developed between approximately 1935 and 1938 [6][8].
- Theoretical text: Bellmer later wrote a theoretical essay titled Petite anatomie de l’inconscient physique ou L’Anatomie de l’image, discussing bodily imagery and the image’s construction; the text is widely cited in scholarship on the doll project [18][19].

## Themes and interpretations

- Surrealist object and photographic practice: Rosalind Krauss has discussed Bellmer’s doll photographs within Surrealism’s broader “conditions of photography” and the Surrealist object’s status, noting their staging of disjunctions, fetishization, and the interplay of text and image in Surrealist publications [18].
- Psychoanalytic and erotics: Hal Foster interprets Bellmer’s dolls as central to Surrealism’s “compulsive beauty,” aligning erotic compulsion with fragmentation, sadism/masochism, and death drive motifs within Surrealist aesthetics [19].
- Anxiety and gender politics: Sue Taylor’s monograph frames Bellmer’s work through psychoanalytic inquiries into erotomania, identification, and anxiety, highlighting the complex gendered dynamics of violence and desire in the images and texts [20].
- Political readings: Museum essays and later critical accounts note Bellmer’s declared opposition to Nazi ideals of physical perfection and the body, reading the diseased or abject surface effects and deformation as a critique of authoritarian body ideology. These political claims are presented alongside recognition that many viewers focused on the sexual content, producing contested interpretations in subsequent scholarship [4][13][20].

## Reception and controversy

- Contemporary Surrealist reception: The photographs were welcomed by Surrealist circles in Paris, with dissemination via Minotaure (1934–35) and inclusion in the discourse on the “Surrealist object,” also represented in the Exposition surréaliste d’objets organized by André Breton at Galerie Charles Ratton in Paris in May 1936. Bellmer’s dolls aligned with an expanded field of Surrealist object-making across publication, exhibition, and photographic practice [5][7][16][21][22].
- Postwar and later reception: Postwar criticism, feminist and queer theory, and psychoanalytic scholarship engaged the work’s erotics, violence, and gender politics. Major exhibitions include the International Center of Photography’s Behind Closed Doors: The Art of Hans Bellmer (2001), which explored the doll photographs in the context of gender and sexuality in Nazi Germany [23][24]. The Museum of Modern Art’s exhibitions on artists’ books have featured Bellmer’s publications in histories of the medium [25].

## Influence and legacy

La Poupée’s images contributed decisively to Surrealism’s photographic vocabulary of the mannequin and the uncanny. The project influenced artists working with staged photography, assemblage, and the manipulated body in postwar and contemporary art. Plates and related photographs from La Poupée and The Doll project are held in major museum collections, including the Museum of Modern Art, the J. Paul Getty Museum, the Centre Pompidou, and The Metropolitan Museum of Art, indicating the work’s continued significance in museum narratives of Surrealist photography and artists’ books [3][10][11][12][13][25].

## Surviving copies and catalog records

- Bibliothèque nationale de France (BnF) catalog record for La poupée, crediting the translator Robert Valençay [3].
- Princeton University Library has recorded holdings of La Poupée as a “landmark work of Surrealist photography” containing ten original gelatin silver prints [5].
- The J. Paul Getty Museum and the Museum of Modern Art hold plates and book-related materials; the Centre Pompidou holds related hand-colored photographs and works associated with the doll project [10][11][12][25].
- WorldCat lists records for La Poupée (1936), Die Puppe (1934), and Les Jeux de la poupée (1949), documenting holdings and bibliographic metadata across libraries [4][8][16].

## Distinctions among La Poupée, Die Puppe, and Les Jeux de la poupée

- Die Puppe (1934): German self-publication; gelatin silver prints; early sequences disseminated through Minotaure [4][5][7][9].
- La Poupée (1936): GLM Paris photobook; ten mounted photographs; French text (translated by Valençay); pocket-format; linked to Surrealist networks and publications [1][3][4][5].
- Les Jeux de la poupée (1949): Éditions Premières; fifteen hand-colored photographs; Éluard’s fourteen prose texts; postwar edition from prewar material [6][8].

## Terminology and translations

Standard forms:
- French title: La poupée (The Doll).
- German title: Die Puppe.
- English usage: The Doll, typically referring to the photographic project; La Poupée specifically denotes the 1936 French GLM photobook when used in bibliographic contexts [3][4][5].

## Bibliography and photobook histories

GLM’s imprint and bibliographic records have been compiled in published bibliographies (Les Éditions GLM: 1923–1974) used by libraries and dealers to verify content and format. Photobook histories (e.g., The Book of 101 Books; The Photobook: A History, Vol. I) routinely include La Poupée among key twentieth-century photographic books, consistent with dealer citations and institutional holdings [1][15][25].
