# Centre for Minority Rights Development (Kenya) and Minority Rights Group International (on behalf of Endorois Welfare Council) v. Kenya

The Endorois case is a decision of the African Commission on Human and Peoples’ Rights (ACHPR) concerning the displacement of the Endorois community from their ancestral lands around Lake Bogoria in Kenya. The communication, formally titled Centre for Minority Rights Development (Kenya) and Minority Rights Group International (on behalf of Endorois Welfare Council) v. Kenya, was registered as Communication 276/2003 and adopted at the ACHPR’s 46th Ordinary Session held in Banjul, The Gambia, from 11–25 November 2009. The Commission found violations of Articles 1, 8, 14, 17(2)–(3), 21, and 22 of the African Charter on Human and Peoples’ Rights and recommended restitution of ancestral lands, recognition of ownership, compensation and royalties, benefit-sharing, and guaranteed access for religious and cultural practices, among other measures [3][6][4]. The African Union Executive Council addressed the Twenty-Seventh Activity Report containing the decision through EX.CL/Dec.548(XVI) adopted on 1 February 2010, and decisions of the Assembly were adopted on 2 February 2010; sources refer to the decision as endorsed by AU organs on 2 February 2010 [1][5][7][4][14]. Subsequent implementation in Kenya has been partial, including establishment of a national task force in 2014, benefit-sharing initiatives by the Baringo County Government, and management planning for Lake Bogoria; assessments up to 2022 describe limited progress on land restitution and compensation [10][20][12][13][16]. The case is widely cited for developing African jurisprudence on indigenous peoples’ collective land rights and for articulating standards on consultation, consent, benefit-sharing, and prior impact assessments under the right to development; it has influenced subsequent cases, including the African Court’s Ogiek judgment in 2017 [10][9][23][6].

## Background

The Endorois are a pastoral community historically occupying lands around Lake Bogoria and the Mochongoi Forest in Kenya’s Rift Valley, including areas within Baringo, Koibatek, Nakuru, and Laikipia. Their livelihood involved seasonal grazing, salt licks for cattle, beekeeping, and habitation; their religious and cultural identity is closely tied to specific sites at and around Lake Bogoria and Mochongoi, which they regard as sacred and central to communal rites and festivals. The community’s burial grounds and ritual sites are integral to their spiritual practices [3][12].

Under colonial administration, the Crown asserted formal title, but Endorois occupation and customary use continued. After independence in 1963, land in the area became Trust Land held by County Councils under Section 115 of the Constitution, which required councils to act for the benefit of residents and to give effect to customary rights [3]. In 1973, the government gazetted the Lake Hannington Game Reserve; in 1978, it re-gazetted the area as Lake Bogoria Game Reserve and denied the Endorois meaningful access. In 1986, approximately 170 families received payments of KSh 3,150 per family, which were documented as relocation facilitation rather than full compensation for the losses from displacement [3][5]. Presidential directives in 1994 and 1995 stated that compensation arrangements and a proposed 25% revenue-sharing scheme should be implemented, but these directives were not carried out [3].

## Parties

The communication was submitted by the Centre for Minority Rights Development (CEMIRIDE) and Minority Rights Group International (MRG), acting on behalf of the Endorois Welfare Council (EWC), the representative body of the Endorois community. The respondent was the Republic of Kenya. The Commission also received submissions and amicus curiae input from other organizations, including the Centre on Housing Rights and Evictions (COHRE) [12][3].

## Proceedings

- Seizure and admissibility:
  - The communication was submitted in 2003 and seized by the Commission at its 34th Ordinary Session (November 2003). The Commission invited submissions on admissibility and issued urgent appeals in 2004 and 2005 to halt state actions that could prejudice the community’s rights while the case was pending [12].
  - At its 37th Ordinary Session (April–May 2005), the Commission declared the case admissible, finding that the conditions under Article 56 of the African Charter were satisfied, including that local remedies were not effective or sufficient in the circumstances. Kenya later requested reconsideration of admissibility, arguing lack of exhaustion of domestic remedies; the Commission declined to reopen admissibility [12].

- Merits:
  - The Commission heard expert testimony and received multiple rounds of submissions through its 40th and 41st Ordinary Sessions, before adopting the decision on the merits at its 46th Ordinary Session (11–25 November 2009) in Banjul, The Gambia [12][6].

- Domestic proceedings:
  - Prior to the ACHPR decision, Kenyan High Court proceedings had dismissed claims brought by the Endorois concerning the Trust Land status and the consequences of the game reserve designations. The Commission reviewed these proceedings in its admissibility and merits analyses [12][3].

## Legal issues

The complainants alleged violations of several African Charter provisions:

- Article 8 (freedom of conscience and religion), due to denial of access to sacred sites and interference with ritual practices at Lake Bogoria and Mochongoi [3].
- Article 14 (right to property), due to dispossession from ancestral lands and denial of recognition of communal ownership under customary tenure [3].
- Article 17(2)–(3) (cultural rights), due to interference with cultural identity and practices tied to specific sites [3].
- Article 21 (right of peoples to freely dispose of wealth and natural resources), due to exclusion from benefit-sharing and resource governance in their territory [3].
- Article 22 (right of peoples to economic, social and cultural development), due to the lack of effective participation, fair benefit-sharing, and prior environmental and social impact assessments in development initiatives affecting their lands [3].

The complainants invoked Articles 60–61 of the Charter, permitting the Commission to draw on international law, and cited comparative jurisprudence on indigenous land rights and customary tenure from the Inter-American Court of Human Rights and other courts. The respondent State contested the characterization of the Endorois as a distinct “people,” argued that Trust Land and related statutory mechanisms provided adequate protection, and maintained that conservation and public interest justified the measures taken [12][3].

## Decision

The Commission found that:

- Status as a “people” and indigenous identity:
  - The Endorois constitute a distinct indigenous community or “people” under the African Charter. The Commission drew on criteria developed by its Working Group on Indigenous Populations/Communities and international sources on self-identification, cultural distinctiveness, historical continuity, and a special relationship to territory and resources [6][12].

- Article 8 (religion):
  - Eviction from ancestral lands and denial of access to sacred sites unjustifiably interfered with religious practice. Limitations must be lawful, necessary, and proportionate; the restrictions were not justified by conservation aims and were disproportionate to any legitimate objective [3][6].

- Article 14 (property):
  - Communal land under customary tenure is “property” protected by Article 14. Traditional possession of land by indigenous peoples requires recognition and protection equivalent to title, without the need for formal deeds. Forced removal and denial of recognition failed the conjunctive test of public need and accordance with appropriate laws, including proportionality for indigenous land [3][6].

- Article 17(2)–(3) (culture):
  - The State has duties to protect and promote cultural identity. Denial of access to cultural sites and disruption of Endorois cultural life violated Article 17. The Commission emphasized the limited scope for restricting cultural rights and the importance of ensuring access to sites necessary for rituals and festivals [3][6].

- Article 21 (natural resources):
  - Exclusion from the benefits of natural resources within the Endorois territory and failure to ensure equitable benefit-sharing contravened Article 21 [3][6].

- Article 22 (development):
  - The Commission elaborated Article 22 and held that the State must ensure effective participation, reasonable benefits, and prior environmental and social impact assessments for development or conservation measures affecting indigenous communities. It endorsed standards analogous to those articulated in comparative jurisprudence (e.g., Saramaka safeguards), including the duty to obtain free, prior and informed consent for major projects within indigenous territories according to their customs and traditions [3][9][11][6].

The Commission thus concluded that Kenya violated Articles 1, 8, 14, 17(2)–(3), 21, and 22 of the African Charter [6][3].

## Remedies

The Commission recommended that the respondent State:

- Recognize rights of ownership of the Endorois to their ancestral lands and restitute those lands to the community.
- Ensure unrestricted access to Lake Bogoria and surrounding sites for religious and cultural rites and for grazing cattle.
- Pay adequate compensation to the Endorois for all losses suffered as a result of the removal.
- Pay royalties to the Endorois from existing economic activities within their territory and ensure equitable benefit-sharing.
- Provide employment opportunities for the Endorois in the Lake Bogoria National Reserve and related activities.
- Engage in dialogue with the community and their representative institutions for effective implementation and report on implementation within a specified period [3][6].

## Implementation and aftermath

- ACHPR resolutions:
  - ACHPR/Res.197(L)2011 expressed concern that Lake Bogoria was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2011 without obtaining the free, prior and informed consent of the Endorois through their representative institutions; the Commission viewed this as inconsistent with the Endorois decision and a violation of the right to development under Article 22 [10].
  - ACHPR/Res.257(LIV)2013 called on Kenya to implement the Endorois decision, noting a lack of feedback and urging compliance with the Charter [11].

- Government actions:
  - On 19 September 2014, a presidentially appointed Task Force on implementation of the Endorois decision was established via Gazette Notice No. 6708, with a one-year tenure. Its terms of reference included advising on restitution, compensation, royalties, and environmental considerations, and reporting quarterly with interim recommendations within six months [12]. Public reporting on outcomes has remained disputed; civil society letters in February 2024 urged publication of the task force report and full implementation of the ACHPR decision [18].

- County-level measures and revenue sharing:
  - The Lake Bogoria National Reserve (LBNR) Management Plan 2019–2029 was approved by the Baringo County Government and endorsed by the Endorois community and Kenya Wildlife Service on 21 October 2020. The plan commits to community partnership, protection of cultural sites, ensuring access for religious and cultural practices, and strengthening benefit-sharing mechanisms, including adoption of the Endorois bio-cultural protocol and revival of joint management structures [14].
  - On 21 September 2020, the Baringo County Assembly adopted the LBNR Management Plan and recommended increasing ploughed-back revenue from 10% to 25%, with 15% dedicated to community grants and 10% to reserve management, and called for regulations to operationalize grants [13].
  - Baringo County has implemented a scheme sharing 10% of revenue collected in Lake Bogoria among local communities, allocating funds for bursaries, livelihood projects, and administration; the initiative reports thousands of bursaries funded since 2015 [15].

- Assessments of compliance:
  - A comprehensive civil society report published in March 2022 assessed implementation as partial and inconsistent. It recorded payment of royalties to the community in certain fiscal years (e.g., KSh 8.2 million from KSh 82 million revenue in FY 2019/20; KSh 2.2 million from KSh 22 million in FY 2020/21), noted some employment of Endorois staff in reserve operations, and described limited access arrangements to sacred sites. The report concluded that recognition and restitution of ancestral lands and adequate compensation had not been achieved as of 2022 [16].
  - Earlier commentary notes public pledges by Kenyan officials in 2010 to implement the decision, followed by slow progress; the case has repeatedly appeared in ACHPR monitoring as an example of challenges in implementing Commission decisions [17][11][9][20].

## Significance

The Endorois decision is frequently cited as a landmark in African regional human rights law for its articulation of indigenous peoples’ collective land rights under the African Charter and for being the Commission’s first detailed finding on the right to development. Commentators describe it as the first legal recognition of an African indigenous community’s rights over traditionally owned land at the regional level and the first global decision to find a violation of the right to development, though such characterizations are presented in the sources’ voices rather than as undisputed facts [17][10]. The decision’s reliance on Articles 60–61 to integrate comparative jurisprudence and standards—consultation, free, prior and informed consent for major projects, equitable benefit-sharing, and prior environmental and social impact assessments—has influenced subsequent African human rights jurisprudence, including the African Court’s 2017 Ogiek judgment, which referenced Endorois in its analysis of indigenous peoples’ rights and land [23][6].

The case has also contributed to policy debates around conservation and World Heritage site governance. ACHPR Resolution 197 (2011) criticized the inscription of Lake Bogoria on the World Heritage List without FPIC, underscoring the need to reconcile conservation frameworks with indigenous rights under the Charter [10]. Management planning documents for Lake Bogoria thereafter included explicit commitments to community participation, cultural site protection, and benefit-sharing [14][13].

## Reception and criticism

- Implementation and enforcement:
  - System-level analysis of compliance with ACHPR decisions indicates low rates of full compliance historically; commentators and ACHPR bodies have highlighted Endorois as emblematic of the difficulty of translating Commission recommendations into effective domestic remedies. Sources emphasize the need for stronger AU mechanisms and state reporting to improve implementation [11][20].

- Conceptual and doctrinal debates:
  - Kenya disputed the characterization of the Endorois as a distinct indigenous “people” and argued that Trust Land and related mechanisms safeguarded rights adequately. The Commission’s approach drew on comparative jurisprudence and emphasized group rights under the African Charter. Academic discussions have explored the Commission’s reliance on external jurisprudence to develop African standards, the contours of the right to development, and the scope of “peoples’” rights under the Charter [12][3][10][9].

- Conservation and World Heritage:
  - The ACHPR criticized the process of UNESCO listing of Lake Bogoria without FPIC, viewing it as inconsistent with the Endorois decision. This prompted calls for procedural reforms to ensure the World Heritage Convention’s implementation aligns with indigenous rights standards, with differing views among conservation bodies and rights advocates about the adequacy of subsequent measures [10][16][14][13].
