# Breton Civil War, 1341–1343

The Breton Civil War, 1341–1343, was the opening phase of the War of the Breton Succession, a dynastic conflict in the Duchy of Brittany closely intertwined with the early Hundred Years’ War. It began with a succession crisis following the death of Duke John III on 30 April 1341 and pitted two claimants—the Montfort line (John of Montfort) and the Blois–Penthièvre line (Charles of Blois, by right of his wife, Joan of Penthièvre)—whose supporters were reinforced by the crowns of England and France respectively. The period saw rapid seizures of towns, sieges and battles across Brittany, including the capture of John of Montfort at Nantes (1341), the defense of Hennebont (1342), English naval intervention off Brest (August 1342), the Battle of Morlaix (September 1342), and protracted fighting around Vannes (late 1342). It culminated in the Truce of Malestroit on 19 January 1343, brokered by papal legates of Clement VI, which paused hostilities without resolving the succession. These events set the pattern for prolonged civil war in Brittany and deepened Anglo-French strategic rivalry during the Hundred Years’ War [1][6][8][9].

## Background and causes

Ducal succession in Brittany rested on customary law (coutume) rather than codified statute. The “Très ancienne coutume de Bretagne,” first compiled in the early fourteenth century, reflected long-standing practices about inheritance, fiefs, and baronial holdings and was frequently cited in later medieval disputes. Breton custom favored the integrity of baronial holdings and the transmission of titles, but specific rules about female succession to the ducal title were contested, and legal arguments drew on older ducal assizes, such as Geoffrey II’s late-twelfth-century ordinance discouraging partition of baronies and emphasizing primogeniture in feudal holdings [3][4]. 

Duke Arthur II’s two marriages produced competing lines. His eldest son, John III (r. 1312–1341), died without legitimate issue. Two principal claims emerged:

- John of Montfort, John III’s half-brother, asserted an agnatic succession through the male line of the ducal house, rapidly taking homage and possession of key ducal centers after John III’s death [1].
- Joan of Penthièvre, John III’s niece, claimed the duchy jure uxoris through her husband Charles of Blois, a nephew of King Philip VI of France. Her party argued that, under Breton custom and feudal law, the right could pass through a female heir to her spouse, and sought royal adjudication from the French crown [2][3].

After witness interrogatories and legal briefs in late summer 1341, King Philip VI received Charles’s homage for Brittany on 7 September 1341 “in our faith and homage by reason of his said wife,” effectively recognizing the Blois–Penthièvre claim and precipitating civil war [2][3].

## Principal factions and leaders

- Montfort party: John of Montfort (d. 1345); his wife Joanna of Flanders (also known as Jeanne la Flamme), who became the leading figure in 1342; English allies included Edward III, William de Bohun (Earl of Northampton), and Sir Walter Manny. Montfort’s supporters held and garrisoned towns in western Brittany and sought English aid by sea [1][5][9].
  
- Blois–Penthièvre party: Charles of Blois and his wife Joan of Penthièvre; supported by King Philip VI of France and royal commanders under the Duke of Normandy (the future King John II). Breton magnates such as Olivier IV de Clisson initially fought for this side [1][6][11].

- Royal intervention: France favored the Blois–Penthièvre claim and committed substantial land forces in 1341–1343. England backed the Montfort cause with naval and expeditionary forces, reflecting broader Anglo-French rivalry in the early Hundred Years’ War [1][9].

Notable figures include Joanna of Flanders, credited by contemporary chroniclers with organizing the defense of Hennebont and leading sorties against besiegers in 1342 [5], and Geoffrey de Charny, a prominent French knight captured at Morlaix and later ransomed [7].

## Chronology, 1341–1343

### 1341: succession crisis and early operations

- April–September: Following John III’s death (30 April), John of Montfort moved quickly to secure ducal strongholds and the treasury. The Blois–Penthièvre party took its case to the French crown [1][2].
- 7 September: Philip VI received Charles of Blois’s homage for Brittany on behalf of Joan of Penthièvre, prompting armed intervention in eastern Brittany [2][3].
- Autumn: A French-led army overran parts of the duchy. John of Montfort was taken at Nantes later in 1341 and imprisoned, leaving Joanna of Flanders to continue the Montfortist resistance from Hennebont and Brest [1].

### 1342: sieges and battles

- Siege and defense of Hennebont (May–June): Charles of Blois besieged Hennebont. Jean le Bel describes Joanna of Flanders rallying defenders, directing the townspeople, and leading a sortie to burn the besiegers’ camp. English aid under Sir Walter Manny eventually arrived by sea after delays caused by storms, helped destroy the besiegers’ principal engines, and forced the lifting of the siege [5].
  
- Naval action off Brest (August): An English fleet reached Brest in mid-August 1342, compelling Charles to abandon operations in western Brittany. Froissart and other sources record the disruption of French-Genoese naval support and the re-establishment of Montfortist positions along the coast [6][9].

- Battle of Morlaix (29–30 September): The Earl of Northampton led an English force against Charles of Blois near Morlaix. Contemporary accounts emphasize English defensive preparations and longbow fire. Geoffrey de Charny was captured; French losses were significant, though Northampton did not take the town and withdrew from the siege thereafter [7][9]. The engagement was an early land battle in the Anglo-French war within Brittany, illustrating tactical patterns (English infantry and archers fighting defensively) that would recur later in the 1340s [9].

- Late 1342 campaign in southern Brittany: Edward III sailed in October and focused on Vannes, a major southern city and strategic port. Froissart recounts repeated assaults and extensive devastation in the surrounding countryside during the winter operations, as English-led forces attempted to force a decision around Vannes and Nantes [6][9].

### 1343: negotiation and truce

- December 1342–January 1343: A large French royal army assembled under the Duke of Normandy advanced to relieve besieged towns; both sides maneuvered without seeking a pitched battle. Papal envoys of Pope Clement VI mediated between the armies gathered near Malestroit [6][8][9].

- 19 January: The Truce of Malestroit was concluded, pausing hostilities in Brittany and other theaters of the Hundred Years’ War. The truce was mediated by papal legates and envisaged a broader negotiation under papal auspices at Avignon. Prisoner exchanges formed part of the settlement. It did not resolve the Breton succession, leaving the duchy divided between rival garrisons and authorities [6][8][9].

## Diplomacy and papal mediation

Pope Clement VI sought to arrest the escalating Anglo-French war by interposing papal authority in local ceasefires and general truces. In Brittany, his legates brokered the Malestroit truce on 19 January 1343, providing for a time-limited cessation of hostilities and the exchange of prisoners and aiming to convene substantive negotiations at Avignon. While the truce reduced major campaigning temporarily, it did not produce a lasting peace in Brittany, and violations and local clashes persisted [8][9]. Contemporary narratives stress the papal envoys’ role in preventing a large engagement between the French royal army and Edward III’s forces in late 1342 [6].

## Political, social, and economic impacts within Brittany

- Urban and rural devastation: Repeated sieges and counter-sieges around key towns, notably Hennebont, Brest, Morlaix, Vannes, and Nantes, produced significant material damage. Chroniclers describe the burning of siege camps, destruction of engines, and ravaging of suburbs and countryside in late 1342 [5][6].

- Governance and justice: The war sharpened questions of royal jurisdiction and treason. In 1343, Olivier IV de Clisson, a prominent Breton lord who had served on the French side, was arrested and executed by order of King Philip VI “by judgement of the king,” without the normal formal judicial process. Shortly thereafter, further executions of Montfort supporters in Paris were ordered as traitors. These acts, recorded in legal-historical scholarship, shocked contemporaries and were widely noted in later accounts [11]. 

- Alignments and patronage: The division of Brittany into rival zones of control spurred the reconfiguration of local allegiances, garrison financing, and recourse to foreign patrons (England and France). Later institutional developments in the ducal chancery and assertions of ducal “sovereignty” belong to subsequent decades, but their origins lay in the wartime struggles for authority and legitimacy in the 1340s [10].

## Outcomes and significance

The 1341–1343 phase ended in stalemate formalized by the Truce of Malestroit. It did not settle the succession but entrenched foreign intervention: England’s naval and expeditionary support sustained Montfortist resistance; France’s royal armies maintained Blois–Penthièvre control of major eastern and central towns. Tactically, early engagements in Brittany, such as Morlaix, illustrated the effectiveness of English defensive infantry-and-longbow dispositions against larger feudal levies, a pattern seen in later campaigns of the Hundred Years’ War [6][7][9]. Strategically, the civil war made Brittany a contested theater and logistical base, linking regional dynastic politics to the wider Anglo-French conflict [1][9].

## Historiography

- Legality of claims: Scholars have analyzed the 1341 dossier, including witness interrogations and royal judgments. The French royal decision to receive Charles of Blois’s homage on behalf of Joan of Penthièvre reflected a view of Breton custom and feudal transmission that prioritized the female heir’s right to transmit title jure uxoris. The Montfortist case stressed agnatic succession and non-partition of principalities. Modern editions and studies of these documents (Michael C. E. Jones; Erika Graham-Goering) have clarified the legal arguments and administrative context [2][3][4].

- Foreign intervention: Academic surveys of the Hundred Years’ War emphasize how Brittany’s civil war aligned with the grand strategies of Edward III and Philip VI, with each backing the claimant whose legal theory contrasted with his own claim to the French throne (England supporting a male-line claim in Brittany while asserting hereditary right through a female line to France, and vice versa for France) [1][9].

- Leadership and narrative: Joanna of Flanders’s role at Hennebont has attracted attention, with chroniclers such as Jean le Bel providing vivid accounts of her command and morale leadership. Historians have debated the extent to which such narratives reflect chivalric tropes versus precise operational realities [5][9].

- Periodization: Some historians treat 1341–1343 as the “first phase” of the Breton civil war, ending with the Malestroit truce, while others emphasize the continuity of operations and shifting control beyond 1343, making the truce a pause rather than a clear endpoint. The subsequent capture and release of principals, renewed campaigns, and the eventual settlement at Guérande (1365) anchor longer chronologies [1][9][10].

## Data points

- Key dates: 
  - 30 April 1341: death of Duke John III [1].
  - 7 September 1341: Philip VI recognizes Charles of Blois’s homage for Brittany [2][3].
  - May–June 1342: Siege of Hennebont; English relief under Walter Manny [5].
  - Mid-August 1342: English fleet reaches Brest; French-Genoese siege lifted [6][9].
  - 29–30 September 1342: Battle of Morlaix; Geoffrey de Charny captured [7][9].
  - Late 1342: Edward III’s campaign against Vannes [6][9].
  - 19 January 1343: Truce of Malestroit [6][8][9].

- Participating forces and commanders: 
  - Montfortist/English: John of Montfort; Joanna of Flanders; Walter Manny; Earl of Northampton; Edward III [1][5][9].
  - Blois–French: Charles of Blois; Duke of Normandy; Olivier IV de Clisson; Geoffrey de Charny [1][6][7][11].

- Casualties: Contemporary narratives for Morlaix emphasize heavy losses among French mounted men-at-arms in repeated attacks and significant prisoner-takings, including Charny; precise totals vary by source and are uncertain [7][9].

## Aftermath

Although campaigning paused under the Malestroit truce, tensions and localized violence continued in Brittany, and the succession remained unresolved. The broader War of the Breton Succession carried on through the 1340s–1360s, with alternating fortunes, major battles (including Auray, 1364), and finally the Treaty of Guérande (12 April 1365), which confirmed Montfort rule under the French crown. The 1341–1343 phase established the military and diplomatic dynamics that would shape the remainder of the conflict [1][9][10].
