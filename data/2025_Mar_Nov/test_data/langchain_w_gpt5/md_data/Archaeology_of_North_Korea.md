# Archaeology of North Korea

Archaeology of North Korea encompasses the study of human material remains within the present-day territory of the Democratic People’s Republic of Korea (DPRK), from the earliest prehistory through historic and early modern periods. Research has identified Paleolithic cave and terrace sites, Neolithic settlements and shell middens with pottery traditions, Bronze Age megalithic dolmen contexts, Iron Age hillforts and protohistoric settlements, and extensive remains of historic polities, notably the Goguryeo (often rendered Koguryo in DPRK and UNESCO sources) and Balhae (Bohai) states, along with later Goryeo and Joseon-period heritage found in the North. The Complex of Koguryo Tombs—about 30 mural and other tombs spanning multiple clusters around Pyongyang—was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List on July 7, 2004, for its outstanding architectural and artistic value and as a record of Goguryeo society; the Historic Monuments and Sites in Kaesong, a serial ensemble of 12 components associated with the Goryeo capital, was inscribed on June 23, 2013 [1][4]. Archaeological practice in the DPRK operates within a distinct institutional and legal framework, with limited access for foreign researchers and constrained publication visibility. External assessments have noted interpretive and methodological differences compared to neighboring countries, and several long-running scholarly debates concern the location and interpretation of Han dynasty Lelang commandery remains, the framing of Goguryeo heritage across DPRK/ROK/PRC scholarship, and the dating or authenticity of specific finds [15][21]. The term “Goguryeo” is widely used in international scholarship, while “Koguryo” is employed in DPRK documents and UNESCO materials; both refer to the same kingdom (ca. third century BCE to 668 CE) [1][5].

## Scope and terminology

This article surveys archaeology within the DPRK’s present borders, focusing on physical remains, site sequences, research history, institutions and laws, methods and conservation, major discoveries, and documented controversies. It excludes general political history except where directly relevant to archaeological interpretation, management, or access.

Terminology varies across sources. UNESCO and DPRK documents typically use “Koguryo,” while many international academic works use “Goguryeo”; both designate the same historical state [1][5]. Balhae is often rendered Bohai in Chinese scholarship.

## Natural and research context

Archaeological investigations in the North have recorded cave sites, river terrace deposits, shell middens, burial mounds, fortifications, urban foundations, palatial platforms, observatories, and religious architecture. The published record is shaped by DPRK institutions and heritage law, selective international collaboration, and UNESCO nomination and monitoring processes for its World Heritage properties [1][2][4]. Within the DPRK, archaeology has been linked to national heritage agendas and cultural policy, including the designation of non-physical heritage since 2012 and a broadened legal framework in 2015 and 2019 [16][17].

## Periodization and cultural sequence

### Paleolithic

Evidence of Paleolithic occupation includes cave and terrace sites around the Pyongyang region and elsewhere. DPRK announcements and museum displays identify Komunmoru (a cave site near Sangwon) as an early Paleolithic locale; scholarly assessments caution that detailed stratigraphic and dating information from DPRK sources is limited in availability outside the country [21]. DPRK media in July 2019 reported late Paleolithic finds—stone tools, bone implements, and mammal fossils—near the Yesong River in North Hwanghae Province, with electron paramagnetic resonance and thermoluminescence cited in separate DPRK reports for other cave sites; the UPI report attributed these claims to KCNA [22]. A peer-reviewed abstract notes luminescence dating work at the Joohyondong Cave site near Hyangmok-ri (Kangdong County), reflecting the application of physicochemical dating methods to DPRK Neolithic/Paleolithic contexts [23].

### Neolithic (pottery traditions and subsistence)

On the Korean Peninsula, the Neolithic period features pottery traditions, varying degrees of sedentism, and mixed subsistence including fishing, foraging, and cultivation. A peninsula-wide synthesis of radiocarbon dates shows that shell middens and pit-house settlements proliferated, and that cultivation of broomcorn and foxtail millet is supported by AMS dating by the middle Neolithic; rice impressions in Neolithic pottery are rare or absent in the verified record [11]. The absolute chronology for the earliest pottery tradition (Gosan-ri-type) has been modeled in Korea using radiocarbon and statistical methods, placing its production around the mid-eighth millennium BCE; these results inform comparative sequences that include northern sites [12]. DPRK research has reported Jeulmun (comb-patterned) pottery and shell middens along the Taedong River basin, including early excavations at Unggi/Hoeryong, although detailed, openly accessible excavation reports remain scarce in English [34].

### Bronze Age (dolmens and associated contexts)

Dolmens are widespread across the Korean Peninsula and are generally associated with Bronze Age societies. UNESCO and ICOMOS describe three typological clusters—table/northern type (above-ground cist under a capstone), go-board/southern type (underground chamber with a capstone supported on ground-laid stones), and variants with capstones laid directly on buried slabs—and outline construction techniques and chronology from the seventh to third centuries BCE based on well-dated southern sites [13][14]. Dolmens occur extensively within DPRK territory, but site-level distribution and counts are less documented in accessible international literature; comparative typology and chronology are used to frame northern assemblages in relation to peninsula-wide Bronze Age developments [13].

### Iron Age and protohistoric periods

Asian Perspectives identifies the Early Iron Age in Korea roughly from 300 to 100 BCE, with evidence suggesting iron technology arrived via contact with the Yan polity and spread through trade networks; domestic iron production becomes archaeologically visible by the later first millennium BCE [10]. Archaeological patterns from the fourth century BCE into the early centuries CE show fortified settlements, walled sites, and evolving material culture. Studies of Goguryeo fortification techniques highlight construction features that also appear in late phases after the capital’s relocation to Pyongyang in 427 CE, providing a comparative framework for DPRK fortresses [36].

### Historic states: Goguryeo and Balhae

Goguryeo originated in the northeastern borderlands and shifted its capital multiple times: to Kungnae Castle (Ji’an) in 3 CE, to the Mt. Taesong area near Pyongyang in 427 CE, and to Jangan Castle in central Pyongyang later in the fifth century; the kingdom fell in 668 CE [1]. Within the DPRK, extensive Goguryeo funerary landscapes include stone-chambered tombs under earthen mounds with complex structural ceilings and mural programs depicting cosmology, processions, and daily life [1][5][6]. Balhae (Bohai), a successor state in the northeast, encompassed parts of Northeast China, the northern Korean Peninsula, and the Russian Far East; joint DPRK–China projects and Korean-led research have documented Balhae fortresses and tomb clusters in North Hamgyong Province (Heoryeong) and near Cheongjin [7][8][9].

### Later Goryeo and Joseon remains in the North

The Kaesong area preserves major Goryeo-period monuments and urban features, including city walls, palace terraces, observatory, gates, academies, and royal mausolea; many were repaired or reconstructed under DPRK management. The Kaesong serial property illustrates political and philosophical content of the Goryeo state and later Joseon conservation practices within the DPRK territory [3][4].

## Major sites and discoveries

### Complex of Koguryo Tombs (UNESCO World Heritage)

The serial World Heritage property comprises about 30 tombs across multiple clusters around Pyongyang, Nampho, and South Hwanghae Province. The nomination and ICOMOS evaluation emphasize structural innovation in spanning wide chambers, varied burial plans, and mural programs of high artistic quality. The property was inscribed under criteria (i), (ii), (iii), and (iv) [1].

- Composition and typology: Stone-piled and stone-chambered tombs under earthen mounds; single-, two-, multi-, and side-chambered plans with corridors. Ceilings use corbeling, lantern tiers, and other load-bearing designs to support heavy superstructures [1].
- Murals and themes: Portraits of occupants, the Four Directional Deities (Azure Dragon, White Tiger, Vermilion Bird, Black Warrior), processions, hunting, dance and music, astronomy (sun with three-legged crow, moon with toad/rabbit, Big Dipper, Milky Way), decorative cloud and lotus motifs; genre scenes record architecture, clothing, instruments, arms, and activities [1][5][6].
- Conservation: Interiors and murals are largely preserved; issues include mural deterioration in some tombs, occasional flooding, and legacy interventions. ICOMOS recommended enhanced monitoring and lighting, interpretive signage, a visitor management plan, and pigment studies; it also noted the desirability of coordination with PRC for broader serial context [1][2].

#### Anak Tomb No. 3

Anak Tomb No. 3 (South Hwanghae Province) is one of the most detailed mural tombs and among the few with a dated inscription. The epitaph records completion in 357 CE. The tomb contains portraits of the deceased couple, a corridor procession exceeding 200 figures, and extensive scenes of domestic spaces (e.g., mill, kitchen, carriage shed, stable) and guards with banners and axes, along with lotus motifs and musical performances [7]. Architectural features include an entrance passage, antechamber, two side rooms, a main chamber, and an L-shaped gallery; polished basalt and limestone were used, with murals painted directly on stone using preliminary sketches, polychrome fills, and black ink outlines [7]. Comparative scholarship frames Anak No. 3 within the development of Goguryeo mural themes from mixed genre scenes to later dominance of the Four Deities [5][6].

#### Other representative mural tombs and clusters

Scholarly and museum publications describe additional DPRK mural tombs included within or related to the World Heritage property:

- Deokheung-ri Tomb (408 CE, Nampho): Two-chamber layout with a 14-line epitaph identifying an occupant’s biography and burial date; murals include officials, a military procession, the myth of the Oxherd and Weaver, auspicious birds, hunting, and a labeled heavenly horse [5].
- Yaksu-ri Tomb (early 5th century, Nampho): Mixed genre scenes and Four Deities; large hunting panel; astronomical motifs (sun, moon, seven stars) [5].
- Ssangyeongchong (“Twin Pillar Tomb,” late 5th century, Nampho): Corridor with octagonal pillars; genre scenes, immortals, processions led by a monk; richly painted ceilings with sun, moon, and lotus [5].
- Susan-ri Tomb (late 5th century, Nampho): Processions with acrobats; refined female portraits; cross-references to Asuka-period Japanese imagery underscore cultural exchange [5].
- Honam-ri Sasinchong (“Four Deities Tomb,” early 6th century, Pyongyang): Large marble construction; murals focus solely on the Four Deities, indicating a thematic shift [5].
- Kangseo Three Tombs (late 6th–early 7th century, Nampho): Masterpieces of the late period with polished Four Deities iconography and rich ceiling auspicious beings and celestial motifs [5][25].

### Pyongyang-area historical remains (Tentative List)

The DPRK has placed “Historical Relics in Pyongyang” on UNESCO’s Tentative List, indicating an intention to develop a nomination for urban and monumental heritage associated with Goguryeo capital functions and later periods. Tentative List content is submitted by the State Party and published by UNESCO with a disclaimer that responsibility for its details lies with the State Party [33]. Published World Heritage documentation on the Koguryo Tombs outlines Goguryeo’s capital moves to Mt. Taesong and central Pyongyang, and mentions defensive works of Jangan Castle in the city area [1].

### Historic Monuments and Sites in Kaesong (UNESCO World Heritage)

The Kaesong serial property consists of 12 components that collectively illustrate the Goryeo capital’s planning, education, defenses, ritual landscapes, and funerary culture [3][4]. Key components include:

- Kaesong City Walls: An Outer Wall (constructed 1009–1029), Inner Wall (1391–1393), and Palocham Wall (896), reflecting successive urban/rural defensive strategies [3].
- Manwoldae Palace and Kaesong Chomsongdae Observatory: The palace foundation (established 919) preserves terraces and central halls; the observatory platform is associated with early Goryeo astronomy [3].
- Namdae Gate: The main south gate of the Inner Wall (1393) features a granite base and reconstructed wooden pavilion housing the 1346 Yonbok Temple bell [3].
- Educational institutions and bridges: Koryo Songgyungwan (academy), Sungyang Sowon (Confucian academy, 1573), Sonjuk Bridge and Phyochung Monuments (stelae of 1740 and 1872) [3].
- Royal mausolea: Mausoleum of King Wang Kon (restored exterior in 1994), Mausoleum of King Kongmin and Queen (completed 1372), and the Seven Tombs/Myongrung cluster [3][4].

ICOMOS noted legal protection through DPRK law and regulations, a management plan approved in January 2011, and issues such as urban development in buffer zones, heavy summer rains, insect damage risks, and the need for visitor flow management [3][4]. Inter-Korean joint excavations at Manwoldae have occurred in multiple campaigns, including an extended season in 2015; public reporting and official project pages document finds and collaboration logistics [26][27][28][29].

### Balhae (Bohai) sites in North Hamgyong

A joint excavation (2012–2013) by the Archaeological Research Institute of the DPRK Academy of Social Sciences and Yanbian University’s Balhae History Institute investigated fortresses (Ingyeri Earthen Fortress, Undu Fortress, Donggeon Fortress) and the Gungsim tomb complex in Heoryeong, North Hamgyong Province, producing a detailed report on site distributions, stratigraphy, and chronology [7]. Additional DPRK publications summarize work at Bugeori (Cheongjin) and link DPRK investigations to broader Northeast Asian Balhae research, including temple sites at Kraskino across the Tumen (Duman) River in Russia; these materials are part of a long-running scholarly effort to define Balhae urban and mortuary landscapes across modern borders [8][9].

### Bronze Age dolmen concentrations

UNESCO’s ICOMOS evaluation of the Republic of Korea’s dolmen properties established typological and chronological frameworks applicable to peninsula-wide dolmens, including those within DPRK territory. It documented quarrying, transport, and erection techniques and emphasized that Korea has the largest concentration of dolmens known worldwide; comparative notes have suggested the potential for northern dolmen inclusion in future serial nominations when feasible [13][14].

### Shell middens, settlements, and material culture

Early DPRK archaeology reported shell middens and mixed-material culture sites in the northeast (Unggi/Hoeryong), with subsequent work across Jeulmun pottery-bearing settlements and Mumun plain pottery contexts; much of the openly accessible synthesis is peninsula-wide, and DPRK site-level excavation data are less available in English-language publications [11][34].

## History of archaeological research

### Japanese colonial-era archaeology (pre-1945)

Systematic survey and excavation in northern Korea began under Japanese rule. Colonial-era laws on protection of “ancient remains and relics” were enacted and Governor-General-sponsored committees inventoried and excavated sites, including fortified places and tombs. Colonial scholarship’s emphasis on confirming Han dynasty commandery centers around Pyongyang (especially Lelang) intertwined archaeology with political legitimation [15][34]. Museum institutions in Pyongyang and Kaesong were established, and materials entered Japanese collections [34].

### Post-1948 DPRK institutions and campaigns

Archaeology in the DPRK was institutionalized under the Academy of Social Sciences, with an Institute of Archaeology and related museum and cultural preservation bodies. Journals such as Munhwa yusan (1957–1962), Kogo minsok (from 1963), and Joseon gogo yeongu (from 1986) have served as main outlets; a recent bibliometric analysis counted 1,656 articles in Joseon gogo yeongu through 2019 and charted shifts in topical emphasis across leadership eras [35][34]. Major early postwar projects included excavations of Goguryeo tombs and Bronze/Neolithic sites. Salvage archaeology and restoration accompanied infrastructure and urban works, and DPRK heritage bodies managed repairs and reconstruction at serial properties like Kaesong [3][4][31].

### International collaborations

UNESCO advisory evaluations and periodic reporting have engaged DPRK authorities on Koguryo and Kaesong properties, with recommendations on conservation, monitoring, and management planning [1][2][3][20]. ICOMOS encouraged DPRK and PRC to consider future joint nomination coordination for Goguryeo serial properties across borders [1][20]. The DPRK has carried out joint excavations on Balhae sites with Chinese institutions in Heoryeong and published reports intended for wider academic use [7][8]. Inter-Korean archaeological collaboration at Manwoldae in Kaesong has been reported across multiple excavation seasons and institutional channels in 2007 and later, including an extended 2015 season [26][27][28][29].

### Publication patterns

DPRK archaeology publications have emphasized national heritage, historical continuity, and ideological frameworks, with shifts toward Kaesong and Manwoldae topics noted in recent bibliometric studies. Language barriers and limited distribution mean that many DPRK excavation reports are not widely accessible in English, and external syntheses often rely on UNESCO documentation, inter-Korean project reports, and comparative peninsula-wide research [35][11][34].

## Institutions and legal frameworks

Heritage management in the DPRK is organized through national and local bodies. UNESCO/ICOMOS documentation identifies:

- Management Bureau for Cultural Property Conservation (MBCPC) under the Ministry of Culture, with provincial/city Bureaus of Cultural Preservation, local cultural preservation departments, and Cultural Relics Management Offices (CRMO); site managers and caretakers implement on-site protection and monitoring [1][3].
- Legal protection deriving from the Socialist Constitution, specific cultural property laws and regulations, and related environmental and land management laws. The Historic Monuments and Sites in Kaesong nomination cites the Law on Protection of Cultural Property (1994) and its implementing regulations (2009), administered by the National Bureau for Cultural Property Conservation (NBCPC), along with a management plan approved in 2011 [3][4].
- Policy evolution since 2012: DPRK enacted a Cultural Heritage Protection law that expanded to intangible heritage, then adopted the Law on the Protection of National Heritage in 2015 (six chapters, 62 clauses; later amended in January 2019 to refine definitions, expand non-physical heritage categories, remove individual ownership, and prioritize excavation, preservation, and economic use). The amended law directs a central institution to pursue UNESCO registrations strategically [16][17].

Museums and academic bodies include the Korean Central History Museum (Pyongyang), the Academy of Social Sciences (Institute of Archaeology), and university departments; these institutions conduct research, curation, and public display of excavated materials [1][35].

## Methods and technology

DPRK archaeology employs excavation, survey, typology, and laboratory techniques, with documented use of radiocarbon (14C), accelerator mass spectrometry (AMS), and luminescence dating in peninsula-wide studies and in specific DPRK site contexts (e.g., Joohyondong Cave abstract). Asian Perspectives provides radiocarbon datasets and analytical methods for Neolithic sedentism and settlement sequences that inform comparative frameworks inclusive of the DPRK sector [10][11][23]. Conservation of mural tombs involves environmental monitoring (hygrometry, temperature), lighting control, and protective measures; ICOMOS recommended enhancing equipment and pigment analysis, installing alarms and protective doors, and developing visitor management plans for serial properties [1][2][3]. Museum and research publications document pigment palettes (e.g., cinnabar, lead white, ochre, verdigris, carbon ink) and iconographic programs in mural tombs, contributing to studies of materials and techniques [25][5].

## Heritage management and conservation

World Heritage serial properties in the DPRK have defined management systems, legal protections, and monitoring protocols. For Koguryo Tombs, ICOMOS noted authenticity of interiors and murals, identified specific risks (deterioration, flooding), and recommended research and visitor management actions; UNESCO’s documents page records periodic reporting and mission follow-ups [1][2]. The Kaesong property’s management plan sets conservation priorities for walls, gates, palatial terraces, academies, and mausolea, with legal backing from DPRK cultural property laws and buffer zone guidelines; ICOMOS identified potential adverse visual impacts from ancillary facilities and stressed mitigation of environmental threats [3][4]. DPRK official media report periodic restoration activities (e.g., repairs to temples, mausolea, and city walls; tree planting; insect monitoring), and national heritage promotion months, reflecting public heritage campaigns [30][31].

## Access, data limitations, and reliability

Access to DPRK sites for foreign researchers is limited, and DPRK publications are not widely distributed internationally. UNESCO publishes State Party nomination files and evaluations but notes that content in national submissions is the responsibility of the submitting State and does not imply positions on legal status or boundaries [20]. External analyses highlight ideological framing and nationalist narratives within DPRK archaeology and caution about verification challenges; paleolithic claims have been critiqued for limited evidentiary support in openly accessible datasets, and the use of traditional accounts for chronological claims has been noted [21][34]. Bibliometric work demonstrates sustained domestic scholarly output, but language and access constraints impede comprehensive external review [35].

## Scholarly debates and controversies

- Goguryeo heritage across borders: Because Goguryeo sites and mural tombs span present-day DPRK and PRC territories (Ji’an region), interpretive disputes and heritage claims have involved institutions in both countries. UNESCO inscribed separate serial properties in 2004 and encouraged dialogue on future joint nomination possibilities [1][20].
- Lelang commandery: Archaeological and historical evidence supports a Han dynasty commandery centered near Pyongyang from 108 BCE; some DPRK scholarship has contested this localization. Hyung Il Pai’s World Archaeology study analyzes the material record—seals, mirrors, jades, ornaments—within an acculturation framework, providing a methodological reference point for debates on commandery presence and cultural dynamics [15].
- Dangun mausoleum and early claims: External observers have cited DPRK uses of archaeology in national historiography, including strong critiques of claims surrounding the Dangun mausoleum; the ISDP study surveys paleolithic and early state narratives and their political contexts [21].

## Recent developments up to 2025

UNESCO’s documents page for the Koguryo Tombs records periodic reporting cycles, including submissions in the 2012 and 2023 cycles, and advisory follow-ups on conservation and management issues [2]. Inter-Korean excavations continued at Manwoldae in Kaesong, with notable collaborative seasons reported publicly in 2015 and institutional pages documenting joint work and findings [26][27][28][29]. DPRK heritage policy evolved through amendments to the Law on the Protection of National Heritage in January 2019, refining definitions and management of non-physical heritage and emphasizing UNESCO registration strategies [16][17]. DPRK media have reported ongoing maintenance and restoration works at heritage sites, including Kaesong municipal actions and national heritage promotion periods [30][31].

## Notes on names and transliteration

Romanization of Korean place names varies by system (Revised Romanization, McCune–Reischauer) and across DPRK, ROK, and international usage; UNESCO and DPRK materials often render names in DPRK transliteration (e.g., “Koguryo,” “Nampho”), while international academic works may use “Goguryeo,” “Nampo.” This article follows the spellings used in cited sources and notes widely accepted variants where helpful [1][4][5].
