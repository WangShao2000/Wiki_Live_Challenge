# Wishworld

Wishworld (also styled Wish World or WishWorld) most prominently refers to the in‑universe name for Earth in Disney Publishing Worldwide’s Star Darlings multimedia franchise, created by Shana Muldoon Zappa and Ahmet Zappa and launched in 2015. In the series, students from Starling Academy in Starland travel to Wishworld to identify and help grant true wishes; the concept appears across print books from Disney Press, digital editions, web videos, Disney Channel specials, and consumer products tie‑ins [1][2][3][4]. The term has also been used as the title of a Doctor Who television episode that aired on BBC One in 2025, as well as for unrelated games, music releases, mobile apps, and a company name; these uses are listed with due weight in the “Other uses” section [17][20][21][22][23][24].

## Definition and scope

Within the Star Darlings franchise, Wishworld denotes Earth—the planet to which Starling wish‑granters travel in disguise to locate a “wisher,” discern their true desire, and convert successful wish‑granting into positive wish energy that powers Starland [1][4][5][6]. In an interview conducted at the franchise’s launch, Ahmet Zappa described the premise as “the secret origin of how wishes get granted,” noting that the first Star‑Charmed Starlings are “the first to travel to Earth (called Wishworld) to help people find happiness and fulfillment” [4]. Disney Publishing’s official series page and book synopses consistently frame missions as assignments to Wishworld, with plot arcs intertwining Starland’s energy crisis and events on Earth [1][5][6][8].

## Origin and creation

Star Darlings was developed by Disney Consumer Products and Interactive Media (DCPI) with creators Shana Muldoon Zappa and Ahmet Zappa beginning in 2011, and revealed publicly at Disney’s D23 Expo in August 2015 as one of the first original brands from the combined DCPI operation [3]. The concept centers on twelve “Star‑Charmed” students at Starling Academy who study wish‑granting and undertake missions to Wishworld, a structure designed to encourage confidence and empowerment among tween readers, according to Disney Publishing Worldwide EVP Andrew Sugerman [3]. The series premise and role of Wishworld are restated across Disney Publishing materials and synopses [1][5][6].

## First appearance and release timeline

- Books: Disney Press published the first main‑series title, Sage and the Journey to Wishworld, on September 15, 2015 (paperback; ISBN 9781423166435), with age range 8–12 and grades 3–7; Penguin Random House distributes and catalogs the title [2]. Publishers Weekly listed Sage and the Journey to Wishworld in its “Hot Off the Presses: Week of September 14, 2015” series launch roundup, signaling the start of the franchise’s publication program [15]. Subsequent volumes followed through 2016–2017 across main and companion arcs (examples below) [8][9][10][12][13][14].
- Television specials: Disney Channel aired two half‑hour specials—Becoming Star Darlings on January 29, 2016, and Star Darlings Friendships on March 25, 2016—introducing the twelve Star‑Charmed girls and their first missions to Earth (“Wishworld”) [16].
- Web videos: Disney published short‑form videos and webisodes tied to the franchise, featuring Wishworld‑related scenarios drawn from the books [18].
- Consumer products: The property rolled out as a multimedia brand including dolls and apparel, with Jakks Pacific’s lines explicitly differentiating “Starland Fashion Dolls” (fantastical looks) and “Wishworld Fashion Dolls” (down‑to‑earth styles) [16]. Contemporary toy reviews documented both the distribution and the visual distinction between “Starland” and “Wishworld” versions [13].

## Premise, worldbuilding, and key features

Starland, a world inhabited by magically gifted Starlings, relies on positive wish energy generated when good wishes are successfully granted on Wishworld. The franchise’s worldbuilding describes a system of “Wish Orbs” (good, bad, and impossible), invisible to human eyes and used by Starlings to focus and convert wishes into energy; mission protocols emphasize discretion and the ethical navigation of wish‑granting [5][6]. Canon materials document an energy crisis in Starland, sabotage against the Star Darlings program, and mysteries tied to leadership figures, all of which entwine with missions conducted on Earth (Wishworld) [5][12][13][14]. Across volumes, individual character arcs unfold through distinct Wishworld assignments (for example, animal shelter work in Tessa’s Lost and Found or election dynamics in Libby and the Class Election), underscoring the franchise’s central conceit that Wishworld is the practical arena for Starling training and service [1][14].

## Ownership and intellectual property

Star Darlings is a Disney Publishing Worldwide property; titles are published by Disney Press. U.S. trademark records show Disney Enterprises, Inc. holds the federal registration for STAR DARLINGS (Reg. No. 5355636) covering goods/services associated with the brand; use of “Wishworld” functions as an in‑universe term within the series rather than a separately registered mark [2][16][25]. Consumer products, including dolls and apparel, were produced in partnership with Jakks Pacific and marketed with “Starland” and “Wishworld” positioning [16][13].

## Reception

- Trade coverage: Kidscreen reported the multimedia launch and strategic intent to reach the “tween girl marketplace,” describing Star Darlings as an original wish‑themed property with books, short‑form content, an app, dolls, and apparel; the coverage also documented development since 2011 [3]. Publishers Weekly signaled the series’ arrival in its “Hot Off the Presses” roundup in September 2015 [15].
- Reviews: Kirkus Reviews assessed several volumes. For Libby and the Class Election (2015), Kirkus remarked that “the humor is far stronger on Wishworld than at Starling Academy,” noting improvement over the first volume [1]. For the later Good Wish Gone Bad (2016), Kirkus characterized the installment as “back‑story heavy” with a large cast, suggesting it primarily served established fans aging with the series [9].
- Consumer products commentary: Reviews of the doll line documented distribution, quality, and the “Wishworld” vs. “Starland” styling distinction, indicating alignment between the books’ setting dichotomy and merchandising [13].

## Cultural impact and related media

The franchise extended beyond print into broadcast specials, web video, and consumer products, with the Wishworld concept—positioning Earth as a destination for missions—serving as the thematic bridge among formats [16][18]. Launch coverage emphasized empowerment themes and wish‑granting as a narrative metaphor intended for tween audiences [3]. While mainstream awards and sales figures for the book series were not widely reported in trade sources consulted here, the cross‑media rollout and trademark registration reflect Disney’s standard brand development practices [3][25].
