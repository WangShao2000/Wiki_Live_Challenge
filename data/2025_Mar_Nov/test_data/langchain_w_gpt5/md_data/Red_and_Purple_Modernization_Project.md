# Chicago Transit Authority Red and Purple Modernization (RPM)

The Red and Purple Modernization (RPM) is a multi-phase capital program by the Chicago Transit Authority (CTA) to rebuild, modernize, and expand capacity on the North Side portions of the Red and Purple Lines. RPM replaces nearly century-old infrastructure, adds a grade-separated bypass to remove a major junction bottleneck, reconstructs stations to meet accessibility standards, and installs a new higher-capacity signal system. The first phase (RPM Phase One) began design-build in 2019, put the Red-Purple Bypass into service in November 2021, and reopened four rebuilt Red Line stations in July 2025; remaining punch-list work continued through late 2025 [1][2][3][14][33]. RPM is part of CTA’s broader Red Ahead initiative and is distinct from the Red Line Extension (RLE) project, which is a separate south-side expansion with its own planning, environmental reviews, and financing [2][34][35].

## Scope and Phases

RPM is structured as a multi-phase corridor program focused on the North Side mainline of CTA’s Red and Purple Lines. The overarching program goal is to renew and optimize approximately 9.6 miles of rail infrastructure and stations on the North Side, addressing state-of-good-repair needs and capacity constraints while improving accessibility and reliability [1][2][36].

- RPM Phase One scope:
  - Construction of the Red-Purple Bypass (RPB) north of Belmont Station, creating a grade-separated flyover for Brown Line trains at Clark Junction to eliminate an at-grade crossing with Red and Purple Line tracks [2][3][4][5].
  - Complete reconstruction of four Red Line stations: Lawrence, Argyle, Berwyn, and Bryn Mawr, including fully ADA-accessible designs and expanded platforms [2][7][14].
  - Replacement and modernization of track structures and viaducts, including approximately six miles of track structure and associated right-of-way improvements within the Phase One footprint [2][36].
  - Installation of a new signal system over 23 track miles between Howard and Belmont to improve train flow and reliability [3][14][26].

CTA states that with Phase One’s completion, the agency has initiated planning for subsequent phases via the RPM Next Phases Study, a multi-year process to define goals, engage the public, and evaluate alternatives for remaining corridor segments and related Purple Line improvements [1][2][5].

## Geographic Coverage and Assets Affected

RPM Phase One focuses on the Red and Purple Lines’ North Side mainline:

- Red-Purple Bypass at Clark Junction: Located just north of Belmont Station in Lakeview, where northbound Brown Line trains previously crossed the inner Red/Purple tracks at-grade, causing delays. The bypass raises Brown Line movements over the Red/Purple tracks on a fifth, grade-separated structure [3][4][5].
- Stations reconstructed: Lawrence, Argyle, Berwyn, and Bryn Mawr on the Red Line (Uptown and Edgewater). The rebuild includes new entrances, elevators, widened platforms, and modern facilities [7][14].
- Track and structures: Reconstruction and realignment of segments between Belmont and Addison (North Belmont) and between Leland Avenue and near Ardmore Avenue (Lawrence to Bryn Mawr), including straightening curves and widening structures for safety and maintenance walkways [7][13][36].
- Signals and power: A new signal system between Howard and Belmont replaces a 60-year-old system; traction power improvements include upgrades such as the Broadway substation to support reliability and capacity [3][14][29].

The City’s RPM Phase One Transit TIF district spans roughly a half mile on either side of the right-of-way from Devon Avenue south to North Avenue, encompassing 16,704 parcels and supporting investments like the four station reconstructions, track/viaduct renewal, and the bypass [21].

## Planning History and Rationale

RPM responds to chronic capacity constraints and aging infrastructure:

- Capacity and reliability constraints at Clark Junction: Prior to RPB, northbound Brown Line trains crossed Red/Purple tracks at grade, creating a chokepoint that delayed up to 40% of trains through the junction during peak periods [5].
- State of good repair: The elevated structures and signals on the North Side were past useful life, with condition ratings reflecting the need for replacement [5][7].
- Goals: Increase corridor capacity, reduce crowding and delays, improve travel times and reliability, and provide fully accessible stations with 60–80-year design lives [7][17][22].

Environmental review:

- Corridor-level planning began with Tier 1 EIS scoping in 2011 to evaluate modernization along the North Red and Purple Lines [8].
- Project-level NEPA documents were prepared for Phase One elements:
  - Red-Purple Bypass: Environmental Assessment (EA) and Section 4(f) evaluation (May 2015), followed by a Finding of No Significant Impact (FONSI) (October 2015). The EA detailed the flyover design, realignments, and property acquisitions; the FONSI found no significant impacts with committed mitigation [4][5].
  - Historic resources: The Section 106 technical memorandum documented adverse effects and mitigation for NRHP-listed resources, including the elevated structure and nearby historic buildings such as the Vautravers Building [6].
  - Lawrence to Bryn Mawr Modernization: Project Summary and EA materials (May 2015) outlined station reconstruction, track structure renewal over 1.3 miles, noise barriers, and staging with temporary stations; a FONSI was issued later in 2015 [7][8].

## Timeline and Key Milestones

- 2009–2012: Early corridor visioning and Tier 1 EIS scoping and preliminary engineering [8].
- 2016: City Council created the RPM Phase One Transit TIF by a 46–0 vote to support the local match [23][21].
- January 2017: CTA and City announced the Federal Transit Administration (FTA) Full Funding Grant Agreement (FFGA) of approximately $1 billion for Phase One (FTA Core Capacity), the final step to secure federal funding [18][19]. FTA’s project profile documents a total Phase One cost of $2,066.7 million and Section 5309 Core Capacity funding of $957 million [17].
- December 12, 2018: Chicago Transit Board advanced RPM Phase One, awarding a $1.2 billion design-build contract to the Walsh–Fluor Design-Build Team; the Owner’s Representative was selected as Elevated Solutions Partners [9][29].
- February 8, 2019: Notice to Proceed to the design-build team; site mobilization and groundbreaking followed later in 2019 [10].
- May 16, 2021: Stage A began with demolition and reconstruction of the four North Side stations and partial track closures; temporary stations at Argyle and Bryn Mawr opened [11].
- November 19, 2021: Red-Purple Bypass entered service at 4:00 a.m., eliminating the at-grade crossing north of Belmont [3].
- July 28, 2023: Stage B (final major phase of the four-station and track reconstruction) began; service patterns adjusted at temporary stations [12].
- December 4, 2023: Two new Red/Purple track structures between Belmont and Addison entered service, advancing North Belmont reconstruction [13].
- July 20, 2025: Reopening of four rebuilt ADA-accessible Red Line stations—Lawrence, Argyle, Berwyn, Bryn Mawr. Temporary stations closed [14][16].
- Late 2025: Remaining punch-list and finishing work continued (e.g., fencing, grading, rail grinding, equipment removal), with Phase One construction nearing completion [1][33].

## Budget, Funding Sources, and Financial Mechanisms

Phase One’s budget is approximately $2.1 billion, funded with a mix of federal and local sources [9][17][20][21][22][25]:

- Federal:
  - FTA Capital Investment Grants (Core Capacity) via FFGA: approximately $957 million (FTA profile); the City/CTA announced a FFGA of about $1 billion in January 2017 [17][18][19].
  - Congestion Mitigation and Air Quality (CMAQ): approximately $125 million awarded to CTA to support signal improvements and related work [20].
- Local:
  - Transit Tax Increment Financing (Transit TIF; RPM Phase One): $625 million local match through a designated district surrounding the corridor, created by the City Council in 2016 [21][23][22].
  - CTA financing: authority sales tax bonds and other debt/notes to cover the local share (City ordinance authorized up to $622 million in TIFIA loan proceeds or equivalent CTA bonds, though the City/CTA ultimately chose a pay-as-you-go approach rather than pursuing a TIFIA loan, per later reporting) [20][25].

Financial performance and evolution:

- The City Council’s Office of Financial Analysis projected the Transit TIF to raise more than $851 million over 17 years, with a portion earmarked to repay expected borrowing if taken; the ordinance also authorized pathways for CTA bonds in lieu of a TIFIA loan [23][20].
- By 2025, the RPM Transit TIF had collected just under $400 million since 2017 and was on pace to deliver the full $625 million for Phase One by 2028; reporting indicated the City opted to pay costs directly rather than take a federal loan, likely reducing interest costs [25].
- Civic Federation analysis noted sustained growth in countywide TIF revenues through 2022, providing context for Transit TIF collections [24].

## Delivery Model and Major Contracts

RPM Phase One was delivered using design-build to overlap design and construction and minimize disruption [9][29]:

- Design-Build Contractor: Walsh Construction Company and Fluor Corporation Joint Venture (Walsh–Fluor Design-Build Team) [9][27][28].
- Owner’s Representative: Elevated Solutions Partners (a team led by Arcadis/Jacobs/Ardmore Roderick) [9][29].
- Lead Designer and key subconsultants: Stantec Consulting Services Ltd. (lead designer), with EXP, International Bridge Technologies, TranSmart/EJM Engineering, and other partners; industry press also cites Atlas Technical Consultants among major subs [26][27][28].
- Workforce, DBE, and community goals: CTA set DBE participation goals at 20% for design and 20% for construction and specific workforce targets; CTA reported extensive DBE participation and workforce hours during execution [29].

The FTA provided project management oversight consistent with Core Capacity grant requirements; CTA managed community engagement and construction staging to maintain service where possible [9][1][29].

## Design Elements and Features

Engineering and architecture under RPM Phase One included:

- Red-Purple Bypass (Flyover):
  - A closed-deck, direct-fixation elevated structure east of the existing mainline that curves west to cross over and merge with the Brown Line at a peak height of about 40–45 feet above ground (up to ~22 feet above existing tracks), with 3–5-foot noise barriers along both sides [4].
  - Realignment and widening of existing Red/Purple structures to modern safety standards, including walkways and clearances; straightening short-radius curves to improve speeds [4][5].
  - EA estimated capital cost at approximately $570 million (year-of-expenditure), including acquisition of 21 properties (16 buildings) needed for permanent right-of-way [4].
- Stations (Lawrence, Argyle, Berwyn, Bryn Mawr):
  - Fully ADA-accessible designs with elevators and escalators at principal entrances, wider platforms, expanded canopies and weather protection, upgraded lighting, signage, seating, and equipment [7][14][26].
  - Viaduct reconstruction with raised clearances (5–10 feet increase over prior conditions), removal of roadway piers to improve street-level conditions, noise barriers, and structures designed for 60–80-year useful lives [7].
  - Historic district considerations (Uptown Square, West Argyle Street, Bryn Mawr Avenue) addressed via design integration and interpretive elements under Section 106 commitments [7].
- Track and structures:
  - Replacement of approximately six miles of track structure and viaducts within the Phase One footprint, plus reconstruction between Belmont and Addison (North Belmont), including two new Red/Purple track structures placed into service in December 2023 [13][36].
  - Construction innovations included top-down segmental installation using custom gantry systems to minimize surface disruption under constrained conditions [36].
- Signals and power:
  - A new higher-capacity signal system spanning 23 track miles between Howard and Belmont to improve train spacing and reliability, replacing a system more than 60 years old [3][14][26].
  - Traction power upgrades including work at the Broadway substation to support higher throughput and reliability [29].

CTA is also pursuing an “Under ‘L’” public-realm project to activate 10 blocks of space beneath the rebuilt elevated tracks between Lawrence and Ardmore with pedestrian trails, dog parks, playgrounds, fitness areas, seating, and flexible public plazas. Construction is anticipated in 2026–2027 [1][14][26][36].

## Expected and Observed Operational Impacts

Modeling and NEPA analyses projected substantial operational benefits:

- Clark Junction capacity: The RPB allows up to eight additional trains per hour through the junction during peak periods, equating to roughly a 30% increase in peak capacity at the chokepoint [5].
- Corridor capacity: FTA’s Core Capacity profile indicates Phase One increases corridor capacity by about 15% (exceeding the 10% minimum for Core Capacity eligibility) [17].
- Reliability and travel time: New signals and straightened track were expected to yield smoother operations and fewer delays. During construction, CTA forecast temporary travel time increases (about 1–2 minutes on Red and up to 4–6 minutes on Purple between Howard and Belmont) due to staging and temporary operations [11].
- Observed changes: Following the RPB commissioning in November 2021, Brown Line trains operated without the prior junction crossing delay; CTA and media noted improved reliability and the removal of the at-grade constraint [3][30][38]. After the 2025 station openings and signal replacement, CTA emphasized enhanced reliability and accessibility benefits, though detailed post-commissioning metrics were not yet widely published by late 2025 [14][1].

As of December 2025, stations and principal track/signal assets were in service, with finishing activities concluding; CTA indicated Phase One construction was wrapping up [1][33].

## Community Engagement, Property Impacts, and Controversies

RPM’s planning and construction included extensive public involvement and documented impacts:

- Public engagement: CTA conducted corridor scoping in 2011, multiple public meetings for project-level EAs, and open houses during design and staging; engagement continued through project storefronts, advisories, and “Open for Business” support for affected local businesses [8][1][7].
- Property acquisitions: The RPB EA identified permanent acquisition of 21 properties (16 buildings) near the bypass, including commercial, residential, and mixed-use buildings; air rights over two properties were also required [4].
- Historic mitigation: Under Section 106, adverse effects were identified for the elevated structure and nearby historic properties. CTA coordinated mitigation measures and notably preserved the 127-year-old Vautravers Building by relocating it approximately 30 feet west (and 10 feet south) rather than demolishing it, enabling track realignment while retaining the historic structure [6][31][32].
- Construction impacts: Staged closures and service adjustments were implemented to maintain operations through temporary stations; CTA provided business support and coordinated with neighborhoods on noise, access, and street closures [7][11][1].
- Controversies and criticisms:
  - Belmont flyover aesthetics and demolitions drew opposition from neighborhood groups; debates included whether modeled delays justified the scale of the bypass and the demolition of more than a dozen buildings [30][4][5].
  - Financing via Transit TIF prompted policy debate. The City Council created the RPM Transit TIF by unanimous vote in 2016; the Council Office of Financial Analysis discussed potential effects on tax rates and repayment structures. Subsequent reporting highlighted strong revenue performance and a policy decision ahead on whether to maintain or dissolve the district after Phase One obligations are met [23][25].
- Construction incident: Industry coverage noted an equipment tip-over involving a drill rig during the project; no injuries were reported and the incident was investigated [37].

## Relationship to Other CTA Projects

RPM is distinct from the Red Line Extension (RLE), which will extend the Red Line south from 95th Street with separate environmental documentation (EIS/ROD) and funding plans, including its own Transit TIF approach. RPM focuses on modernizing and increasing capacity on the North Side mainline, while RLE addresses geographic expansion and access on the Far South Side [2][34][35].

## Future Plans and Next Phases

CTA indicates that with Phase One construction concluding, the agency has begun the RPM Next Phases Study to plan subsequent modernization along remaining North Side segments and Purple Line improvements, with public engagement to define priorities, alternatives, and phasing. Future funding is expected to rely on federal programs (e.g., FTA Core Capacity) and local matches, with details to be determined through the planning process [1][2][5][25].

Complementary improvements include the Under ‘L’ public-realm project beneath the rebuilt structures between Lawrence and Ardmore, scheduled for construction in 2026–2027. CTA continues to publish advisories and updates as final Phase One tasks finish and planning for the next phases progresses [1][14][26][36].
