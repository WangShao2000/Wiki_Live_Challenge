# Love, Anger, Madness

Love, Anger, Madness (French: Amour, colère et folie) is a triptych of three novellas by Haitian writer Marie Vieux-Chauvet. Written in French and first published as a single volume by Éditions Gallimard in Paris in 1968, the book depicts life under authoritarian rule in Haiti through interconnected explorations of class, race, gender, and state terror [2][3]. The volume was rapidly withdrawn from circulation amid fears of reprisals during the Duvalier dictatorship and circulated only sparsely for decades, before authorized French reissues in the 2000s and an English translation published by Modern Library (Random House) in 2009–2010 with an introduction by Edwidge Danticat [4][1][6]. The work has been discussed in scholarship on Haitian literature and women’s writing and is frequently cited for its frank treatment of sexual violence as a tool of political repression and its depiction of social hierarchies under Duvalierism [4][15].

## Nature, authorship, and origin

- Nature of the work: Three novellas published as a single literary volume (triptych) [2][4].
- Author: Marie Vieux-Chauvet (1916–1973), Haitian novelist and playwright [1].
- Original title and language: Amour, colère et folie; French [2][6].
- Country/region of origin: Haiti (subject matter and author), first published in France [2][3].

## Publication history

- Original French edition: Paris: Éditions Gallimard, 1968. 428 pages. Library and bibliographic records identify the publication and extent; contemporary catalog entries and library holdings confirm Gallimard as publisher and the 1968 date [2][3][7][8].
- Withdrawal and limited circulation: Publication generated alarm among those close to the Haitian regime, and Vieux-Chauvet requested withdrawal to protect family still in Haiti. Madison Smartt Bell recounts that remaining copies were discreetly sold by the author’s family and that an unauthorized “pirated” edition surfaced in 2003 [4].
- Later French reissues: The book was republished in France in the mid‑2000s; sources cite houses including Zellige and Maisonneuve et Larose/Emina Soleil as republishers, followed by new pocket editions from Zulma (including a 2021 printing, ISBN 979‑10‑387‑0040‑6) [4][9][6].
- English translation: Modern Library (Random House) published an English translation by Rose‑Myriam Réjouis and Val Vinokur, with an introduction by Edwidge Danticat. The hardcover appeared in 2009 (ISBN 0679643516), followed by a paperback in March 2010 (ISBN 0812976924) and an e‑book (ISBN 9781588368706) [1][11][12][10].

## Historical and cultural context

The triptych was composed and published during the Duvalier era in Haiti (François “Papa Doc” Duvalier, elected 1957; presidency-for-life decreed 1964). The novellas depict the psychological and social effects of state terror, class and color hierarchies, and the permeation of fear into private life. Bell’s account emphasizes how Duvalierism inverted the social order by arming the most marginalized to police and punish the bourgeoisie, and how the regime’s violence colonized citizens’ consciousness, compelling complicity and self‑censorship [4]. Scholarly work in feminist and Haitian studies reads the book’s representation of sexual coercion and assault as a central instrument of political control and a deliberate challenge to silences surrounding such violence [15].

## Synopsis

The volume comprises three novellas whose plots and characters do not overlap but which share setting, motifs, and an escalating sense of siege.

- Love (Amour): Framed as the private journal of Claire Clamont—a darker‑skinned, unmarried woman in an elite mulatto household in a provincial town circa 1939—the narrative follows her unreciprocated love for her brother‑in‑law Jean Luze, her fraught relations with sisters Félicia and Annette, and the tightening grip of a new police commandant, Calédu. Claire observes arbitrary arrests and sexual violence by authorities (including the gang rape of a neighbor, Dora), and she records the collusion between officials and foreign business interests. In a climactic street upheaval, Claire stabs Calédu, redirecting a murder she had long fantasized against her sister [4][2].
- Anger (Colère): A multi‑generational family awakens to find their home and ancestral tomb encircled by “men in black” who function as paramilitaries; their land is confiscated. A brutal enforcer known as “the Gorilla” demands that Rose, the daughter, submit to sustained abuse in exchange for the family’s property rights. Though the land is eventually restored on paper, the family’s standing collapses, multiple members die, and Rose’s sacrifice cannot halt their dispossession and humiliation [4][2].
- Madness (Folie): René, a poor, mixed‑race poet, hides with fellow poets as “devils” overrun the city. The narrative, shifting toward the surreal, records hunger, rum‑fueled delirium, and Vodou‑tinged visions as René longs for Cécile, a young woman from a higher class. After he ventures out with improvised firebombs, the “devils” prove in part to be hallucinations; nevertheless, the authorities summarily condemn the poets for disturbing public order, and Cécile is taken to jail to be raped. The novella ends with apocalyptic and visionary imagery [4].

## Principal characters and setting

- Love: Claire Clamont; sisters Félicia and Annette; Jean Luze (a French expatriate); Commandant Calédu; Dora; Mr. Long (businessman). The action unfolds in a provincial Haitian town, with explicit attention to class and color gradations within the elite [4].
- Anger: Rose and her family (multi‑generational household); “the Gorilla” and “men in black” as local paramilitary figures; a lawyer who manipulates the family’s claims. The setting is a rural or peri‑urban community focused on land and ancestors’ tombs [4].
- Madness: René (first‑person narrator); poets André, Jacques, Simon; neighbor Cécile; the police commandant. The action centers on a besieged urban quarter and blurs between perceived and actual state violence [4].

## Themes and motifs

- Authoritarianism and terror: Arbitrary detention, torture, sexual assault, and summary justice are represented as core techniques of rule, with fear shaping intimate life and self‑censorship [4].
- Class, race, and colorism: The books examine a spectrum of Haitian social hierarchy, from elite mulatto families to impoverished urban intellectuals, and stage tensions between “snobs” and “armed beggars,” with race/class animus often interchangeable in perpetrators’ rhetoric [4].
- Sexual violence as political weapon: Coercion and rape recur as means of domination across the triptych, and later criticism highlights the work’s intervention in public discourse about sexual violence under dictatorship [4][15].
- Entrapment and claustrophobia: Domestic spaces, fenced properties, and barricaded rooms mirror psychological imprisonment; each novella intensifies the sense of enclosure [4].
- Religion, ritual, and the supernatural: Vodou practices, ancestor veneration, and apocalyptic Christian imagery contribute to the movement from realism (Love) to surrealism (Madness) [4].
- Revolt and ambivalence: Moments of rebellion (Claire’s stabbing; plans for violent resistance) coexist with resignation, internalized oppression, and complicity [4].

## Style and genre

Critics describe the triptych as integrated by tone and theme rather than plot continuity. Love is the longest and most realist; Anger occupies a middle ground with tragic structure; and Madness is shorter, lyrical, and hallucinatory. The English translators and introduction frame the book as a “Haitian triptych,” with unified purpose across place (Haiti), time (post‑occupation through Duvalier era), and action (the workings of terror), while shifting modes from diary realism to stream‑of‑consciousness and allegory [4][1].

## Reception

- Contemporary response and suppression: The 1968 publication quickly drew warnings that it would provoke reprisals in Haiti. Vieux‑Chauvet asked Gallimard to suppress the book; copies were recovered or sold discreetly, and the author left for permanent exile in New York, where she died in 1973 [4].
- Later critical assessment: Edwidge Danticat’s introduction to the Modern Library edition places Vieux‑Chauvet among Haiti’s leading twentieth‑century writers and calls Love, Anger, Madness “the cornerstone of Haitian literature,” as quoted by Bell [4]. Reviews and essays in reputable outlets have emphasized the book’s sustained analysis of state power, social hierarchy, and the psychology of fear [4]. Library acquisitions and standard bibliographic coverage reflect the work’s circulation in English and French [10][9][6][7][8].

## Legacy and adaptations

- Stage adaptations: BnF records document a stage adaptation of Amour by José Pliya (mise en scène Vincent Goethals) presented at L’Artchipel in Basse‑Terre in November 2007, and a production of Colère adapted by José Pliya and staged by François Rancillac at L’Artchipel in Basse‑Terre in November 2009 [13][14].
- Scholarship: The book is the subject of academic analysis in Haitian, Caribbean, and feminist studies, including discussions of its representation of sexual violence and its intervention in narratives of the Duvalier era [15].
