# Modes of underwater diving

Underwater diving encompasses several distinct modes defined by how the diver is supplied with breathing gas and supported, the operational pressure regime, the purpose of the dive, and the environment. Authoritative technical and regulatory sources classify modes primarily by breathing gas supply (breath-hold, self-contained, surface-supplied), by operational approach (bounce versus saturation), by sector or purpose (recreational, commercial/occupational, scientific, military, public safety), and by environment (open water, overhead, contaminated, ice, altitude) [1][2][3][4][5][6][7]. Each mode is associated with characteristic equipment configurations and surface support systems, typical depth and duration ranges, decompression or no-decompression practices and planning tools, training and competency standards, procedures and safety systems, hazard profiles and risk mitigations, and, in work settings, applicable legal and regulatory frameworks. This article summarizes those frameworks and modes with citations to official manuals, standards, and peer‑reviewed or professional guidance up to December 12, 2025.

## Classification frameworks used by authoritative sources

- By breathing gas supply and pressure regime:
  - Breath-hold diving (freediving, including snorkeling), where the diver does not breathe compressed gas and remains at ambient pressure [7][8][9][10][11].
  - Self-contained underwater breathing apparatus (scuba), in which the diver carries the breathing medium; open-circuit systems discharge exhaled gas, whereas closed-circuit/semi-closed rebreathers recycle and control gas composition at ambient pressure [1][2][4][13][17][18][19].
  - Surface-supplied diving, in which gas is delivered via an umbilical from the surface; the diver remains at ambient pressure and may use a helmet or full-face mask. Variants include “hookah” systems in light-duty applications [1][5][6][20][21].
  - One-atmosphere systems (e.g., atmospheric diving suits) keep the occupant at surface pressure and are treated in standards as a distinct category of equipment used in diving operations rather than as ambient-pressure diving by a human [6][31].

- By operational approach:
  - Bounce diving: single excursions from surface pressure with decompression (if required) during ascent [1][2].
  - Saturation diving: prolonged living under pressure with transfer under pressure via a closed bell, performing multiple excursions at depth and decompressing once at the end of the project [1][2][22].

- By purpose/sector:
  - Recreational diving (including technical diving in the recreational industry sense), governed internationally by service provider and training standards (ISO 24801/24802/24803 and related snorkeling standards) rather than occupational regulations [4][13][14].
  - Commercial/occupational diving, regulated by national authorities (e.g., U.S. OSHA, UK HSE, CSA Z275 in Canada) and guided by industry codes (IMCA, ADCI) [3][5][6][20][22][23][24][25][26][27].
  - Scientific diving, defined by purpose and process; in the U.S. it may be exempt from commercial diving regulations if strict program criteria are met [7][23][28][29].
  - Military diving, governed by defense manuals (e.g., U.S. Navy Diving Manual) and service qualification schemes [2][30].
  - Public safety diving, which responds to emergencies and is generally outside commercial-diving regulations in the U.S., though agencies adopt their own standards and risk controls [23].

- By environment/specialty:
  - Overhead environments (caves, wreck penetrations, ice) with no direct vertical access to the surface; specialized procedures and redundancy are required [1][5][26].
  - Contaminated water diving, with heightened protective equipment and communications requirements [6][21][26].
  - Altitude diving above sea level, which affects decompression planning and post-dive ascent to altitude [1][12].

These frameworks are consistent across major manuals and standards, though terminology and scope differ slightly between recreational, scientific, and occupational publications [1][2][3][4][5][6][7].

## Breath-hold diving (freediving, including snorkeling)

Definition and scope
- Breath-hold (apnea) diving comprises underwater activities performed on a single breath without breathing apparatus. Sports governing bodies regulate competitive disciplines such as constant weight, dynamic apnea, and static apnea [8][9]. Snorkeling is surface swimming using a snorkel and mask, with brief submergence on a breath-hold; international standards specify minimum requirements for organized snorkeling excursions and guide training [10][11].

Typical objectives and applications
- Recreational exploration, underwater photography, spearfishing where legal, and competitive freediving [8][9][10][11].

Breathing gas supply and equipment
- No compressed gas is breathed. Equipment typically includes mask, snorkel, fins, exposure suit, and in some disciplines, a monofin or bifins; competitive rules constrain equipment per discipline [8][9][10][11].

Depth and duration
- Performance varies by discipline and training. Organized snorkeling is conducted in open water under conditions suitable to the participants’ abilities, with supervision and rescue capabilities per ISO standards [10][11].

Procedures, safety systems, and planning
- Freediving requires buddy supervision and rescue competence. Competitive rules prescribe safety protocols, surface protocols after surfacing, and penalties for prohibited propulsion or assistance [8][9].
- Snorkeling excursions must include risk assessment, participant briefing, rescue capability, and appropriate ratios of guides to participants [10][11].

Training and standards
- Snorkeling excursions and snorkel guide training are covered by ISO 13289 and ISO 13970, which specify competence in rescue, supervision, and environmental awareness [10][11].
- Competitive freediving is governed by AIDA and CMAS rules, which also incorporate anti-doping provisions and discipline-specific conduct [8][9].

Common hazards and mitigations
- Hypoxic blackout (including shallow-water blackout) and barotrauma of descent or ascent are the principal medical risks; equalization techniques and conservative exposure are emphasized in training [7][12].
- DAN guidance emphasizes safe equalization, ear injury prevention, and general fitness for diving; for scuba, minimum surface intervals before flying are well established, and divers often adopt conservative post-apnea practices when mixing modes [12].

Regulatory considerations
- Breath-hold activities are generally not regulated as occupational diving unless performed at work; where at work (e.g., media projects), applicable national regulations and duty-holder responsibilities apply [5][25].

## Self-contained scuba diving

Definition and scope
- Scuba divers carry breathing gas. Open-circuit systems discharge exhaled gas; closed-circuit rebreathers (CCR) and semi-closed systems recycle exhaled gas, adding oxygen and controlling inert gas content [1][2].

Objectives and applications
- Recreation, training, guided dives, scientific observation and sampling, public-safety tasks, and some commercial work under restricted conditions [1][4][7][26].

Breathing gases and mixtures
- Air, nitrox (oxygen-enriched air), and, for deeper exposures, helium mixtures (trimix, heliox) to mitigate nitrogen narcosis and manage oxygen partial pressure [1][2][5][20].
- Occupational guidance commonly recommends limiting working ppO2 to 1.4 bar for nitrox mixtures to control oxygen toxicity risk [5].

Equipment and surface support
- Open-circuit: cylinders, regulator with alternate air source, buoyancy compensator, exposure suit, weights, instruments; for overhead or contaminated water, full-face masks and communications are used [1][21][26].
- Rebreathers: breathing loop, oxygen supply, diluent supply (air/nitrox/helium mixes), scrubber for CO2, electronics to control setpoint and monitor PO2, bailout gas for emergencies [1][17][19].

Depth and duration ranges
- Recreational Level 2 (autonomous) divers under ISO are qualified to 20 m with no in-water decompression stops; deeper diving requires further training [13].
- Occupational and technical dives may be deeper, planned with decompression obligations and appropriate breathing mixes and support [1][2][5][20][26].

Decompression practices and planning tools
- Recreational training emphasizes staying within no-decompression limits (NDL), typically managed by dive computers or tables; provider requirements for training and organized diving are specified in ISO 24803 [4][13][14].
- For decompression diving, planning uses validated decompression tables or computers; U.S. Navy and NOAA tables are widely referenced in professional contexts [1][2]. Deep-stop strategies have been debated; a UHMS workshop concluded that while short deeper stops may reduce bubble scores for some schedules, schedules incorporating deep stops led to severe decompression sickness in some military testing, underscoring the need for evidence-based profiles [16].

Training and certification norms
- Recreational scuba diver competencies are specified in ISO 24801 series, and instructor competencies in ISO 24802 [13][14]. Service provider requirements for training, guided diving, and equipment rental are in ISO 24803 [4].
- Scientific programs adopt additional training and authorization requirements under AAUS-based standards [7].
- Occupational scuba is constrained by national occupational standards and may be prohibited for certain work tasks (e.g., construction) in some jurisdictions [6][26].

Operational procedures and safety systems
- Redundancy (alternate air source; for technical/overhead dives, independent gas sources), pre-dive checks, gas analysis for nitrox/mixes, and emergency oxygen on site are standard practices [1][4][7][26].
- Communications are by hand signals for recreational scuba; in contaminated water or some professional contexts, full-face masks with two-way communications are required [21][26].

Common hazards and risk mitigations
- Decompression sickness (DCS), arterial gas embolism, oxygen toxicity, nitrogen narcosis, hypercapnia (especially with rebreathers), barotrauma, and entanglement/overhead risks. Mitigations include adherence to NDLs or decompression schedules, appropriate gas selection and ppO2 limits, progressive training, conservative ascent rates and safety stops, and equipment redundancy [1][2][4][5][15][16][21].

Regulatory frameworks
- Recreational scuba conducted for leisure is generally outside occupational regulations but is subject to consumer safety and service provider standards (ISO 24803) and national law. Diving “at work” falls under occupational rules regardless of equipment (see Commercial/occupational diving) [3][4][5][6].

### Closed-circuit rebreather (CCR) diving

Definition and scope
- CCRs provide a near-constant oxygen partial pressure by adding oxygen into a recirculating breathing loop and controlling gas composition, improving gas efficiency and thermal comfort and enabling longer and deeper dives when used with appropriate diluents [1][17][19].

Applications
- Technical recreational diving, scientific diving where authorized, and professional/military operations [1][17][19].

Training and standards
- ISO has adopted rebreather training standards covering progressive levels of depth and decompression complexity; an instructor standard (ISO 24808:2024) specifies requirements for CCR instructor training. A series of ISO standards for rebreather divers at different levels has been developed and adopted by training organizations, and several governments have adopted the ISO framework for diver training [17][18].

Hazards and mitigations
- Specific risks include hypoxia, hyperoxia, hypercapnia from scrubber failure or inadequate ventilation, and system floods. Mitigations include rigorous pre-dive checklists, setpoint management, bailout readiness, and training and authorization specific to the unit and mode of use [1][17][19].

## Surface-supplied diving (including hookah)

Definition and scope
- Divers breathe gas delivered from the surface through an umbilical that also carries communications, pneumo-fathometer lines, hot-water supplies, and strength members, using helmets or full-face masks. “Hookah” denotes light-duty surface-supplied air from a compressor to a second-stage regulator without a heavy helmet; its use is limited by standards and regulations [1][5][6].

Objectives and applications
- Occupational diving for construction, inspection, maintenance, and intervention; scientific work where authorized; public-safety operations; limited light-duty work and training with hookah under specific controls [1][5][6][26].

Breathing gases and mixes
- Air and nitrox for moderate depths; surface-supplied mixed-gas operations (nitrox/trimix/heliox) are used for greater depths and bottom times, commonly with a wet bell for safe refuge [5][6][20].

Equipment and surface support
- Required systems include a dive control station, primary and reserve gas supplies, communications, umbilicals, bailout cylinders, launch and recovery (for bells), and a deck decompression chamber for specified operations [5][6][20][24][26].

Depth and duration ranges
- Inland/inshore occupational guidance typically limits surface-supplied air/nitrox to around 50 m, with ppO2 limits and decompression procedures specified; mixed-gas wet-bell operations extend deeper (e.g., to 75 m per IMCA guidance), with strict manning and refuge requirements [5][20].

Decompression practices and planning tools
- Occupational operations use approved decompression tables or procedures and require on-site chamber availability for certain depths or decompression profiles (jurisdiction-specific). For example, Oregon OSHA specifies chamber proximity and limits for scuba and surface-supplied diving; UK HSE specifies team sizes and chamber access principles; Canadian regulations and standards specify chamber requirements and the use of DCIEM tables in some provinces [24][5][26].

Training and standards
- Competency for occupational surface-supplied diving is addressed in CSA Z275.4 (Canada) and equivalent national schemes; industry guidance is provided by IMCA documents for offshore operations [6][20][31][22].

Operational procedures and safety systems
- Continuous voice communications, lifelines, designated person-in-charge/supervisor at the dive location, standby divers, and emergency drills are standard. In contaminated water, full-face masks with two-way communications, dry suits, and enhanced bailout systems are prescribed [21][23][26].

Common hazards and mitigations
- Hazards include DCS, oxygen toxicity, narcosis (for air), delta-P (differential pressure) entrapment, umbilical snagging, and environmental hazards. Controls include risk assessments, equipment redundancy, wet bell refuges, bailout capacity, team size minima, and delta-P avoidance procedures [5][20][21][27].

Regulatory frameworks
- Occupational surface-supplied diving is regulated under national rules (e.g., OSHA 29 CFR 1910 Subpart T in the U.S.; HSE Diving at Work Regulations in the UK; CSA Z275 suite and provincial OHS in Canada) and guided offshore by IMCA and ADCI consensus standards [3][5][6][20][23][24][26][27].

## Saturation and bounce diving

Definitions and distinctions
- Bounce diving entails single exposures from surface to depth and back for each dive, with decompression as required. Saturation diving maintains divers under pressure in a deck chamber complex; they transfer under pressure via a closed bell to work sites and decompress once at the end of the tour [1][2][22].

Applications
- Saturation is used for deep and/or long-duration offshore work where repeated decompressions would be inefficient and risky. Bounce diving is common for shallower or shorter tasks and for dives within limits where sat is not justified [1][2][22].

Breathing gases, equipment, and support
- Mixed gases (heliox/trimix) are used to control narcosis and oxygen exposure. Saturation systems include living chambers, transfer chambers, closed bells, life support, gas reclaim where fitted, and robust launch and recovery systems, subject to design and inspection guidance (IMCA DESIGN documents) [2][22].

Depth and duration
- Saturation dives are conducted over days to weeks at working depths determined by project needs and system capabilities; bounce mixed-gas wet-bell dives may be limited to around 75 m per IMCA guidance, with defined bottom time and bailout planning [20][22].

Decompression and procedures
- Saturation decompression follows controlled schedules over days based on inert gas loading. Bounce dives follow tables with in-water or surface decompression as specified. Emergency procedures, standby and rescue plans, and chamber treatment capabilities must be in place [2][20][22].

Training and regulation
- Occupational standards and company procedures govern competence, manning, and system certification; offshore operations follow IMCA codes and national regulations. The U.S. Navy Diving Manual provides doctrine for military operations [2][5][6][20][22].

Hazards and mitigations
- In addition to general diving risks, sat introduces risks from prolonged confinement, fire, toxic exposures, and complex system failures. Design, maintenance, inspection, and rigorous life-support control are critical mitigations [2][22].

## Purpose/sector categories

### Recreational diving (including “technical” diving)

Scope and governance
- Recreational diving services and training are governed internationally by ISO 24803 (service providers) and ISO 24801/24802 (diver and instructor competencies). These standards define entry-level and advanced competencies, provider duties, supervision, and safety procedures for training, guided diving, and equipment rental [4][13][14].

“Technical diving”
- The term “technical diving” is widely used in the recreational industry for diving beyond conventional recreational limits (e.g., planned decompression, overhead environments, advanced gas mixes), but it is not a legal or regulatory category in occupational frameworks, which classify dives by whether the diver is “at work,” not by techniques used [3][5][6]. Standardization has advanced for specific technologies (e.g., rebreather diver and instructor training), and several governments have adopted ISO training frameworks; nevertheless, occupational regulations apply when diving is performed at work, regardless of whether techniques are branded as “technical” [17][18][3][5][6].

Hazards and mitigations
- Technical dives carry increased risks of DCS, oxygen toxicity, narcosis, and overhead entrapment. Mitigations include specialized training, redundant life-support systems, appropriate gas planning and analysis, team procedures, and conservative decompression [1][4][16].

### Commercial/occupational diving

Scope and governance
- In the U.S., OSHA 29 CFR 1910 Subpart T governs commercial diving operations, with defined exemptions (e.g., instructional scuba within NDLs, scientific programs meeting criteria, public safety) and requirements for supervisors, team size, equipment, decompression, and chamber availability; states may adopt and clarify these rules [3][23][24].
- In the UK, the Diving at Work Regulations 1997 apply across offshore, inland/inshore, scientific/archaeological, and media projects with supporting Approved Codes of Practice (ACOPs) detailing planning, team sizes, ppO2 limits, chamber access, and supervisor duties [5][25][29].
- In Canada, the CSA Z275 series sets the national framework for occupational diving safety codes and competencies, referenced in provincial OHS regulations. Additional provincial rules (e.g., British Columbia OHS Part 24) detail requirements for tables, chamber use, and restrictions on scuba for hazardous work [6][26][31].

Equipment, procedures, and manning
- Occupational operations require designated supervisors (DPIC), risk assessments, standby divers, voice communications for surface-supplied modes, bailout provisions, emergency oxygen, and access to recompression therapy per jurisdictional requirements [23][24][5][6][20][26].
- Offshore guidance (e.g., IMCA) specifies minimum manning and equipment for wet-bell mixed-gas operations; industry standards (ADCI) further detail team sizes and operating limits by mode (including new constraints for occupational scuba in Edition 6.5) [20][27].

Hazards and mitigations
- Hazards include DCS, oxygen toxicity, narcosis, delta-P entrapment, heavy tools, vessel traffic, and contaminated water. Controls are specified in regulations and guidance, including delta-P precautions and contaminated-water configurations (full-face mask with communications, dry suit, bailout with switch block) [5][21][27].

### Scientific diving

Definition and scope
- In the U.S., OSHA recognizes a scientific diving exemption from commercial-diving regulations when programs meet Appendix B criteria, including governance by a Diving Control Board (majority active scientific divers), non-proprietary scientific purpose, observation/data-gathering tasks (not construction/troubleshooting), and divers using scientific expertise [23][28].

Program governance and training
- AAUS-based standards (widely adopted by academic programs) require a Diving Safety Manual, Diving Safety Officer, Diving Control Board, defined training and authorization pathways (typically ≥100 hours of theory/practical), equipment maintenance, air quality standards, and incident reporting. The buddy system is mandatory for scuba; deviations must be documented [7].

Hazards and mitigations
- Research diving emphasizes risk assessment tailored to project tasks and environmental conditions, conservative profiles, emergency oxygen availability, and stringent equipment inspection and gas quality testing [7][28].

Jurisdictional differences
- In the UK, scientific and archaeological diving at work is covered by HSE regulations and the L107 ACOP, and there is no exemption comparable to OSHA’s; program governance and qualification recognition are specified nationally [5][29].

### Military diving

Scope and governance
- Military diving is governed by defense manuals and service qualification schemes (e.g., U.S. Navy Diving Manual Rev. 7A), covering scuba, surface-supplied, mixed-gas, closed bell, and saturation operations for missions including ship husbandry, special operations, and salvage [2].
- National authorities recognize military qualifications for specified at-work diving under approved lists (e.g., HSE Approved List of Diving Qualifications) [30].

### Public safety diving

Scope and governance
- Public safety diving involves emergency response tasks (search, rescue, recovery) for law enforcement and emergency services. In the U.S., such operations are generally exempt from OSHA Subpart T, though agencies typically follow internal standards and best practices for risk management [23]. When operations are planned and non-emergency, some jurisdictions may apply occupational rules.

## Environment-defined specialties and conditions

Overhead environments (cave, wreck penetration, ice)
- Overhead diving removes direct access to the surface; procedures emphasize guideline use, training specific to the environment, gas management rules (e.g., thirds), redundant breathing systems, and surface tenders for ice [1][5][26]. Occupational rules may prohibit scuba for penetration or entrapment-risk tasks, requiring surface-supplied systems [26].

Altitude diving
- Post-dive ascent to altitude and dives at altitude require adjustments to decompression planning or conservative surface intervals; scientific standards advise extended intervals before flying or ascending by land, and altitude corrections when diving [7][12].

Contaminated water
- Occupational standards prescribe category-based controls, with minimum equipment including full-face masks with two-way communications, dry suits, gloves/hoods, and bailout with switch block. Certain work processes are prohibited on scuba in contaminated water; increased crew sizes and procedures are required [21][26].

Polluted and hazardous environments
- Manuals and ACOPs identify additional hazards (currents, visibility, vessel traffic, ROVs, electricity/jetting, lifting operations) and prescribe risk-specific controls (lock-outs, exclusion zones, communications protocols) [5][26].

## Breathing gases and exposure limits

- Air is standard for shallow diving; nitrox increases no-decompression limits by reducing nitrogen fraction but requires gas analysis and control of oxygen exposure. Many occupational codes recommend a maximum working ppO2 of 1.4 bar to mitigate central nervous system oxygen toxicity [1][5].
- Helium-based mixtures (trimix, heliox) reduce narcosis and density at depth for bounce and saturation diving, with selection based on depth, work of breathing, and thermal considerations [1][2][20][22].
- Gas planning includes reserve/bailout capacity: occupational guidance specifies minimum bailout durations/volumes for surface-supplied divers and wet-bell operations [20][26][27].

## Decompression management and post-dive considerations

- Professional operations use validated tables and procedures (e.g., U.S. Navy, NOAA, DCIEM) and require recompression chamber availability for specified profiles and depths by jurisdiction [1][2][24][26].
- Recreational diving primarily uses NDLs managed by computers or tables, with service providers responsible for safe conduct and emergency preparedness per ISO 24803 [4].
- The scientific community has debated “deep stops”; a UHMS workshop found mixed evidence and advised caution in applying deep-stop strategies outside validated schedules [16].
- After scuba, minimum surface intervals before flying are recommended to reduce DCS risk; DAN guidance indicates conservative intervals depending on profile (e.g., 12–24 hours for recreational exposures) [12]. Scientific standards often adopt or extend these intervals and apply similar caution for land ascents to altitude [7].

## Operational procedures and safety systems

- Pre-dive planning: risk assessment, dive plan with objectives, depth/time limits, gas plans, emergency action plans, and communications arrangements are required across sectors; providers and supervisors have defined duties [4][5][7][23][26].
- Supervision and team size: occupational rules prescribe a designated supervisor at the dive location, minimum team sizes, and a standby diver ready to assist; offshore guidance specifies additional personnel for mixed-gas and bell operations [5][20][23][27].
- Communications: continuous voice communications are standard for surface-supplied diving; in contaminated water, full-face masks with two-way comms are required; recreational/scientific scuba commonly use hand signals with surface support comms where appropriate [21][26].
- Emergency resources: oxygen first aid and access to recompression treatment must be planned and documented; recent professional bulletins highlight hyperbaric safety topics and network support [26][32].

## Legal and regulatory frameworks by sector and jurisdiction

United States
- OSHA 29 CFR 1910 Subpart T regulates commercial diving operations, specifying scope, definitions, equipment, procedures, decompression, and medical requirements, with exemptions for instructional scuba within NDLs, public safety, human-subject research, and scientific diving programs meeting Appendix B criteria. An enforcement directive provides inspection guidance and clarifies supervisor (DPIC) roles [3][23].
- States may adopt Subpart T with clarifications; for example, Oregon OSHA sets explicit chamber proximity and operating limits for scuba and surface-supplied modes [24].
- Scientific diving programs operate under the Appendix B exemption with AAUS-based governance when criteria are met [7][28].

United Kingdom
- The Diving at Work Regulations 1997 apply to all diving at work, supported by ACOPs for offshore (L103), inland/inshore (L104), media (L106), and scientific/archaeological (L107) sectors, specifying planning, team sizes, ppO2 limits, chamber access, qualifications, and training [5][25][29].
- The HSE Approved List of Diving Qualifications sets recognized qualifications for divers and supervisors across sectors, including recognition pathways for military and overseas qualifications [30].

Canada
- The CSA Z275 series provides national standards for occupational diving safety (Z275.2) and competencies (Z275.4); provinces (e.g., British Columbia) incorporate these by reference and add regulatory requirements (e.g., use of DCIEM tables; restrictions on scuba for construction and contaminated environments) [6][26][31].

Offshore industry
- IMCA codes and DESIGN documents and ADCI consensus standards guide offshore and international contractors for surface-supplied mixed-gas, bell, and saturation operations, including manning, equipment, and emergency provisions [20][22][27].

Recreational sector
- ISO 24803 specifies service provider responsibilities; ISO 24801/24802 define diver and instructor competencies; snorkeling standards (ISO 13289 and ISO 13970) apply to excursions and guides [4][10][11][13][14].

## Boundaries and gray areas

Atmospheric diving systems (ADS)
- ADS and other one-atmosphere systems are used within “diving operations” but keep the occupant at surface pressure. NOAA treats ADS under “advanced platform support,” and CSA Z275.2 addresses “one-atmosphere systems” as a distinct category from ambient-pressure diving. Many regulators and industry codes treat ADS as related technology rather than a mode of ambient-pressure underwater diving by a diver [31][6].

“Technical diving” classification
- “Technical diving” is a widely used industry term for dives beyond standard recreational limits (e.g., planned decompression, overheads, advanced gases). It is not a formal regulatory category in occupational frameworks; authorities classify dives by whether they are undertaken “at work” and by operational mode (scuba, surface-supplied, saturation) and applicable risk controls. ISO standards now cover rebreather training, and several governments adopt ISO frameworks for recreational training, but occupational rules apply when diving is performed as work, regardless of “technical” labeling [3][5][6][17][18].

Mixing modes and activities
- Some environments (e.g., contaminated water, entrapment risks) prohibit or restrict specific modes (such as scuba) in occupational rules, driving selection of surface-supplied systems even for tasks otherwise similar to recreational or scientific diving [21][26].
- Public safety diving may be exempt from commercial standards during emergency response in the U.S., but planned non-emergency operations can trigger occupational requirements; agencies often adopt best practices beyond minimum legal obligations [23].

## See also: common hazards and medical support

- Hyperbaric oxygen therapy is indicated for DCS and arterial gas embolism among other conditions; UHMS publishes indications and standards of practice [15].
- Professional bulletins emphasize hyperbaric facility safety and emergency preparedness, including electrical safety in oxygen-enriched environments and chamber access networks [32].
