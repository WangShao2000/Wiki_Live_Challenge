# Edwardsville Amazon warehouse collapse

The Edwardsville Amazon warehouse collapse occurred on December 10, 2021, when an EF‑3 tornado struck Amazon’s delivery station DLI4 in Edwardsville, Madison County, Illinois, causing portions of the tilt‑up concrete building to fail and resulting in six fatalities and additional injuries. The National Weather Service (NWS) St. Louis rated the tornado EF‑3 with estimated peak winds of 150 mph, a path length of about 4.3 miles, and maximum width of approximately 300 yards; the facility experienced the worst damage along the track [1]. The event was part of a larger December 10–11, 2021 tornado outbreak across the Midwest and South. The NWS issued a tornado warning at 8:06 p.m. Central Standard Time (CST), providing roughly 25 minutes of lead time before impact [2]. Following investigations, the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) issued hazard alert recommendations to Amazon and several delivery service partners but did not issue citations, while multiple wrongful‑death and other civil suits were filed in Madison County Circuit Court and remained in litigation as of late 2025 [21][23][36][37][38][39]. In 2025, Illinois lawmakers enacted a warehouse tornado safety law addressing preparedness plans, sheltering, and inspection standards, citing the Edwardsville incident as a catalyst [31][32][33][34].

## Facility and location

The facility struck was Amazon’s delivery station designated DLI4, operated by Amazon and used for last‑mile sorting and dispatch of packages [26]. The site is located within the Gateway Commerce Center, a 2,300‑acre master‑planned logistics and industrial park in Edwardsville, Illinois, developed by TriStar Properties [7]. Street addresses associated with the complex include 3050 Gateway Commerce Center Drive South, Edwardsville, IL 62025, widely used in local directories, and 3077 Gateway Commerce Center Drive South, listed in OSHA’s inspection records; both refer to the same facility area within the park [4][5]. Coordinates published by a logistics directory place the facility near approximately 38.7724°N, −90.0407°W [6].

The building is a large warehouse constructed using tilt‑up concrete panels. A 593,940‑square‑foot speculative distribution center known as “Gateway East 594” was completed in 2018 at Gateway Commerce Center by Contegra Construction, designed for cross‑dock operations, with reported clear heights of around 36 feet and expandable floor area to roughly 1 million square feet [9]. The property at 3077 Gateway Commerce Center Drive was sold in July 2021 by a TriStar‑led joint venture to an institutional buyer; the site was noted as leased by Amazon [8]. Amazon subsequently completed tenant improvements totaling about 717,000 square feet at “Amazon STL4,” a project also at Edwardsville, encompassing offices, racking, maintenance spaces, and building systems [10]. News coverage and public reports vary on the total square footage of the facility involved in the collapse, with figures ranging from approximately 500,000 square feet to about 1.1 million square feet; such differences likely reflect distinct phases (core shell vs. tenant improvements vs. build‑out) and reporting variations [14][16][9][10][8].

Edwardsville’s building code framework at the time of construction followed the 2006 International Building Code (IBC), as subsequently verified by a structures specialist’s post‑incident review. The city later updated its adopted codes effective January 1, 2023 [12][11]. City inspection records reviewed by local media indicated the warehouse passed municipal inspections during construction and subsequent tenant occupancy, though the inspection reports were characterized as minimal in detail [24].

## Meteorology and timeline

The tornado that struck the Amazon facility formed during a significant severe weather outbreak across the Midwest and South on the evening of December 10, 2021. NOAA characterized the event as an uncommon but high‑impact December tornado outbreak across eight states, with dozens of confirmed tornadoes and substantial loss of life [3]. The NWS St. Louis office determined the Edwardsville tornado occurred between approximately 8:27 and 8:32 p.m. CST, with peak winds near 150 mph. The tornado intensified after crossing Interstate 255 and struck the Amazon facility, where west‑facing walls collapsed and a large segment of the roof was lost; the tornado continued northeast causing additional damage before lifting [1].

The NWS Joint Regional Service Assessment documented warning services for the outbreak. For Madison County, a tornado warning was issued at 8:06 p.m. CST, providing roughly 25 minutes of lead time before the warehouse was struck. Average warning lead times across affected offices during the outbreak exceeded 19 minutes [2].

## Collapse and on‑site conditions

NWS damage surveys described the worst damage along the Edwardsville track at the Amazon facility, where west‑facing walls collapsed inward, additional structural failures occurred, and a substantial roof section was removed. Debris from the warehouse was carried tens of miles downstream along the tornado’s path [1]. News reporting noted that approximately 11‑inch‑thick tilt‑up concrete wall panels failed and the roof was ripped off [14].

A structures specialist deployed to the scene documented observations about steel column base connections and wall failure patterns. The specialist reported numerous steel columns appeared to have been “lifted out of the floor,” with no visible welds or bolts at column bases, and noted IBC requirements for anchorage of structural members against uplift from wind loads. Observed wall failures included west walls collapsing inward and some east walls failing outward, with vehicles crushed in the collapse zones. The same narrative recorded that Edwardsville followed the 2006 IBC at the time and provided documentation to OSHA investigators [12].

Contegra Construction, the builder, publicly defended its work, explaining that the structural design used metal sleeves anchored to the foundation, with steel support columns placed into and perimeter‑welded to those sleeves; caulk at the floor line served as finish work over welds. The contractor stated that welds and bases were sheared by extreme tornado forces and emphasized that the building met local code requirements, which specify design for straight‑line winds substantially below the tornado’s wind speeds [25].

On‑site emergency actions and conditions were also documented. OSHA’s investigation noted that electronic warning systems delivered tornado alerts to the site between 8:06 and 8:16 p.m., and managers directed employees toward the designated shelter‑in‑place location in the northern portion of the building. Approximately ten workers sheltered instead in a restroom on the south side near loading docks; six of those individuals were killed when the collapse occurred around 8:27 p.m. [22]. Early response reports described hazards complicating search and rescue, including downed power lines, unstable concrete, and water from the fire suppression system [13].

## Casualties and injuries

Six people died at the facility. Their identities were released by local authorities and reported by national and local outlets: Deandre S. Morrow (28); Kevin D. Dickey (62); Clayton Lynn Cope (29); Etheria S. Hebb (34); Larry E. Virden (46); and Austin J. McEwen (26). The victims resided in Missouri and Illinois communities including St. Louis, Carlyle, Alton, Collinsville, and Edwardsville [15][16][17][18]. OSHA identified that five of the deceased and an injured worker were delivery drivers employed by independent delivery service providers (DSPs) sheltering in a bathroom on the south side, and one deceased individual was an in‑house contractor employed by CBRE [21].

## Emergency response and recovery

Regional mutual aid was extensive. A structures specialist narrative documented multi‑agency deployment, with hundreds of responders and vehicles assisting over the first 24 hours, and noted that operations transitioned from rescue to recovery in the early morning hours of December 11 [12]. Early public briefings by local officials confirmed fatalities and described ongoing search operations and hazards at the scene [13].

Federal assistance followed. On December 13, 2021, the President approved an emergency declaration for Illinois, including Madison County, authorizing federal aid for emergency protective measures at 75 percent federal funding [19]. State agriculture and local news coverage summarized the declaration and state commitments to support recovery in communities affected by the outbreak [20].

## Investigations, findings, and regulatory reviews

OSHA opened an investigation into the collapse shortly after the event, maintaining a presence at the site and conducting multiple related inspections. OSHA’s case records include a fatality/catastrophe inspection, accident reports, and inspections of Amazon and DSP entities associated with the facility [5][22]. On April 26, 2022, OSHA issued a news release stating that severe weather emergency procedures at Amazon’s Edwardsville site met minimal federal guidelines but called on Amazon to improve planning and training to better protect workers and contract drivers in future emergencies. OSHA sent hazard alert letters to specific DSPs, addressing training, drills, clarity of shelter locations, and identification and accessibility of warning devices; OSHA did not issue citations in connection with the collapse [21][23]. Public reporting on the OSHA outcome emphasized the absence of citations while noting OSHA’s recommendations to Amazon and partners [23].

Local and building code considerations were also reviewed. Media obtained city inspection records indicating the warehouse had passed municipal inspections, though documentation was limited in detail [24]. Edifice code adoption is shown on the city’s website, which updated its codes effective January 1, 2023 [11]. Engineering analyses and professional discourse following the event focused on performance of large‑box, tilt‑up warehouses in tornadoes, noting vulnerabilities when roofs are removed and panels lose support. NIST has described emerging tornado design provisions in building codes and the importance of storm shelters in tornado‑prone regions; ASCE publications have analyzed fragility of large tilt‑up buildings under tornado loads [40][41].

## Lawsuits and legal outcomes

Multiple civil suits were filed in Madison County Circuit Court related to the Edwardsville incident. The parents of Austin McEwen filed a wrongful‑death suit against Amazon, Contegra Construction, TriStar Properties, and others, alleging deficiencies in emergency procedures and sheltering and seeking damages under Illinois law [36][37]. Additional suits included a wrongful‑death action on behalf of Deandre Morrow’s family and a negligent infliction of emotional distress claim by several delivery drivers who survived the collapse [38][39]. Allegations commonly include assertions regarding failure to provide adequate tornado sheltering and to cease operations or evacuate with sufficient warning. As of late 2025, public reporting indicated these cases remained in litigation without publicly reported final judgments; procedural histories include filings, motions, and amendments typical of complex wrongful‑death and negligence claims [38][39].

## Amazon’s response and policy changes

Amazon executives expressed condolences and support for affected families and the Edwardsville community in statements following the incident, and the company stated it was working with authorities at the site [14]. In a January 3, 2022 letter to lawmakers, Amazon described the facility’s emergency actions: upon receiving the tornado warning at 8:06 p.m., site leaders implemented the emergency action plan and instructed employees and DSP drivers to shelter in place; Amazon noted that FEMA‑rated safe rooms were not required by local codes for this type of building [26]. The company has stated that employees and partners were permitted to keep their mobile phones during shifts, countering speculation about a phone ban; in mid‑2022 Amazon made the temporary allowance permanent across its operations [27][28].

Amazon has described changes to emergency preparedness after the Edwardsville event, including increased manager training, more frequent severe weather drills, distribution of emergency response cards with facility maps and instructions, and hiring an in‑house meteorologist to enhance severe weather monitoring and operational decision‑making [29]. In 2023 reporting, Amazon reiterated that it designs and builds to applicable codes and does not plan to add storm shelters where not required by law, while continuing to expand severe weather monitoring and communication [31].

## Public and policy impact

Public officials and community leaders responded with statements emphasizing worker safety and support for those affected. Illinois Governor J.B. Pritzker publicly underscored expectations of workplace safety and pledged resources [14]. Memorials and vigils were held in Edwardsville for the six workers who died [35].

Policy discussions in Illinois led to formal review and legislative action. A state Warehouse Safety Standards Task Force convened to study improvements in warehouse safety, later recommending, among other measures, tornado shelters and strengthened emergency planning [30]. In June 2025, Illinois lawmakers passed a warehouse safety bill; Governor Pritzker signed the measure in August 2025. The law requires warehouses to implement tornado safety plans, mandates specified shelter/refuge areas for new facilities, sets timelines for compliance at existing sites, and creates inspection and certification standards, including International Code Council certification requirements for municipal inspectors on a future effective date. Legislative sponsors cited the Edwardsville event in supporting the bill [31][32][33][34].

## Media coverage and discourse

The collapse and its aftermath received extensive national and local coverage. Major outlets reported the EF‑3 tornado’s impact on the warehouse, the damage pattern at the site, the fatalities and identities of victims, and real‑time search and rescue operations [13][14][15][16][17][18]. Subsequent reporting covered OSHA’s investigation and recommendations, the absence of citations, and public debate regarding building design standards, the adequacy of severe weather procedures and sheltering, and mobile phone policies [21][23][24][27][28]. Local and trade outlets tracked the progress of civil litigation, community memorials, and policy developments through 2025 [35][38][39][31][32][33][34].
