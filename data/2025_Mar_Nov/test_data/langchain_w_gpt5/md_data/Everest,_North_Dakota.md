# Source guide to research and write a complete, neutral article on Everest, North Dakota (as of December 11, 2025)

## Overview

This report identifies reliable, non-Wikipedia sources and a verification workflow to research Everest, North Dakota comprehensively, in line with neutral encyclopedic standards and the constraint to avoid en.wikipedia.org. It maps each required aspect (existence/classification, identifiers, history, geography/climate, demographics, infrastructure, education, culture/landmarks, notable people, present-day status) to authoritative data sources, explains how to use them, and highlights due-weight and verifiability considerations. Every statement below is supported by an inline citation and the “Sources” list provides full URLs.

## Existence and classification

- Use the U.S. Geological Survey’s GNIS “Domestic Names” search to confirm that “Everest” exists in North Dakota and to determine its official feature class (e.g., Populated Place, Locale, Historical), associated county, variant names, coordinates, elevation, and GNIS feature ID. GNIS is the federal authoritative gazetteer, and its records carry due weight for identifying settlements and historical localities [1][2].
- GNIS variant names and classification notes help determine whether Everest is an inhabited unincorporated community, a former settlement/ghost town, a railroad siding, or a historical locale. Where GNIS indicates “historical” or “locale,” treat status accordingly and corroborate with other sources (e.g., newspapers, historic maps, aerial imagery) to avoid original research [1][2][10][16].

## Location and identifiers

- Coordinates, elevation, county, and the GNIS feature ID should be taken from the GNIS record. GNIS is the definitive federal source for these identifiers and geographic attributes [1][2].
- Township identification can be derived by overlaying GNIS coordinates on North Dakota’s PLSS township dataset in the ND GIS Hub; the interactive mapping tools and downloadable layers allow precise township/section verification without speculation [20][21].
- ZIP code(s) are assigned to postal delivery areas rather than to unincorporated places. Use the USPS City/State or Address lookup to determine the servicing post office and ZIP code for locations proximate to Everest’s coordinates; avoid unsourced assignment of a “community ZIP” if USPS does not designate one for Everest itself [5].
- Area code information is managed by the North American Numbering Plan Administrator; verify coverage (North Dakota is served by NANP) using NANPA’s official site and mapping resources [6].
- If FIPS codes are applicable (e.g., county ANSI FIPS code), source them from the U.S. Census Bureau’s ANSI code lists. If Everest is not an incorporated place or census-designated place (CDP), it will not have a unique place FIPS code, and this should be stated explicitly [3][4][23].

## Founding and naming

- Consult Douglas A. Wick’s “North Dakota Place Names” for the etymology, who/what “Everest” was named after, and founding/platted context. Wick’s volume is widely cited in North Dakota local histories and is suitable for neutral attribution; cite the book and page/entry where available (Google Books page provides bibliographic details) [29].
- The State Historical Society of North Dakota (SHSND) archives contain county histories, local manuscripts, and vertical files that often document naming and founding details, including railroad townsite promotion, homesteading, and early development [8].
- Historical newspapers via Chronicling America and SHSND’s newspaper resources can corroborate founding announcements, townsite plats, early commerce, and naming attributions. Use precise dates and avoid relative time phrasing [9][10].

## Historical development

- Post office establishment and discontinuation dates are best sourced from Jim Forte Postal History, which compiles official postal records and is widely used in postal historiography. Cite specific dates for the “Everest” post office and any name changes or relocations [7].
- Railroad station/siding history should be verified through:
  - North Dakota State Rail Plan for line ownership and active rail corridors; it provides authoritative background on rail infrastructure and historical context for lines across the state [12].
  - Current network maps (e.g., BNSF) to confirm whether any nearby line remains active and the broader rail context in the area [15].
  - Railroad historical societies (e.g., GNRHS, NPRHA) and scanned timetables or line histories for station lists and naming provenance; use due weight and attribute to the specific railroad publication when available [13][14].
- Historic topographic maps (USGS TopoView) can show “Everest” as a labeled settlement, siding, or locale across decades, providing evidence for status changes, road/rail alignments, and nearby features. Cite the specific quadrangle and year(s) used [32].
- If jurisdiction changes (e.g., township boundary adjustments) appear, verify through county plat maps and ND GIS Hub layers to avoid conjecture [11][20][21].

## Governance and administration

- For current oversight: identify the county from GNIS and the township via ND GIS Hub. Unincorporated communities in North Dakota are administered at the county level and, where applicable, within civil townships; they do not have municipal governments unless incorporated, and this distinction should be stated plainly [1][20][21].
- Representation and public services (e.g., sheriff, county road maintenance) are under county authority; cite the county’s official website for general governance context where relevant, without overemphasis or local detail beyond verifiable scope. County sources are appropriate for neutral, factual descriptions [11].

Use USGS topo maps and the National Map Viewer to describe topography and nearby natural/man-made features with verifiable references (e.g., elevation contours, watercourses, railroad lines, roadways). - Climate normals (temperature, precipitation) can be presented using:
  - NOAA/NCEI station-based 1991–2020 normals from the nearest weather station with adequate exposure records; describe selection criteria and cite station identification [17].
  - PRISM 30-year normals for the grid cell encompassing Everest’s coordinates; PRISM provides high-resolution climatologies suitable for rural localities without nearby stations [18].
- If including a Köppen climate classification, use a peer‑reviewed global classification (e.g., Beck et al. 2018) and explain the basis for the classification in the locality’s region, avoiding original synthesis beyond what the source permits [19].

## Present-day status and usage

- Determine current status (inhabited, abandoned, historical site) by triangulating GNIS classification, recent aerial imagery via the National Map Viewer, county GIS/plat maps, and recent local news references. Avoid original synthesis; present what reliable sources show about ongoing habitation or repurposing (e.g., farms at the site of a former town) [1][10][11][16].

## Recommended research workflow

1. Confirm existence, classification, coordinates, elevation, county, and GNIS feature ID via GNIS Domestic Names search; record all variant names [1][2].
2. Use coordinates to determine the civil township and PLSS section with ND GIS Hub data; note township name formally [20][21].
3. Verify postal history (establishment/closure and name changes) in Jim Forte Postal History; note precise dates [7].
4. Investigate naming origin and early development in Wick’s “North Dakota Place Names,” then corroborate via SHSND archives and historic newspapers [8][9][10][29].
5. Establish transportation context: ND DOT county map for roads; State Rail Plan and railroad sources for rail lines and station history; National Map Viewer and TopoView for spatial verification [11][12][15][16][32].
6. Check Census geographies in TIGERweb and data.census.gov; if no place/CDP exists, state the lack of separate demographic data [3][4].
7. Compile climate normals using NCEI station normals and/or PRISM grid normals; optionally add Köppen classification with a peer‑reviewed global reference [17][18][19].
8. Identify school district boundaries via ND GIS Hub and ND DPI; check for historic consolidation via newspapers/archives [24][25][8][9][10].
9. Search NRHP and SHSND historic sites for landmarks; cite any registered properties or documented local sites [26][27][8].
10. Review newspapers and archives for notable people and present-day status; corroborate with aerial imagery and county pages [10][11][16].

## Citation and style guidance

- Inline citations: Provide bracketed citations [n] for every factual claim, including identifiers, dates, and measurements; map these to the “Sources” list with full URLs.
- Neutrality and due weight: Attribute opinions or contested assertions to specific sources; present majority and minority views proportionately [9][10].
- Precision: Prefer exact dates and figures; avoid vague or relative time references; specify sources for each datum [7][9][10][17][18].
- Avoid prohibited sources: Do not consult or cite en.wikipedia.org; avoid user-generated content unless corroborated by authoritative sources [1][2][9][10].
