# Operation Forager logistics

Operation Forager logistics were the plans, organizations, and systems that moved, supplied, and sustained U.S. Fifth Fleet and expeditionary forces for the Marianas campaign of June–August 1944. The operation’s strategic significance was to seize Saipan, Tinian, and Guam to sever Japanese sea lines, neutralize the central Pacific bastions, and establish bases for the U.S. Army Air Forces’ B-29 strategic bombardment of Japan. Forager required long over‑the‑sea movements from Hawaii and the Marshalls; a very large amphibious lift; rapid ship‑to‑shore supply under fire; a mobile “fleet train” able to fuel, rearm, and provision at sea; and immediate engineering to restore and expand airfields and build major forward bases. Logistics organizations included Admiral Chester W. Nimitz’s Central Pacific command and staff, Admiral Raymond A. Spruance’s Fifth Fleet, Vice Admiral Richmond K. Turner’s Joint Expeditionary Force (TF 51) and amphibious task forces, and the Service Force Pacific (notably Service Squadron Ten) that enabled at‑sea replenishment and forward repair. Key constraints were shipping allocation and loading methods, beach and reef hydrography, ammunition and water supply, weather, and Japanese interdiction and defense. Lessons learned—palletization and commodity loading, expanded underway replenishment, forward service squadrons, and robust shore party engineering—shaped subsequent operations at Iwo Jima and Okinawa. [1][2][3][11][12]

## Strategic and operational logistics planning and command arrangements

U.S. planning integrated the Central Pacific Area’s naval command with expeditionary ground and air components. Admiral Nimitz (CINCPOA) directed Operation Forager to seize the southern Marianas by mid‑June 1944 under a Joint Chiefs of Staff directive; Admiral Spruance executed the operation with the Fifth Fleet and subordinate task forces, notably the Joint Expeditionary Force (TF 51) under Vice Admiral Turner, Northern and Southern Attack Forces for Saipan/Tinian (TF 52) and Guam (TF 53), and the fast carrier force (TF 58). Expeditionary Troops (TF 56) under Lieutenant General Holland M. Smith integrated Marine and Army divisions and corps troops for amphibious assault and seizure. Service Force Pacific, commanded by Vice Admiral William L. Calhoun, sustained the fleet and landing forces through mobile service squadrons—especially Service Squadron Ten (ServRon 10)—and Service Squadron Eight, which organized at‑sea fueling groups. These commands coordinated staging, replenishment, repair, hospital evacuation, and follow‑on development of island bases. [1][2][3][6][7][11][12]

The Fifth Fleet’s logistics concept relied on:
- Forward anchorages in the Marshalls for staging and servicing (Majuro, Kwajalein, Eniwetok) and the Admiralties (Seeadler Harbor) to echelon stores and repair facilities, with ComServPac assigning oilers, ammunition, reefer and general stores ships, tenders, and floating drydocks as a mobile base. [1][2]
- A formal underway replenishment organization—Task Group 50.17—of multiple fueling task units, coordinated replenishment areas, and a cycle to keep the fast carrier and escort groups supplied while distant from fixed bases. [1]
- Detailed amphibious loading and shore party plans to land 32 days of rations, 20 days of fuel and general supplies, 30 days of medical stores, and initial ground and antiaircraft ammunition (seven and ten days, respectively) by commodity streams to division and corps dumps. [3]

## Staging bases and lines of communication

The operational lines of communications ran from Pearl Harbor and the U.S. West Coast to the Marshalls and forward to the Marianas:
- In the Marshalls, Majuro, Kwajalein, and especially Eniwetok were developed as major fleet anchorages and logistics bases after the February 1944 Eastern Mandates campaign, providing the springboard for Forager. [9]
- Fifth Fleet and assault forces staged through these atolls on a carefully timed schedule; fast carriers cycled to Eniwetok for rearming and replenishment, and oilers shuttled fuel from rear areas to rendezvous “rectangles” for at‑sea refueling. [1][2]
- Sea lines to the Marianas were protected by the fleet and by submarines forward; the transports and cargo shipping followed staged movements to arrival off Saipan on 15 June (Dog Day), Tinian on 24 July, and Guam on 21 July (William Day). [3][5][10]

## Amphibious lift and assault logistics

Moving and landing the assault echelons demanded a large, mixed fleet of specialized amphibious ships and craft:
- For Saipan, U.S. sources report 59 troopships and 64 LSTs in the assault lift, moving divisions over distances of 2,400–3,500 miles and putting roughly 20,000 U.S. troops ashore on D‑Day despite heavy opposition. Attack transports (APA), attack cargo ships (AKA), LSTs, LCI gunboats, and dock landing ships (LSD) carried troops, vehicles, tanks, artillery, and supplies; rocket‑armed LCIs and LVT(A) armored amphibians spearheaded landings, followed by troops in LVTs and LCVPs/LCMs shuttling over reefs. [10][3]
- Six amphibian tractor battalions supported Saipan: Marine 2d, 4th, and 10th, and Army 534th, 715th, and 773d. Reef conditions forced reliance on LVTs for initial waves; later waves transferred from LCVPs to returning LVTs at the reef line. [3]
- Loading methods emphasized combat loading to land early priority equipment and supplies by beach and by echelon. A palletization directive (25–50 percent of initial supplies) was unevenly implemented among divisions; subsequent assessments favored commodity (selective) loading of follow‑on ships to improve discharge flexibility. [3][4]
- On Tinian, the assault employed a shore‑to‑shore movement from Saipan with pontoon causeways to speed unloading across narrow northern beaches; two pontoon piers were towed from Saipan, with one operational on 25 July, and LSDs and LSTs shuttled tanks and heavy equipment. [5]
- Guam’s fringing reefs and channels made causeways and channel dredging high priorities. TF 53’s plans specified early seizure of Apra Harbor, with detailed estimates for LST/LCT capacities on the Luminao Reef breakwater, oil dock causeway, and inner channels to enable rapid development of an unloading port during the assault phase. [6][7]

Initial attrition among amphibian vehicles was heavy. On Saipan, official Marine Corps studies recorded losses of 65 armored LVTs and 139 cargo LVTs, reflecting both enemy fire and hydrographic hazards, but overall assault logistics held as shore parties established beach dumps and evacuation points under fire. [4]

## Naval logistics at sea: the fleet train and underway replenishment

Forager’s tempo depended on the fleet train—oilers, ammunition, and stores ships—and standardized at‑sea replenishment:
- Vice Admiral Calhoun’s Service Force Pacific arranged to deliver roughly 1,400,000 barrels of fuel oil every two weeks beginning 1 June 1944 to support the operation. [1]
- Task Group 50.17 organized underway fueling in eleven large rectangles, each 75 by 25 miles and named for oil companies, with eight fueling task units typically composed of three oilers and escorts cycling between Eniwetok and the operating areas. [1]
- Japanese air attacks first targeted Central Pacific oilers on 18 June 1944; Saranac, Neshanic, and Saugatuck were damaged, but repairs and replacements kept the replenishment cycle intact. [1]
- Ammunition logistics combined rear‑area stowage at Eniwetok with at‑objective rearming. After unexpectedly high expenditures in naval gunfire and ashore, ammunition ships were sent forward. Figures recorded for the period include Rainier handling 9,410 tons; Mazama issuing 3,448 tons off Saipan in 15 days (with a daily peak near 400 tons); and total transfers at the objective of about 17,152 tons (14,629 tons of shells/rockets and 2,523 tons of bombs/.50‑caliber ammunition), of which 10,960 tons were fired at Saipan. [2]
- Refrigerated (“reefer”) ships and general stores ships maintained provisioning; pre‑assault standards called for 120 days’ dry and medical stores on ships, while fresh provisions were cycled by reefer shuttle and floating cold storage. [1][2]

ServRon 10, established at Eniwetok for Forager, concentrated tenders, repair ships, floating drydocks, and workshops to repair battle damage and keep destroyers and auxiliaries on station. In early July, an average 488 ships and craft were present (1–15 July), with more than 520 at peak; even amid weather damage to boats and barges, the squadron maintained repair, ammunitioning, and provisioning services. [2]

## Port and beach operations, salvage, and medical evacuation

Ship‑to‑shore operations were organized by naval beach parties and shore parties (Marine pioneer battalions and Seabees, or Army engineer shore groups). Their tasks included beach traffic control, beach dump establishment, casualty evacuation, and rapid installation of pontoon causeways:
- On Saipan, beach operations were complicated by reef lines, surf, and fire, and by the temporary withdrawal of transports during the Battle of the Philippine Sea. Shore parties adapted with pallet handling where possible and by shifting dump sites when initial picks proved unsuitable. [3][4]
- On Tinian, causeways and pontoon piers reduced lighterage bottlenecks; LVTs and DUKWs moved priority supplies while causeways enabled truck operations as soon as they were secured. [5]
- On Guam, TF 53 emphasized early port control and harbor operations: minesweeping, pilotage, seaplane servicing, unloading supervision, a central boat pool, and directing salvage under a designated harbor authority once Apra Harbor was seized. One LST was to serve temporarily as a fleet post office and as a tender/fuel source for small craft to stabilize boat operations in the initial days. [6][7]

Salvage and repair assets supported continuity of operations. Service Squadron Ten and attached salvage craft refloated the grounded SS Sea Flyer at Eniwetok in July after offloading about 1,900 tons, and maintained ARD/AFD drydocks and repair ships to turn around damaged units rapidly. [2]

Medical evacuation was planned from the beaches to LSTs outfitted as receiving ships, then to transports and hospital ships, and by air once fields were available. Hospital ships Solace, Bountiful, Relief, and Samaritan evacuated large numbers of casualties from Saipan. On D‑Day, three LSTs equipped as improvised hospital ships handled about 1,500 casualties; by 18 June Solace and Bountiful had embarked over 1,000 patients, and air evacuation from Isely Field began within days, eventually flying hundreds to Marshalls hospitals. [2][3]

## Engineering and construction logistics

Engineering tasks began during combat: clearing beaches and roads; restoring captured airfields; and rapidly constructing major bases to sustain continuing operations and strategic air campaigns.

- Saipan (Aslito/Isely Field): Seabees landed on D‑Day, restored Aslito Airfield within days, and widened and strengthened it; it was renamed Isely Field. Army and Navy engineers then built heavy bomber facilities—two long parallel B‑29 runways with coral base and paved surfaces, dispersal areas, fuel storage, shops, and utilities—using blasting and coral fill. The first B‑29 of the 73d Bombardment Wing arrived in October; the second runway opened in mid‑December, and the base matured through spring 1945. [2][11][12]
- Tinian: The 6th Naval Construction Brigade, with Naval Construction Regiments, built North Field and West Field into the world’s largest B‑29 base complex, with extended runways, hardstands, taxiways, fuel systems, depots, and housing, supplied via pontoon piers and shore facilities installed immediately after the landings. [12][5]
- Guam: Seabees and Army engineers rebuilt and expanded airfields and constructed a vast advanced naval base with port facilities at Apra Harbor, warehouses, roads, water and power systems, hospitals, and magazines to support both the fleet and the Twentieth Air Force’s later bomber groups. [12]

Higher‑level arrangements for strategic air operations assigned responsibilities between CINCPOA and the Twentieth Air Force: Nimitz’s command provided base construction, shipping and local supply, communications, and protection; Twentieth Air Force retained operational control of B‑29s and their specialized maintenance and supply trains. Air base designs specified very long runways (about 8,500 feet by 200 feet) and large hardstand capacity, affecting engineer, material, and shipping requirements and priorities. [11]

## Japanese logistics in the Marianas and Allied interdiction

Before the invasion, Japanese infrastructure in the Marianas included airfields, seaplane bases, and limited port facilities. However, Allied blockade and interdiction increasingly severed supply lines:
- U.S. submarines executed reconnaissance and interdiction patrols in the approaches to the Marianas in May–June 1944, sinking multiple transports and disrupting planned troop movements. A U.S. Naval Institute account cites that roughly half a Japanese division’s reinforcement of Saipan failed due to submarine attacks, with survivors arriving short of weapons and equipment. Submarines also struck the Japanese carrier force during the Battle of the Philippine Sea, sinking the fleet carriers Taihō and Shōkaku, undermining Japanese ability to defend and resupply the Marianas by sea. [13]
- The United States Strategic Bombing Survey’s naval analysis emphasized Japan’s failure to secure its sea lanes to the Southern Resources Area and the cumulative impact of shipping losses, which by mid‑1944 severely limited Japanese capacity to reinforce or provision isolated island garrisons, including those in the Marianas. [14]

During the campaign, Japanese attempts to resupply garrisons by barges or small craft were constrained by U.S. naval and air interdiction. The destruction of Japanese carrier air power in June 1944 ended any practical possibility of large‑scale Japanese maritime reinforcement into the Central Pacific. [10][13][14]

## Constraints, bottlenecks, shortages, and solutions

- Shipping and loading: Competing demands for shipping and the need to load by tactical priority caused tension between “block loading” by units and commodity loading for selective discharge. After‑action reports for Saipan recommended commodity loading for resupply echelons to speed targeted unloading. [3][4]
- Hydrography and beaches: Reefs and surf at Saipan and especially Guam limited direct beaching. Solutions included extensive use of LVTs in assault waves, rapid installation of pontoon causeways and piers, dredging of channels, and early seizure and development of Apra Harbor as a controlled port. [3][5][6][7]
- Ammunition consumption: Extended resistance ashore and heavy naval gunfire led to unexpectedly high ammunition expenditure. Ammunition ships were moved forward to the objective to maintain rates of fire, and later planning increased units‑of‑fire carried for assaults. [2][4]
- Fuel and at‑sea risks: Underway replenishment was critical but exposed. The 18 June attacks on oilers demonstrated vulnerabilities; increased screening and repair capacity and continued cycling from Eniwetok maintained steady sustainment. [1]
- Water supply: Fresh water was a recurring shortage in forward areas; TF 51’s war diary emphasized the need for a large fresh‑water reserve (on the order of one million gallons) in the first garrison echelon and close follow‑on by water ships until shore water points and distillation plants were operational. [8][2]
- Weather: Swell and storms damaged small craft and disrupted unloading in open anchorages. After a prolonged southwest blow at Eniwetok, ServRon 10 highlighted the need for better shelter and securing of boat pools and recommended “mother ships” (LST/LSD) to house and maintain small craft in exposed anchorages. [2]
- Air support coordination: Early reliance on fleet carriers for close support strained availability. Post‑Saipan recommendations led to dedicating escort carriers (CVEs), including those with Marine pilots, for direct support of landing forces. [4]

## Quantitative data and their operational effects

- Scale of forces: One authoritative naval logistics history summarizes the assault force for Forager as about 634 vessels, including 14 battleships, 25 carriers and escort carriers, 26 cruisers, and 144 destroyers, with roughly 2,000 aircraft and several hundred thousand Navy, Marine, and Army personnel. The fleet train and service squadrons raised the total count of ships and craft far higher at staging points. [1][2]
- Saipan shipping: NHHC records for Saipan cite 59 troopships and 64 LSTs in the assault lift. [10]
- Underway fueling: ComServPac planned approximately 1,400,000 barrels of fuel oil delivered every two weeks starting 1 June 1944; underway fueling took place in 11 prescribed rectangles, each 75 by 25 miles. [1]
- Ammunition: At‑objective transfers during Saipan operations totaled over 17,000 tons of ammunition and ordnance stores; Saipan alone consumed nearly 11,000 tons of naval and ground ammunition. [2]
- At‑objective smoke defense: In the Saipan transport areas, smoke screens were used a dozen times (about 18.5 hours total), consuming on the order of 57,000 gallons of fog oil and about 11,400 smoke pots and floats, which senior commanders judged a major factor in mitigating air attack. [2]
- Medical evacuation: On 15 June, three LSTs adapted as casualty ships received roughly 1,500 casualties; by 18 June the hospital ships Solace and Bountiful had embarked over 1,000 patients; air evacuation from Isely Field subsequently moved an additional several hundred to rear hospitals. [2]
- Amphibian vehicle losses: On Saipan, 65 armored LVTs and 139 cargo LVTs were lost during the campaign, reflecting the sustained intensity of ship‑to‑shore operations. [4]
- Airfield construction: B‑29 bases required ~8,500‑by‑200‑foot runways and extensive hardstand capacity; engineers delivered one Saipan runway by October and a second by mid‑December 1944, with Tinian’s two fields and Guam’s bases following into 1945. [11][12]

These measures sustained continuous operations ashore and afloat and enabled the rapid transition to strategic bombing of Japan from the Marianas beginning in late 1944. [11][12]

## Assessments, critiques, and lessons learned

Official Marine Corps histories and naval logistics accounts emphasize:
- The criticality of commodity loading and palletization for selective discharge under fire, particularly after early difficulties with “block loading” of resupply ships. [3][4]
- The need to increase initial units‑of‑fire for artillery and mortars; post‑Saipan recommendations called seven units generally inadequate, with future operations carrying more. [4]
- The value of dedicated CVE groups for close air support to reduce contention with fast carrier tasking. [4]
- The decisive effect of mobile service squadrons and fleet train procedures, including standardized replenishment areas, to keep fast carrier and amphibious forces supplied far from fixed bases. [1][2]
- The importance of early harbor seizure and organization (e.g., Apra Harbor), robust salvage, and forward repair capacity to reduce port‑throughput bottlenecks and return damaged units to service quickly. [6][2]
- The necessity of planned fresh‑water reserves and immediate follow‑on of water carriers, and of “mother ship” support for small craft in exposed anchorages, based on weather disruptions at Eniwetok and the Marianas. [2][8]

## Immediate logistical aftermath and influence on later operations

Following the capture of the Marianas, Service Squadron Ten continued to expand the forward mobile base concept, soon shifting its principal anchorage to Ulithi to support Philippines, Iwo Jima, and Okinawa operations. Forager’s methods—commodity loading, palletization, causeway employment, designated harbor authorities, standardized underway replenishment, and integrated service squadrons—became standing practice. The Seabees’ rapid construction of major fleet anchorages and air bases in the Marianas enabled sustained B‑29 operations against Japan from late 1944 and provided the logistics platform for subsequent amphibious assaults. The command arrangements for base construction and strategic air employment—dividing responsibilities between CINCPOA for logistics and the Twentieth Air Force for operations—also informed later joint service integration in the Central Pacific. [1][2][11][12]
