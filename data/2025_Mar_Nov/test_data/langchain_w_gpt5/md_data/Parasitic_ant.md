# Parasitic ant

Parasitic ants are ants that exploit the colonies of other ants through specialized life histories, most prominently social parasitism—where a parasite ant species depends on the social structure and labor of a host species for reproduction or colony maintenance. Major forms include temporary social parasitism (queens usurp host colonies during founding), dulosis or slave-making (parasites raid other colonies to steal brood that develop as a workforce), inquilinism (permanent, workerless parasites living inside host nests), and xenobiosis or “guest ants” (species cohabit and exploit host resources without fully replacing the workforce). Some authors also include kleptoparasitism (brood robbery without long-term integration) as related exploitative behavior. These systems have evolved repeatedly in multiple ant lineages and are notable for extreme behavioral and morphological adaptations, tight coevolution with hosts, and ecological and conservation implications in the communities where they occur [1], [22], [68].

## Terminology and classification

Social parasitism in ants comprises several distinct life history strategies, each defined by how parasites obtain and maintain access to host resources:

- Temporary social parasitism: Newly mated parasite queens infiltrate host colonies, typically kill or displace the host queen, and rely on resident host workers to rear the parasite’s first brood. As host workers die, the colony transitions to being composed of the parasite species’ workers. Examples occur across several genera, including Lasius and Formica, with some taxa exhibiting facultative temporary parasitism (can also found independently) [1], [3], [22].

- Dulosis (slave-making): Parasite colonies conduct raids on host colonies to steal larvae and pupae, which emerge and work in the parasite nest. Slave-making can be obligate (parasites cannot function without slaves) or facultative. Genera include Polyergus (Formicinae), Formica (sanguinea group), Harpagoxenus and Protomognathus (Myrmicinae), and Rossomyrmex (Formicinae) [1], [22], [52].

- Inquilinism (workerless social parasitism): Permanently parasitic species that typically lack a worker caste and invest primarily in reproductive individuals; they live inside host nests and often tolerate host queens. Exemplar taxa include Anergates atratulus (parasite of Tetramorium in the Palearctic), species in the Tetramorium inquilinum group, and attine inquilines such as Acromyrmex insinuator [1], [34], [38], [57].

- Xenobiosis (“guest ants”): Parasites cohabit with a host, often in a spatially segregated way, exploiting resources and sometimes host workers but maintaining distinct nest spaces. The genus Formicoxenus includes Holarctic guest ants living with wood ants (Formica) [1], [3].

- Kleptoparasitism and brood robbing: Ants or other arthropods that raid or rob brood or food from host colonies without establishing long-term mixed nests. Some ants, such as Solenopsis fugax, use chemical repellents to temporarily disperse nurses and steal brood; although related, these are typically distinguished from social parasitism sensu stricto because they do not rely on host workers to rear their brood long-term [23].

The umbrella term “parasitic ant” is most consistently applied to social parasites sensu stricto, but usage varies. Xenobiosis is sometimes treated as commensalism rather than parasitism because guest ants may not fully replace host labor; however, many sources include xenobiosis within social parasitism due to exploitation of host social organization [1]. Reviews emphasize distinguishing intraspecific social parasitism (e.g., small queens or “microgynes” exploiting conspecific colonies) from obligate interspecific parasitism, as their evolutionary pathways and ecological effects can differ [1].

Across ant subfamilies, social parasites are concentrated in Formicinae and Myrmicinae but also occur in Dolichoderinae, Ectatomminae, and Attini (fungus-growing ants). Catalogs and syntheses list workerless inquilines, dulotic species, and xenobionts across dozens of genera, with Holarctic lineages particularly rich (e.g., Formica, Polyergus, Temnothorax/Leptothorax, Strongylognathus) [1], [3], [68]. Phylogenetic work indicates multiple independent origins of social parasitism across the ant tree of life, including repeated origins of dulosis and inquilinism in different clades [13], [27], [57].

## Evolution and phylogeny

### Emery’s rule and host–parasite relatedness

Emery’s rule, formulated in the early 20th century, proposes that social parasites are closely related to their hosts; in its “strong” form, parasites and hosts are sister species, whereas the “weak” form allows close but non-sister relationships. Comparative and molecular studies generally support Emery’s rule for temporary social parasites and workerless inquilines, which often exploit a single, very closely related host. In contrast, dulotic and xenobiotic systems can involve less closely related hosts and broader host ranges, reflecting different ecological mechanics of raiding and cohabitation [8], [22]. A meta-analysis across types found Emery’s rule applies to inquilinism and temporary parasitism, but not consistently to dulosis or xenobiosis [8].

### Origins and speciation pathways

Hypotheses for the origin of social parasites include:

- Sympatric speciation via intraspecific social parasitism: Parasites arise within host species through behavioral divergence and reproductive isolation, sometimes as small queens that exploit conspecific colonies. This pathway has empirical support in ants, with work demonstrating sympatric divergence associated with social parasitism in Formica and Myrmica systems [14].

- Allopatric routes and interspecific shifts: Parasites may evolve in isolation and later invade related host species or shift hosts following secondary contact. Phylogenetic studies of Formica indicate repeated origins of dependent colony-founding strategies and transitions among temporary social parasitism and dulosis within the genus, consistent with multiple evolutionary routes [13].

The number of independent origins varies by clade. Genome-wide comparative analyses indicate slave-making behavior evolved independently multiple times, with convergent molecular signatures across lineages, and inquilines appeared independently at least twice within Acromyrmex leaf-cutting ants [27], [57].

### Genomic and developmental correlates

Genomic studies reveal convergent reductions in chemosensory gene repertoires in social parasites, consistent with reduced reliance on intra-colony communication and outsourced labor:

- Slave-making ants harbor substantially fewer gustatory receptor genes than their hosts, and show losses in olfactory receptors across independent origins of dulosis, suggesting convergent genomic erosion tied to parasitic life history [27].

- Inquilines in leaf-cutting ants show genome-wide erosion under relaxed selection, with parallel losses especially in olfactory receptor gene families. MicroCT-based neuroanatomy further indicates reduced olfactory lobe complexity in parasite brains, aligning with restricted behavioral repertoires and loss or reduction of worker caste [57].

These changes complement developmental modifications (e.g., worker caste loss in inquilines, altered queen morphologies), and may include adjustments to caste determination pathways, although detailed genetic mechanisms remain an active research area [57], [22].

## Life history and behavior

### Colony founding and invasion strategies

Parasite queens employ diverse tactics to enter and integrate into host colonies:

- Queen usurpation: Parasite queens infiltrate host nests and eliminate resident queens. In Polyergus slave-makers, newly mated queens can accompany raiding parties, enter target Formica nests, deploy Dufour’s gland secretions that appease defenders, locate and kill the host queen, and acquire host cuticular odor during the kill, facilitating acceptance by host workers [80], [84]. Chemical analyses and behavioral assays show appeasement allomones from Polyergus queens reduce host aggression during usurpation [80].

- Adoption and delayed queen killing: Some parasites initially coexist with host queens and delay killing; in certain Leptothorax/Doronomyrmex inquilines, queen elimination can occur months after entry [1].

- Dependence on host workers: Workerless inquilines rely entirely on host workers and often have reduced mobility and specialized morphologies for life inside host nests; they invest primarily in producing sexuals and may mate within the host nest [34], [38], [22].

### Reproductive biology and caste shifts

Workerless inquilines represent extreme reproductive specialization: they typically produce few or no workers, retain or tolerate host queens, and emphasize sexual reproduction. Male morphologies can deviate from typical winged males, with ergatoid or pupoid forms reported in some social parasites (e.g., Anergates) [3], [38]. Slave-makers maintain their own worker caste but often exhibit morphological specialization (e.g., sickle-shaped mandibles in Polyergus workers) that impairs independent brood care and foraging, reinforcing dependence on slaves for most tasks [1], [22].

### Dulotic raiding

Slave-making raids involve scouting, recruitment, rapid breaching of host nests, and brood transport:

- In Formica sanguinea group slave-makers, workers lay odor trails and discharge “propaganda substances”—acetates from Dufour’s glands—that trigger alarm and disorganize host defenders, increasing raid success. Artificial trails and gland extracts can guide raiders and initiate raids in experimental settings [72].

- In Polyergus, raids are often large-scale and repeated, with specialized fighters incapable of performing routine colony tasks; they rely on stolen pupae that imprint on the parasite colony’s odor upon eclosion [1], [22].

- Rossomyrmex exhibits distinctive raiding: adult transport in single recruitment chains rather than pheromone trails, minimal fighting, exploitation of host nests over two days, and slave workers do not participate in raids. Queens usurp host nests using repellent Dufour’s gland secretions (e.g., tetradecanal), which later decline after host queen elimination [52], [54], [90].

Raid outcomes can severely impact host colonies, reducing worker numbers, reproductive output, and altering allocation strategies, with effects varying across landscapes [48].

## Chemical ecology and integration

Nestmate recognition in ants is predominantly mediated by cuticular hydrocarbons (CHCs), complex blends that encode colony identity and other social information. Key mechanisms enabling parasitic integration include:

- Chemical mimicry and camouflage: Parasites can biosynthesize host-like CHCs (mimicry) or acquire them through contact (camouflage). Experimental reconstructions of CHC blends demonstrate sufficiency for nestmate recognition and colony-specific aggression in Formica. Parasites and other myrmecophiles adjust CHC profiles to match hosts, often within days, and may combine acquisition with synthesis depending on context [23], [22].

- Chemical insignificance: Callow ants and some parasitic queens at the moment of usurpation can be “odorless” relative to colony-specific labels, facilitating entry before acquiring host odors (“gestalt” integration) [22].

- Propaganda and appeasement allomones: Slave-makers deploy offensive chemicals that disrupt host cohesion and reduce aggression. Propaganda substances in Formica sanguinea group are alkyl acetates that both attract raiders and disperse defenders. Harpagoxenus sublaevis uses Dufour’s gland compounds to induce intraspecific fighting among hosts, with geographic mosaics in host reactions and variation in gland composition across populations. Polyergus queens produce appeasement secretions that lower resident worker aggression during usurpation [72], [91], [94], [80].

- Chemical labeling and exchange: In some dulotic systems, parasite workers “label” slaves with their own hydrocarbons; enslaved hosts become chemically differentiated from free-living conspecifics, reinforcing parasite-centric colony identity. Mixed colonies exhibit interspecific hydrocarbon exchange that brings profiles closer, though species-specific differences may remain [73], [74].

Neurophysiological and behavioral evidence supports specialized antennal sensilla and contact-based detection of foreign CHC profiles, enabling rapid discrimination and response [23].

## Host–parasite interactions and coevolution

Host specificity varies by parasitism type. Inquilines often specialize on a single, closely related host species, whereas dulotic parasites may exploit multiple hosts. Host defenses include aggressive recognition, nest evacuation, architectural barriers, and seasonal shifts in acceptance thresholds to balance defense costs. Parasites counter-adapt through chemical deception, rapid raiding, and local tuning of CHC profiles [8], [23], [47].

Coevolutionary arms races are well documented. In the Protomognathus americanus–Temnothorax system, parasites adjust their chemical profiles to local hosts and induce reciprocal changes in enslaved host CHCs; hosts show efficient enemy recognition, discriminating invading slave workers from free-living conspecifics, and adjust defenses seasonally. Field manipulations demonstrate strong parasite impacts on host density, social structure, and reproductive allocation, with local adaptation of parasites to sympatric host populations—patterns consistent with geographic mosaic theory [47], [48].

## Diversity, distribution, and examples

Social parasites occur worldwide but are disproportionately recorded in Holarctic temperate regions. Reviews report a small fraction of described ants are confirmed social parasites, with fewer records from the tropics. This uneven distribution remains an open question, potentially reflecting both ecological constraints and research bias [1], [68].

Representative taxa and traits:

- Polyergus (Formicinae): Obligate slave-makers of Formica. Workers have scimitar-like mandibles adapted for raiding; queens usurp host nests using appeasement secretions and kill host queens while acquiring host odor. Colonies maintain high proportions of slave workers and cannot survive without them [1], [80], [84].

- Formica sanguinea group (Formicinae): Facultative slave-makers; raiding coordinated by odor trails and Dufour’s gland acetates functioning as propaganda substances. Enslaved F. fusca workers in mixed colonies become chemically differentiated from free-living conspecifics, indicating parasite-centered labeling [72], [73].

- Rossomyrmex (Formicinae): Obligate slave-makers specialized to arid steppe environments, each species associated with a single Proformica host. Raids rely on adult transport chains rather than pheromones; queens use Dufour’s repellent during usurpation, and slaves do not raid [52], [54], [90].

- Harpagoxenus sublaevis; Protomognathus americanus; Temnothorax dulotic species (Myrmicinae): Small-colony systems nesting in twigs and acorns; Harpagoxenus uses propaganda secretions that can trigger host–host fighting; Protomognathus–Temnothorax exhibits documented arms races and local adaptation [91], [94], [48].

- Strongylognathus (Myrmicinae: Tetramoriini): Dulotic parasites of Tetramorium; raids have been observed in laboratory and field settings; several species display degenerate or workerless states [88], [3].

- Anergates atratulus (Myrmicinae): Workerless inquiline parasite in Tetramorium colonies; queens are fed exclusively by host workers and produce only sexuals. Laboratory observations and field records document its rarity and host associations in the Palearctic, with occurrences also in North America where Tetramorium immigrans is introduced [38], [36].

- Tetramorium inquilinum species-group (Myrmicinae): Degenerate workerless inquilines parasitic on Tetramorium hosts; new species (Tetramorium albenae) expands known host range and highlights morphological reductions typical of the group [34].

- Solenopsis daguerrei (Myrmicinae): Parasitic ant of imported fire ants (Solenopsis invicta), reduces host colony growth, production of sexuals, and queen numbers in polygyne colonies; proposed as a biocontrol agent but difficult to rear and introduce [41], [44].

- Megalomyrmex symmetochus (Myrmicinae): Guest ant parasitic on fungus-growing Sericomyrmex amabilis; integrates chemically, alters host foraging toward protein-biased resources, and imposes long-term costs on host colony performance (reduced fungus garden and brood mass), though host populations can remain stable [63], [65].

- Acromyrmex insinuator and other leaf-cutting ant inquilines (Attini): Workerless parasites with recent independent origins in Central and South America; show convergent genome erosion and reduced chemosensory repertoires alongside extreme morphological and behavioral specialization [57].

## Ecology, conservation, and human relevance

Social parasites are generally rare and sensitive to habitat loss and fragmentation, largely because they depend on specific hosts and colony demography. Conservation of host species and habitats is thus critical for parasite persistence. Legal protections for wood ants in Europe illustrate policy approaches to safeguarding keystone host species; a systematic review documents protection status and threat classifications for European red wood ants, with many jurisdictions recognizing their ecological importance and implementing conservation measures [98].

Parasites can impact host population dynamics and community structure by reducing host densities, altering life-history traits, and modifying trophic flows. In fungus-growing ant systems, guest parasites change host foraging and nutrient allocation with measurable performance costs [65]. In northern forests, slave-makers influence the demography of twig-nesting hosts across landscapes [48].

Human relevance includes biocontrol considerations. Solenopsis daguerrei is a candidate biological control agent against imported fire ants; it suppresses colony growth and reproduction but faces practical challenges in rearing and establishment, and integrated management combining biological agents (e.g., decapitating phorid flies, microsporidia) with chemical tactics is currently more feasible than parasite-based eradication [44], [41].

## History of research and current debates

Historical milestones include early descriptive work on social parasitism, formulation of Emery’s rule for host–parasite relatedness, and mid‑ to late‑20th‑century syntheses of ant social behavior and chemical ecology. Foundational reviews and monographs established the terminology and classic classifications of temporary social parasitism, dulosis, inquilinism, and xenobiosis, and emphasized the centrality of chemical communication to nestmate recognition [22], [1].

Contemporary research integrates phylogenomics, chemical ecology, neurobiology, and field experiments. Current debates and open questions include:

- Classification boundaries: Whether xenobiosis is best treated as parasitism or commensalism; how to categorize behaviors like kleptoparasitism relative to social parasitism [1], [23].

- Emery’s rule scope: Strong support for close relatedness in inquilines and temporary parasites, but mixed evidence in dulosis and xenobiosis, with host range breadth and colony size influencing relatedness patterns [8].

- Speciation pathways: Relative contribution of sympatric vs allopatric routes; frequency of transitions among parasitism types within clades [14], [13].

- Genomic mechanisms: Extent and causes of chemosensory receptor losses, links to neuroanatomy and behavior, and whether similar patterns occur across parasitism types beyond dulosis and inquilinism [27], [57].

- Biogeography and discovery biases: Reasons for the apparent rarity of slave-makers in the tropics and uneven distribution of social parasites across subfamilies and regions [1], [68].

Taxonomic issues also persist, such as distinguishing true inquiline species from morphs or microgynes within host species, which bears on counts of independent origins and ecological interpretations. While some systems show clear interspecific parasitism, others blur species boundaries and highlight the need for combined genetic, behavioral, and chemical evidence [1].
