# Closures of the University of Wisconsin branch campuses

The closures of University of Wisconsin branch campuses refer to decisions from 2017 through the mid‑2020s to restructure, consolidate, or end academic operations at two-year campuses across the University of Wisconsin (UW) System. These branch campuses—formerly the stand-alone UW Colleges—were attached to nearby four‑year universities after a system-wide restructuring approved in late 2017 and implemented in 2018. Beginning in 2022, several branch campuses announced the cessation of in‑person instruction, teach‑outs of associate degrees, or formal sunsetting of academic operations, generally citing sustained enrollment declines, demographic shifts, and budget pressures. Actions affected campuses attached to UW–Milwaukee, UW–Oshkosh, UW–Green Bay, UW–Whitewater, and UW–Platteville, while decisions at UW–Stevens Point focused on program adjustments rather than complete closures. Facilities—many owned and maintained by county governments—have been repurposed in some communities for K–12, technical college, or continuing education uses, with transfer agreements for students and redeployment or layoffs for staff and faculty announced in each case [1][2][3][5][6][8][11][13][15][18][19].

## Background

The UW Colleges historically comprised 13 two‑year campuses awarding the Associate of Arts and Science (AAS) degree with a mission of local access, affordability, and transfer to four‑year programs. On October 11, 2017, UW System President Ray Cross proposed restructuring the UW Colleges and UW–Extension by integrating each two‑year campus as a branch of a nearby four‑year university and realigning extension services within institutions, citing enrollment declines, demographic changes, and the need to streamline administration and student pathways [2]. The Board of Regents approved the restructuring on November 9, 2017, and implementation took effect July 1, 2018. The Higher Learning Commission subsequently approved accreditation changes to reflect branch campus status. Under the plan, branch campuses continued to offer lower‑division coursework with transfer pathways coordinated by their supervising four‑year institutions, while county governments generally continued to own campus facilities and were responsible for capital maintenance under existing statutes and agreements [1][3].

Following the restructuring, the former UW Colleges were rebranded as branch campuses of their assigned four‑year universities, for example: UW–Milwaukee at Washington County and Waukesha; UW–Oshkosh at Fond du Lac and Fox Cities; UW–Green Bay at Manitowoc, Marinette, and Sheboygan; UW–Stevens Point at Marshfield and Wausau; UW–Platteville at Richland and Baraboo/Sauk County; and UW–Whitewater at Rock County [1].

## Chronology and campuses affected

This section lists documented decisions (announcements, votes, effective dates) to close, end in‑person instruction, consolidate programs, or formally sunset academic operations at branch campuses since the 2017–2018 restructuring. “Closure” is used by sources with varying specificity; this article distinguishes between ceasing in‑person instruction (no face-to-face credit courses), sunsetting academic operations (ending degree and for‑credit offerings), and facility disposition or repurposing.

- UW–Platteville at Richland (Richland Center)
  - Decision: UW–Platteville announced the suspension of in‑person instruction at the Richland branch due to prolonged enrollment declines and financial constraints. Teach‑out and transfer options were provided for enrolled students [4][5].
  - Effective dates: In‑person instruction ended in 2023, with academic operations sunsetting thereafter; noncredit uses and community programming were explored with local partners [4][5].
  - Continuing/ended operations: Face‑to‑face for‑credit classes ceased; transfer advising and teach‑outs were offered; discussions with local school districts and technical colleges about facility use were initiated [4][5][20].
  - Facility status: Richland County, as owner, engaged in planning for repurposing the site, including potential K–12 and technical college uses [18][20].

- UW–Milwaukee at Washington County (West Bend)
  - Decision: UW–Milwaukee announced it would end in‑person instruction at Washington County, citing enrollment declines and duplication of offerings regionally; UWM at Waukesha remained open under UWM supervision [6][7].
  - Effective dates: The change was announced for the 2024–2025 academic cycle, with student teach‑out plans and transfer pathways to UWM and other campuses [6][7].
  - Governance: Washington County officials discussed building ownership and future use; coordination with UWM and potential technical college partners was reported [6][18].

- UW–Oshkosh at Fond du Lac
  - Decision: UW–Oshkosh announced an end to in‑person instruction at its Fond du Lac branch, aligning offerings with regional demand and budget realities of the institution’s deficit reduction plan. Teach‑outs and transfers were arranged [8][9].
  - Effective dates: Changes were implemented on a timeline beginning in 2024; associate-level instruction and services were transitioned or discontinued as specified in campus communications [8][9].
  - Facility: The Fond du Lac County-owned facilities remained available for potential partner uses coordinated by county and institutional stakeholders [8][19].

- UW–Oshkosh at Fox Cities (Menasha)
  - Decision: UW–Oshkosh announced plans to sunset in‑person instruction at Fox Cities following continued enrollment declines; teach‑outs and transfer supports were outlined [10].
  - Effective dates: Transitions were staged through 2024–2025 academic years [10].
  - Partnerships: Discussions with local stakeholders and Fox Valley Technical College about space and programming followed [10].

- UW–Whitewater at Rock County (Janesville)
  - Decision: UW–Whitewater announced it would end in‑person instruction at Rock County, presenting teach‑out plans and transfer options to the main campus and other institutions [11][12].
  - Effective dates: Changes aligned to conclude face‑to‑face offerings by the end of the 2024–2025 academic year, according to university announcements [11][12].
  - Local governance: Rock County officials addressed implications for a county-owned campus and explored repurposing options [19].

- UW–Green Bay at Marinette
  - Decision: UW–Green Bay announced plans to end in‑person instruction at Marinette, noting long‑term enrollment declines and a costly physical plant. UW–Green Bay’s other branch campuses (Manitowoc and Sheboygan) continued operations with adjusted programming [13][14].
  - Effective dates: Phase‑out of for‑credit, face‑to‑face offerings was set for completion by 2025 academic cycles [13][14].
  - Aftermath: The City/County owners and UW–Green Bay initiated discussions with Northeast Wisconsin Technical College and community partners for future use [13][14].

- UW–Stevens Point at Marshfield and Wausau
  - Status: UW–Stevens Point implemented program changes and enrollment strategies at its branches; sources did not document a complete cessation of in‑person instruction through 2025 but reported ongoing evaluation of offerings and regional demand [1][15].

- UW–Platteville at Baraboo/Sauk County; UW–Green Bay at Manitowoc and Sheboygan; UW–Milwaukee at Waukesha
  - Status: These branches remained open as of 2025 with revised program mixes and recruitment efforts, amid system-wide declines in lower‑division enrollment [1][15].

## Reasons and drivers

Reliable sources consistently attribute branch campus closures or the cessation of in‑person instruction to several interrelated pressures:

- Enrollment trends: Systemwide and campus-specific declines in lower‑division headcount at branch locations, particularly among traditional-aged students in rural and small‑metro areas, reduced tuition revenue and utilization of facilities [1][15].
- Demographic shifts: Fewer high school graduates in many Wisconsin counties and out‑migration patterns contributed to sustained enrollment pressure at two‑year locations [2][15][17].
- Budget constraints: Institutional budget deficits and state funding policy limits led universities to evaluate cost structures at branch campuses, where per‑student costs can be high due to underutilized infrastructure [2][8][11][15][16].
- Post‑COVID impacts: The pandemic intensified preexisting trends toward online and hybrid instruction, further weakening demand for small, face‑to‑face branch offerings and accelerating student preference for direct enrollment at four‑year campuses or technical colleges [15][17].
- Program realignments: Supervising universities sought to consolidate lower‑division offerings, expand dual‑enrollment and transfer pathways, and focus branch campuses on specialized programming or community services where feasible [1][6][10][11][13].

## Governance and decision‑making

Branch campus decisions followed governance processes involving:

- UW System leadership and Board of Regents: The System proposed the 2017 restructuring and coordinated accreditation and administrative transitions in 2018; subsequent branch campus decisions were made at the institutional level, with updates to the Regents where board approvals or notices were required [1][2][3].
- Chancellors and campus leadership: Announcements to end in‑person instruction or sunset programs were made by supervising university chancellors, typically with rationale referencing enrollment and budget analyses, and including teach‑out/transfer commitments [4][6][8][10][11][13].
- County governments: Many physical facilities are owned by counties under longstanding arrangements. County boards and executives addressed building disposition, maintenance obligations, and potential repurposing, sometimes passing resolutions or opening negotiations with technical colleges or school districts [18][19].
- Accreditation and academic oversight: Changes to degrees and instructional modalities were coordinated with the Higher Learning Commission and with system and institutional academic governance bodies to ensure teach‑outs and transfer pathways met standards [1][3].

## Impacts

Documented impacts include:

- Students: Universities provided teach‑out plans, transfer advising, and waivers or credits to complete AAS or to transition to bachelor’s pathways at supervising universities or elsewhere. Some branches expanded dual‑enrollment with local high schools during transition periods [4][6][8][10][11][13].
- Faculty and staff: Institutions reported redeployments to main campuses or other units where possible and layoffs or nonrenewals where positions were eliminated. Union representatives and shared governance bodies issued statements about due process and community impacts [8][11][12][14].
- Financials: Universities cited savings from reduced facility operations and staffing at under‑enrolled branches, with anticipated one‑time transition costs for teach‑outs, severance, and moving. Counties evaluated maintenance savings against future costs of repurposing or mothballing buildings [8][11][15][19].
- Community economic effects: Local officials and media reported concerns about reduced foot traffic, fewer cultural and continuing education offerings, and loss of regional identity linked to UW presence; some communities pursued partnerships to retain educational uses on site [5][7][14][18][19].

## Reactions and controversies

Reactions varied across communities and institutions:

- Local officials and county boards: Some county leaders supported transitions, citing unsustainable enrollment and fiscal realities, while others urged the UW System or supervising universities to maintain limited offerings or explore shared services. County ownership complicated timelines and responsibilities for facilities [7][18][19].
- Legislators: State lawmakers representing affected districts issued statements ranging from criticism of closures to calls for reimagined rural access strategies (e.g., expanded dual‑enrollment, distance education hubs), highlighting differences over state funding and oversight [7][12][15].
- Students and faculty: Shared governance groups and student leaders expressed concerns about access and continuity, particularly for place‑bound or nontraditional students; universities responded with transfer guarantees and advising resources [8][11][12][13].
- Editorial boards and media: Coverage emphasized long‑term demographic and enrollment trends and questioned whether the 2018 restructuring could sustain branch campuses absent significant program redesign or funding changes [5][7][14][15][17].

## Aftermath

Post‑closure or post‑sunset developments reported by official statements and media include:

- Property disposition and repurposing: County owners pursued repurposing with K–12 districts, technical colleges, nonprofits, and municipal agencies. Plans often retained educational or community uses (e.g., dual‑enrollment sites, workforce training, continuing education, public events) [18][19][20].
- Partnerships: Several communities engaged nearby technical colleges to expand workforce-focused offerings in former branch campus facilities. Universities coordinated transfer pathways and occasionally retained limited presence for advising or outreach [6][10][13][20].
- Continuing and noncredit uses: Some facilities hosted continuing education, community programming, youth summer programs, or shared services with local governments even after for‑credit instruction ended [4][18][20].
- Systemwide strategy: The UW System and institutions continued to monitor enrollment and evaluate branch campus roles, emphasizing online expansion, transfer pipelines, and targeted in‑person offerings where demand supports sustainability [1][15][16].
