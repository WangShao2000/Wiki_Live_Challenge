# 2011 Sawyerville–Eoline tornado

The Sawyerville–Eoline tornado was a large, long‑tracked EF3 tornado that struck parts of west‑central Alabama late on April 27, 2011, during the peak of the 2011 Super Outbreak. It formed at 5:30 p.m. CDT (22:30 UTC), tracked northeast for 72.13 miles across Greene, Hale, and Bibb counties, reached a maximum path width of approximately 1 mile, and dissipated at 6:55 p.m. CDT (23:55 UTC) near Marvel in eastern Bibb County. National Weather Service (NWS) Birmingham surveyors estimated peak winds of 145 mph. The tornado caused seven fatalities and at least 50 injuries, with extensive destruction of homes and mobile homes, substantial tree damage including within Talladega National Forest, and the destruction of the Eoline Volunteer Fire Department station and another nearby business in Bibb County. NWS Birmingham refers to this event as the “Sawyerville–Eoline tornado,” and its detailed track and impacts are documented in the office’s formal survey archive for April 27, 2011 [1][2].

## Background and broader context

The tornado occurred during the historic April 27, 2011 severe weather day in Alabama, part of a multi‑day outbreak across the eastern United States (April 25–28). In central Alabama, NWS Birmingham documented two distinct waves on April 27: a pre‑dawn quasi‑linear convective system that produced widespread wind damage and numerous tornadoes, and an afternoon–evening wave of discrete supercells that spawned long‑lived, strong to violent tornadoes. Across central Alabama on April 27 alone, the office confirmed 29 tornadoes, including eight EF3s and four EF4s, with at least 1,998 injuries and 240 fatalities statewide. The Sawyerville–Eoline tornado was among the longer tracks and more severe events in the Birmingham forecast area that afternoon and evening [2].

The Storm Prediction Center (SPC) highlighted a High Risk for severe thunderstorms (including strong tornadoes) over portions of Alabama on April 27 and issued multiple tornado watches, including Particularly Dangerous Situation (PDS) Tornado Watch 235 (effective 1:45 p.m.–10:00 p.m. CDT) and PDS Tornado Watch 241 (effective 5:52 p.m.–1:00 a.m. CDT), which covered the counties impacted by this tornado. NWS Birmingham issued 49 tornado warnings during the day. These official SPC outlooks and watch issuances indicate a classic, high‑end severe weather environment supporting long‑track tornadic supercells [2].

## Meteorological synopsis

A robust synoptic setup over the Southeast supported tornadic supercells on April 27. A deepening surface low and strong upper‑level trough provided lift, with rich Gulf moisture and strong instability across Alabama. Vertical wind shear was extreme, with a strong low‑level jet and high storm‑relative helicity favoring persistent mesocyclones. By mid‑afternoon, discrete supercells developed over Mississippi and moved into Alabama within overlapping PDS tornado watches. Within NWS Birmingham’s area, radar imagery showed tight storm‑relative velocity couplets and strong reflectivity cores along the tracks of several long‑lived tornadoes, including the circulation responsible for the Sawyerville–Eoline tornado [2]. NWS Birmingham specifically notes 0.5° reflectivity and storm‑relative velocity imagery at 5:55 p.m. CDT showing the circulation over Sawyerville [1].

## Formation, path, and dissipation

- Time and coordinates: The tornado touched down at 5:30 p.m. CDT (22:30 UTC) in far southwestern Greene County near the Tombigbee River, west of Tishabee, at approximately 32.6152°N, 88.0543°W. It dissipated at 6:55 p.m. CDT (23:55 UTC) just northeast of Marvel in eastern Bibb County near 33.1524°N, 86.9898°W [1].
- Track: After forming near County Road (CR) 69 and Head Drive, the tornado moved northeast across rural Greene County north of Forkland, then crossed the Black Warrior River into Hale County. It intensified as it approached and crossed Alabama Route 14 just north of Sawyerville, tracked across multiple local roads (including CR 18, CR 21, and Alabama Highway 69 south of Harper Hill), and entered Talladega National Forest near Ingram. Crossing into Bibb County, the tornado passed through and near Eoline, crossed Alabama Highway 5 south of West Blocton, weakened to EF2 near CR 26, and lifted northeast of Marvel [1].
- Counties and communities affected: Greene County (near Tishabee; rural areas north of Forkland), Hale County (Sawyerville area; Harper Hill; Ingram), and Bibb County (Eoline; areas south of West Blocton; Marvel vicinity) [1].

## Intensity, duration, and EF‑scale changes

The Enhanced Fujita (EF) scale rates tornado damage from EF0 (weak) to EF5 (violent) based on observed damage indicators (e.g., types of structures, degree of damage to buildings and trees). NWS Birmingham assigned an overall EF3 rating to the Sawyerville–Eoline tornado, with estimated peak winds of 145 mph and a maximum path width of about 1 mile (1,760 yards). The tornado was on the ground for approximately 85 minutes and traveled 72.13 miles [1].

Intensity varied along the path:
- Greene County: Initial EF1 damage at touchdown, strengthening to EF2 with damage to several mobile homes, outbuildings, and churches, and extensive tree damage [1].
- Hale County: The tornado reached EF3 intensity near AL‑14 just north of Sawyerville (145 mph), producing extensive structural damage and severe treefall. This segment accounted for many of the casualties and the widest damage swath [1].
- Bibb County: EF3‑level damage occurred in and around Eoline, where numerous homes and mobile homes were destroyed or sustained major damage. The tornado weakened to EF2 as it progressed northeast, including near AL‑5 south of West Blocton and CR 26, before dissipating near Marvel [1].

## Damage and impacts

- Structural damage: The tornado damaged or destroyed numerous mobile homes, single‑family residences, and at least one church in Greene and Hale counties, with thousands of trees snapped or uprooted along the track. In Bibb County, multiple homes sustained major damage, about a dozen homes/mobile homes were destroyed, the Eoline Volunteer Fire Department station and another business were destroyed, and widespread tree damage was observed in and adjacent to Talladega National Forest [1].
- Critical infrastructure: The Eoline Volunteer Fire Department building was destroyed; twelve residents sheltered in the building’s hallway and survived with injuries. Both fire trucks at the station were crushed, prompting a community fundraising effort to rebuild the facility and restore equipment. Event organizers and local officials anticipated federal assistance to help finance reconstruction and planned to include a safe room in the new station [3].
- Casualties: NWS Birmingham documented 7 fatalities and approximately 50 injuries for this tornado, concentrated in Hale County (6 fatalities, ~40 injuries) with one fatality and at least ten injuries in Bibb County, including one death in a vehicle near the Eoline fire station [1].

## Radar observations and warning lead time

NWS Birmingham’s KBMX radar showed a strong storm‑relative velocity couplet and high reflectivity consistent with a significant tornadic circulation as the storm crossed Hale County; imagery at 5:55 p.m. CDT highlighted the circulation over Sawyerville [1]. Tornado Warnings were in effect across Greene, Hale, and Bibb counties from 4:50 p.m. until 7:45 p.m. CDT, providing roughly 40 minutes of lead time ahead of the initial touchdown (actual lead times to specific locations varied along the path) [1][2].

## Damage survey

NWS Birmingham meteorologists conducted ground surveys across Greene, Hale, and Bibb counties. Surveyors used Enhanced Fujita scale damage indicators—including assessments of residences (e.g., construction type and degree of structural failure), mobile homes, churches, outbuildings, and the extent of tree damage—to determine intensity along the track. Their report concluded the event was consistent with a strong tornado, with EF3 segments near Sawyerville and Eoline and weakening to EF2 toward West Blocton and Marvel. The surveyed path length was 72.13 miles, with a maximum width near 1 mile; peak winds were estimated near 145 mph. The survey notes extensive tree damage within Talladega National Forest, consistent with the tornado’s track and intensity variations [1].

## Emergency response and immediate aftermath

Local responders and residents undertook immediate rescue, debris removal, and support activities. In Eoline, the destruction of the volunteer fire station spurred a community “Fun Day” fundraiser held in June 2011 to raise money for rebuilding and equipment replacement. Organizers projected a need for substantial funds and coordinated with state and federal partners (including anticipated FEMA assistance) to rebuild the station with a safe room and procure a replacement truck. The event also reflected the broader community’s reliance on volunteer departments for firefighting and emergency support in rural Bibb County [3].

## Context and notability within the 2011 Super Outbreak

Within NWS Birmingham’s forecast area, the Sawyerville–Eoline tornado was one of several long‑track, high‑impact tornadoes on the afternoon–evening of April 27. While the Tuscaloosa–Birmingham EF4 drew national attention due to its urban impacts, the Sawyerville–Eoline tornado produced severe, deadly damage across rural Hale and Bibb counties and is among the office’s documented major tracks from that day. NWS Birmingham’s official event archive and track catalog refer to the “Sawyerville–Eoline” designation, reflecting the tornado’s passage near those communities and its relative prominence within the outbreak’s central Alabama component [2].

## Naming

NWS Birmingham’s dedicated survey page uses the “Sawyerville–Eoline tornado” designation, which is the authoritative name for this event. Some secondary materials refer to “Sawyerville/Eoline” or include nearby localities, but the NWS Birmingham usage is the reference standard in official documentation [1][2].

## Use in later studies

The track and damage footprint of the Sawyerville–Eoline tornado have been used in subsequent remote sensing and damage‑assessment studies, including analyses of Landsat imagery for tornado damage detection and land cover impacts, illustrating the utility of satellite‑based methods to corroborate and complement in‑situ surveys from historic outbreaks [5].
