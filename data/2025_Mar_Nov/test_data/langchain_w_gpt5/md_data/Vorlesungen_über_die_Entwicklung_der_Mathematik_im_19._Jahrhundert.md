# Vorlesungen über die Entwicklung der Mathematik im 19. Jahrhundert

Vorlesungen über die Entwicklung der Mathematik im 19. Jahrhundert is a two‑part German‑language history of nineteenth‑century mathematics by Felix Klein. It was issued posthumously as volumes 24 and 25 of Springer’s Grundlehren der mathematischen Wissenschaften: Teil I in 1926 (xiii + 385 pages), edited for publication by Richard Courant and Otto Neugebauer, and Teil II in 1927 (x + 208 pages), edited by Courant and Stefan Cohn‑Vossen. The volumes synthesize biographical sketches and thematic accounts of major developments in analysis, geometry, algebra, and mathematical physics, and trace institutional and social contexts such as the rise of journals and technical schools in the nineteenth century [1][4][6][18].

## Authorship, editors, and original publication

- Author: Felix Klein (1849–1925), mathematician and long‑time professor at Göttingen.
- Editors:
  - Teil I: “Für den Druck bearbeitet von R. Courant und O. Neugebauer” (Richard Courant; Otto Neugebauer).
  - Teil II: “Für den Druck bearbeitet von R. Courant und St. Cohn‑Vossen” (Richard Courant; Stefan Cohn‑Vossen).
- Publisher and series: Julius Springer (Berlin), in Grundlehren der mathematischen Wissenschaften, vol. 24 (Teil I) and vol. 25 (Teil II).
- Dates and pagination:
  - Teil I: 1926; xiii + 385 pp. [1][4].
  - Teil II (subtitled Die Grundbegriffe der Invariantentheorie und ihr Eindringen in die mathematische Physik): 1927; x + 208 pp. [6][18].

Contemporary notices and later catalog records consistently describe the work as published after Klein’s death in 1925; editorial prefaces emphasize the need to bring the lectures into print “after he died the previous year” [22]. The Teil I bibliographic statement and pagination are given in a 1927 Science review [1], while the Teil II subtitle and editorial attributions are recorded in union catalogs (WorldCat) and library listings [6][23].

## Origin and context

The books derive from lecture courses Klein conceived before the First World War and delivered privately during the war years. Göttingen archival records preserve manuscripts from a course held between 28 April and 4 August 1915 (Vorträge über Entwicklung der Mathematik im 19. Jahrhundert II. Vorlesungsmanuskript), corroborating the lecture origin of the material [12]. Educational histories document Klein’s broader program of teacher training and his efforts to integrate historical development into mathematics instruction; dedicated offerings on the history of nineteenth‑century mathematics were planned in 1910 and 1912 and later taught informally during the war years [13]. The editors note in prefaces (as reflected in later reprint paratexts and catalog descriptions) that they aimed to preserve Klein’s voice while organizing draft‑like lecture notes into publishable form [22].

## Structure and contents

### Overview of Teil I (1926)

Teil I combines biographical sketches with thematic surveys of nineteenth‑century advances. A preview of the original 1926 edition’s table of contents on Google Books lists the major sections and chapter headings [4]:

- Gauss: overview of his scientific trajectory and contributions across applied and pure mathematics, including astronomy, geodesy, differential geometry, and physics (with references to collaborations and context such as Alexander von Humboldt and Wilhelm Weber).
- France and the École Polytechnique: institutional setting and key figures, including Cauchy (analysis and rigor), Monge (descriptive geometry), and Galois (foundations of group theory and algebra).
- Crelle’s Journal and the rise of pure mathematics in Germany: analytical developments (Dirichlet, Abel, Jacobi) and geometry (Möbius, Plücker, Steiner; synthetic vs. analytic methods and projective geometry). Klein’s narrative highlights the role of journals, editorial practices, and methodological debates [10][11].
- Algebraic geometry and invariant theory: Cayley and “main lines of development” (Anfänge und Hauptlinien), selected problems, Grassmann’s extension theory, and Hamilton’s work on mechanics and quaternions.
- Mechanics and mathematical physics: Hamilton’s canonical equations, Routh’s transformations, and broader nineteenth‑century mathematical physics in Germany and England.
- Complex function theory: Riemann’s geometric approach and Weierstrass’s analytic construction, with the spread of ideas through German and international schools.
- Algebra and number theory: algebraic curves, algebraic number theory (Kummer, Dedekind, Hilbert), and structural insights connecting function theory and algebra.
- Group theory and function theory: automorphic functions and further topics on symmetry, conformal mapping, and special functions.

The digitized excerpts and scholarly compilations based on the 1926 volume provide detailed case studies of the figures discussed (e.g., Möbius, Plücker) and examples illustrating methodological themes (such as Hesse’s Hessian determinant and its role in counting inflection points of plane curves) [10][11].

### Overview of Teil II (1927)

Teil II is announced with the subtitle Die Grundbegriffe der Invariantentheorie und ihr Eindringen in die mathematische Physik and turns from biographical narrative to more systematic expositions. Catalog records and library descriptions indicate that it treats:

- Linear invariant theory and its conceptual reach (connecting to Klein’s Erlangen Program).
- Special relativity and its mathematical formulations in mechanics and physics (Maxwell’s equations, Lorentz transformations).
- Transformation groups built around quadratic differential forms, leading into Riemannian geometry (foundations, normal coordinates, and contributions from Beltrami, Lipschitz, Christoffel) [6][23][24].

### Style and approach

Across both parts, Klein balances historical narrative with mathematical exposition. He emphasizes how nineteenth‑century mathematics shifted toward rigor and structural thinking while retaining links to physical problems and geometric intuition. The volumes also foreground institutional developments, including the roles of universities and journals in disseminating new methods [10][4].

## Reception and scholarly assessment

Contemporary reception was prompt and broadly favorable. A 1927 notice in Science recorded the appearance of Teil I, summarized its scope, and gave the exact pagination and publisher details [1]. Reviews in the historical journal Isis (1927 for Teil I; 1928 for Teil II) by Heinrich Wieleitner assessed the volumes’ historiographical value and content, situating Klein’s work among then‑emerging approaches to the history of mathematics [8][9]. The Mathematical Gazette listed a review of Teil I in 1926–1927, confirming attention from the Anglophone mathematics community [7]. Later historical summaries and educational studies reference Klein’s lectures as influential in bringing historical development into the mathematical curriculum and teacher training, especially in Göttingen’s environment [13].

The volumes have been noted for:
- Integrating biographical sketches with thematic surveys across analysis, geometry, algebra, and mathematical physics.
- Emphasizing institutional and methodological shifts of the nineteenth century, such as the increasing rigor of analysis and the rise of projective and invariant methods in geometry.
- Balancing pure and applied topics and connecting mathematical theory to physical science [4][10][11].

Part II’s more systematic orientation (invariant theory, relativity, Riemannian geometry) complements the biographical emphasis of Part I and reflects Klein’s broader programmatic interests (e.g., the Erlangen Program’s emphasis on transformation groups) [6][23].

## Disambiguation

This work should not be confused with Moritz Cantor’s multi‑volume Vorlesungen über Geschichte der Mathematik (Lectures on the History of Mathematics), published in several large volumes between 1880 and 1908. Cantor’s title is an extensive general history covering periods up to the late eighteenth century, distinct in scope and authorship from Klein’s focused account of the nineteenth century [2].

## Contents summary by theme

- Analysis and rigor: The French school’s contributions (Cauchy, Fourier, Poisson) and Weierstrass’s formal foundations are contrasted with Riemann’s geometric approach; Klein illustrates how rigor and function theory reshaped analysis in the nineteenth century [4].
- Geometry and invariants: Projective geometry (Monge, Möbius, Plücker, Steiner), Cayley’s invariants, Grassmann’s multidimensional algebra, and the rise of invariant theory in Part II, connecting transformation groups to classification problems and later to physics [4][6][11].
- Algebra and number theory: From Galois’s insights into symmetry to Kummer’s and Dedekind’s number theory, Klein traces structural algebraic thinking and its links to geometry and analysis, culminating in Hilbert’s work [4].
- Mathematical physics and mechanics: Hamilton’s canonical equations, the British and German schools of mathematical physics, and Maxwell’s field theory lead into the Lorentz group and relativity in Part II [4][6][24].
- Institutions and social change: Klein observes shifts from academy‑centered science to university systems, the emergence of journals (notably Crelle’s Journal), and growth in international congresses and communications, placing mathematical advances within broader societal change [10].
