# 2020 Jonesboro tornado

The 2020 Jonesboro tornado was a significant, damaging tornado that struck the city of Jonesboro, Arkansas, in Craighead County during the late afternoon of March 28, 2020. The National Weather Service (NWS) rated the tornado EF3 on the Enhanced Fujita scale, with maximum estimated winds around 140 mph. The tornado impacted densely developed commercial and residential areas—including the Mall at Turtle Creek and the municipal airport—injured 22 people, and caused no fatalities. Damage estimates from officials and media ranged in the “several hundred million dollars” owing to widespread structural losses and infrastructure impacts. The event occurred amid the early COVID‑19 pandemic, when reduced occupancy of public spaces and effective warnings were credited by officials and news outlets with limiting casualties. Local and state authorities instituted curfews and disaster measures, and federal assistance was sought following the storm. The tornado’s path was approximately 12.6 miles long and reached a maximum width near 1,800 feet according to post‑event engineering assessments. It was the strongest tornado in Arkansas since the April 27, 2014 Mayflower–Vilonia EF4 tornado. [1][6][7][9][12]

## Meteorological synopsis

A warm, unstable environment was present across Arkansas on March 28, 2020, with temperatures reaching the 80s to low 90s as a warm front moved northward. This setup supported supercell development and tornado potential. The Storm Prediction Center (SPC) issued Tornado Watch 68 that afternoon for portions of Arkansas and surrounding areas; the watch text and associated products outlined probabilities of tornadoes and severe weather hazards. SPC Day 1 Convective Outlooks for the day depicted organized severe weather risk across parts of the Mississippi Valley and the Mid‑South, with mesoscale discussions highlighting ingredients favorable for supercells, including moderate instability and strong deep‑layer shear. Satellite monitoring (GOES‑East) tracked the convective evolution and subsequent tornadoes across Arkansas. [3][4][5][15]

Regional NWS analyses documented the Jonesboro supercell’s evolution after an earlier tornado near Amagon in Jackson County. Radar indicated rotation and classic supercell structure, with intensification near the south and east sides of Jonesboro shortly before 5:00 p.m. CDT. The storm then produced the EF3 tornado through the city and continued northeastward, with additional damage reported as it moved through Brookland and just east of Paragould in Greene County. [1][2]

## Tornado event and chronology

- Date and local time: Saturday, March 28, 2020, around 5:00 p.m. CDT. [1]
- County and location: Jonesboro, Craighead County, Arkansas. The tornado formed on the south/east side of the city and traveled northeast through Jonesboro, continuing toward Brookland and near Paragould. [1]
- Warning timeline: SPC issued Tornado Watch 68 earlier in the afternoon; NWS tornado warnings were in effect as the supercell approached Jonesboro. Media and local officials relayed urgent protective actions during the event. [1][3]
- Naming: The event is commonly referred to as the “March 28, 2020 Jonesboro tornado” by NWS and media sources; the NWS Memphis Weather Forecast Office compiled a detailed StoryMap titled “A Saturday To Remember” summarizing the event. [2]

## Characteristics

- EF rating and winds: EF3 on the Enhanced Fujita scale, with maximum estimated winds around 140 mph, per NWS surveys and official statements. The EF scale rates tornadoes based on observed damage to structures and vegetation. [1][6]
- Path length and maximum width: Approximately 12.6 miles in length; maximum width near 1,800 feet. [12]
- Track and duration: The tornado tracked from the south/east side of Jonesboro northeastward through the city. As the parent supercell moved on, additional damage occurred into Greene County. Precise minute‑by‑minute duration varies by source; NWS and state summaries consistently place the main urban impact near 5:00 p.m. CDT. [1]
- Related tornadoes: The same day produced at least two additional tornadoes in northeast Arkansas, including an EF1 tornado in southeast Greene County. [1][6]

## Damage and casualties

The tornado produced extensive damage across commercial corridors, residential neighborhoods, and public infrastructure:

- Casualties: 22 injuries were reported; two were hospitalized. No fatalities occurred. [6][7]
- Commercial and public sites: The Mall at Turtle Creek sustained severe structural damage, with multiple stores significantly damaged. Damage was also noted at and near the Jonesboro Municipal Airport, including hangars and aircraft. A train derailment north of the airport occurred as high winds affected rail operations. [1][6][8]
- Utility impacts: Strong winds and tornado damage caused widespread power interruptions. A measured 74 mph wind gust was recorded at the airport during the event. [1]
- Residential damage counts: Early assessments varied as surveys progressed. Officials reported more than 50 private residences destroyed with up to 200 structures damaged at roughly the halfway point of the Red Cross assessment; subsequent local news summaries cited totals such as 55 homes completely destroyed (with major and minor damages to additional structures) as assessment coverage increased. [9][10]
- Economic losses: City and state officials projected damages in the “several hundred million dollars” range. [6][9]

## Emergency response and immediate aftermath

Local authorities established command posts and instituted curfews to facilitate search‑and‑rescue, secure damaged areas, and coordinate debris removal:

- Curfews: Curfews were imposed beginning the night of March 28–29 to limit travel and enable emergency operations; curfew hours were adjusted over subsequent days and later lifted as conditions improved. [6][10][11]
- Disaster declarations: The Governor declared a state disaster for the area and indicated pursuit of a federal disaster declaration; damaged businesses were acknowledged as eligible for pandemic‑related assistance programs. [9]
- COVID‑19 context: Officials and media emphasized that reduced occupancy due to COVID‑19 precautions likely mitigated casualties. Guidance during response stressed maintaining social distancing while conducting cleanup and assistance operations. [6][7][9]
- City actions: The City of Jonesboro coordinated volunteer management, debris guidance, and resource distribution through public information channels, emphasizing safety and logistical support for affected residents. [10]

## Recovery and longer-term outcomes

Recovery actions in the months and years following the tornado included:

- Commercial reconstruction: Numerous businesses pursued rebuilding and reopening, including retail and service sites along damaged corridors. The city documented examples of properties that were restored after being heavily damaged (e.g., a local tire shop along Caraway Road). [13]
- Mall at Turtle Creek: The mall sustained severe damage during the tornado, and subsequent reporting characterized the structure as beyond repair; closures and longer‑term disposition reflected the extent of structural loss. [16]
- Community operations: Utilities restored power to broad areas as debris removal progressed. Curfews were lifted as emergency access gave way to repair and cleanup, with continuing guidance to avoid sightseeing and to follow official debris disposal instructions. [11][10]

## Notability and broader context

The Jonesboro tornado is notable for its intensity, urban impact, and unusually low casualty count relative to the severity of damage:

- Strongest in Arkansas since 2014: NWS noted that the March 28, 2020 Jonesboro EF3 tornado was the strongest in the state since the EF4 Mayflower–Vilonia tornado on April 27, 2014. [1]
- Early pandemic conditions: The event occurred during the early COVID‑19 period, when reduced public activity and effective communication of warnings were repeatedly cited as factors limiting injuries and preventing fatalities despite striking a major retail center on a late‑afternoon weekend. [6][7]
- Broader outbreak: The parent system produced multiple severe storms and tornadoes across the Mid‑South and Ohio Valley on March 28–29, with regional offices highlighting environments of moderate instability and strong shear in the broader area. [5][15]

## Controversies and uncertainties

- Warning/emergency terminology: Media and social posts referenced enhanced warning language during the event, and unofficial archives catalog tornado emergencies issued by NWS in recent years. However, direct, event‑specific public NWS text products confirming a “tornado emergency” issuance for Jonesboro are not universally cited in publicly accessible archives; accounts consistently note urgent, high‑impact messaging in warnings and local coverage. [16]
- Damage counts and cost estimates: Early damage surveys provided partial counts that changed as coverage expanded, producing differing tallies of destroyed and damaged structures. Officials and outlets consistently projected high economic losses but did so in ranges rather than final totals, reflecting evolving assessments. [9][10]
