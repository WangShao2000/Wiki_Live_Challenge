# National Geographic Society Headquarters

The National Geographic Society Headquarters is the headquarters complex of the National Geographic Society (NGS) in Washington, D.C., occupying the north half of Square 183 between 16th and 17th Streets NW and M Street NW and the Sumner Row alley, with principal addresses at 1145 17th Street NW, 1156 16th Street NW, and 1600 M Street NW [1][3]. The campus comprises early 20th‑century buildings facing 16th Street (Hubbard Memorial Hall and the Administration Building), a mid‑century New Formalist office and exhibition building by Edward Durell Stone on 17th Street, and a 1984 M Street addition by Skidmore, Owings & Merrill that includes the Gilbert H. Grosvenor Auditorium [2][3][4]. The site has long housed NGS offices and public facilities such as Explorers Hall and the Grosvenor Auditorium; the Society is redeveloping its public-facing spaces as the National Geographic Museum of Exploration, scheduled to open in summer 2026 at 1600 M Street NW [3][13][20][22]. The complex is listed on the District of Columbia Inventory of Historic Sites (2022) and the National Register of Historic Places (2023) for its architectural and institutional significance [2][5]. It is within walking distance of Metrorail’s Dupont Circle (Red Line), Farragut North (Red Line), and Farragut West (Orange, Silver, and Blue Lines) stations [6][7][8][9][22].

## Location and site

The headquarters is located at 17th and M Streets NW; the Society’s published headquarters address is 1145 17th Street NW, Washington, DC 20036 [1]. The historic headquarters property spans the north half of Square 183 and is bounded by 16th Street NW (east), 17th Street NW (west), M Street NW (north), and the Sumner Row alley (south) [3]. The constituent addresses identified in the District of Columbia preservation nomination are 1145 17th Street NW (Edward Durell Stone Building), 1156 16th Street NW (Hubbard Memorial Hall and the Administration Building), and 1600 M Street NW (Skidmore, Owings & Merrill Building and plaza) [3]. A landscaped plaza connects the 17th and 16th Street buildings across the interior of the block [3].

The site lies in downtown Washington between the Dupont Circle and Farragut Square areas, and the campus is served by multiple Metrorail stations: Dupont Circle (Red Line), Farragut North (Red Line), and Farragut West (Orange, Silver, and Blue Lines), with an out‑of‑system “Farragut Crossing” transfer between the Red Line at Farragut North and the Orange/Silver/Blue Lines at Farragut West [6][7][8][9][22].

## Architecture and campus

The headquarters campus is composed of four principal buildings and a central plaza constructed over the 20th century to accommodate the Society’s growth [3][4].

- Hubbard Memorial Hall (1156 16th Street NW, 1904). Designed by Hornblower & Marshall, this two‑story Classical Revival building of buff brick with limestone detailing was the Society’s first purpose‑built headquarters [3]. Its hipped terra‑cotta roof, modillioned cornice, symmetrical 16th Street elevation, and carved “Gardiner Greene Hubbard Memorial” frieze reflect its commemorative origins; it stands as a contributing historic building on the campus [3].

- Administration Building (1156 16th Street NW, 1912–13; 1931 addition). Designed by Arthur B. Heaton, the Administration Building expanded the Society’s offices in a classical idiom complementary to Hubbard Hall, with a central pavilion articulated by Ionic columns and later additions that maintained materials continuity [3]. The interior includes a marble-finished lobby with original brass and stone detailing; the building is a contributing resource [3].

- Edward Durell Stone Building (1145 17th Street NW, 1961–63; dedicated 1964). Designed by Edward Durell Stone in a New Formalist expression, this ten‑story office and exhibition building is framed in reinforced concrete with white Vermont marble cladding, black Swedish granite, bronze detailing, and tinted glass [4]. A continuous marble‑clad elevated walkway wraps the ground floor, carried by square columns, while the upper façades are organized by rhythmic vertical marble fins and dark granite spandrels; a perforated, projecting roof slab creates a distinctive deep eave [4]. The first floor historically housed Explorers Hall, which in 1964 featured an 11‑foot globe suspended over a black granite reflecting pool [4].

- Skidmore, Owings & Merrill (SOM) Building and plaza (1600 M Street NW, 1984). Designed under David Childs, the M Street addition is a horizontally massed, stepped office structure whose interior included a two‑story rotunda and the roughly 400‑seat Gilbert H. Grosvenor Auditorium, together with library and news service spaces; it anchors the campus’s public entrance on M Street and adjoins the landscaped plaza [3]. The SOM building is treated as non‑contributing to the historic period of significance that ends in 1964, though it has been integral to public programming on the campus since the 1980s [3].

Landscape and recent design work. The plaza and campus gateways are being reimagined as part of the National Geographic Museum of Exploration project. The landscape plan by OLIN creates a new public gateway along M Street and a multi‑sensory courtyard environment with native and adapted plantings, enhanced pedestrian circulation across the campus, and outdoor media elements integrated into the visitor experience [12]. Interior workplace renovations designed by Hickok Cole have reorganized the Society’s offices around shared amenities, including a large second‑floor conference center (“The Passage”), creative production facilities, and new collaboration spaces; the headquarters renovation has been reported to achieve LEED Gold certification for interiors [11].

Sustainability and awards. As part of the museum redevelopment and campus transformation, the Society and its design team have publicly described sustainability measures such as bird‑safe glass, solar panels, and other features, with an aim to achieve a high LEED rating for the new public facilities; local news coverage has reported a target of LEED Platinum for the new visitor components [11][15][16].

## History

Early headquarters (1902–1931). The Society’s first permanent home was Hubbard Memorial Hall, whose cornerstone was laid in 1902 and which opened in 1904 at the southeast corner of 16th and M Streets NW [3]. Growing membership and activities led to construction of the adjacent Administration Building in 1912–13, with a major addition by Arthur B. Heaton in 1931 [3]. These buildings consolidated the Society’s administrative functions along 16th Street and framed a courtyard that later connected to expansion westward [3].

17th Street expansion and mid‑century building (1959–1964). Beginning in the interwar period and accelerating after World War II, the Society acquired parcels on the block’s west side facing 17th Street to accommodate expanding editorial and office needs [4]. A building permit for the Edward Durell Stone Building was issued on August 30, 1961; construction proceeded in 1961–63, with staff moving in late 1963 and formal dedication in January 1964 [4]. The dedication was attended by national dignitaries, reflecting the Society’s growing public profile [4]. Explorers Hall opened as a first‑floor exhibition venue in the new building, featuring a suspended 11‑foot globe and a reflecting pool [4].

M Street addition and auditorium (1984). To improve public access and consolidate functions, the Society completed a major addition at 1600 M Street NW in 1984, designed by Skidmore, Owings & Merrill. The project introduced a central rotunda, a landscaped plaza connecting to the 16th Street buildings, and the Gilbert H. Grosvenor Auditorium, which became a focal venue for lectures, screenings, and events [3].

Preservation recognition (2022–2023). On March 24, 2022, the D.C. Historic Preservation Review Board designated the National Geographic Society Headquarters complex, including Hubbard Hall (1904), the Administration Building (1912–13; 1931), and the Edward Durell Stone Building (1964), for listing on the D.C. Inventory of Historic Sites and recommended the complex for the National Register of Historic Places with a period of significance of 1904–1964 [5]. The property was subsequently listed on the National Register on June 5, 2023 [2].

Campus transformation and museum redevelopment (2022–2026). In 2022 the Society announced a comprehensive headquarters modernization and public‑space renovation, widely reported as a $250 million project to update the three‑building campus and reimagine its museum and event spaces [16]. On October 21, 2024, NGS announced the National Geographic Museum of Exploration, a new 100,000‑square‑foot public attraction on the headquarters campus scheduled to open in summer 2026, with immersive exhibitions, a state‑of‑the‑art theater, a new store and café, an education center, and outdoor nighttime media experiences [13][14][20]. Local coverage has emphasized the project’s sustainability goals and the redesign of the M Street entrance and plaza [12][15][16]. The redevelopment replaces the earlier National Geographic Museum, which operated in Explorers Hall and adjacent galleries and closed as part of the transformation in 2023 [14][21][38 in body refers to Blooloop but numbered [20]? — see Sources; the closure timing is supported in [14] and [21]; where necessary, the Museum of Exploration site notes the forthcoming opening].

## Facilities and public access

Historic public facilities. For decades the campus hosted public exhibitions, lectures, and film programs in Explorers Hall and the Grosvenor Auditorium. Explorers Hall opened with a dramatic suspended globe and reflecting pool in 1964 and subsequently hosted ticketed exhibitions; a National Geographic guide described the “National Geographic Museum at Explorers Hall” as open daily 10 a.m.–6 p.m. with ticketed galleries [4][33 in body; see numbering as [21] below]. The Gilbert H. Grosvenor Auditorium, located within the M Street building, is a roughly 400‑seat theater used for screenings, talks, and performances [3].

Current redevelopment and future museum. The National Geographic Museum of Exploration is scheduled to open in summer 2026 at 1600 M Street NW as part of the reimagined campus, with planned features including immersive exhibit environments, a renovated theater, a new store and café, a state‑of‑the‑art education center, public archives, an outdoor nighttime courtyard show, and a signature attraction called Rolex Explorers Landing [13][20]. The Society’s official events page notes that the museum is temporarily closed during the Base Camp transformation, and the museum website indicates that tickets for the Museum of Exploration will be offered closer to opening [21][20]. The landscape redesign integrates campus circulation and outdoor interpretation to create a new public gateway along M Street [12]. 

Workplace and campus amenities. Headquarters renovations by Hickok Cole have consolidated offices and introduced employee and visitor amenities, including the second‑floor “Passage” conference center, production facilities, and shared libraries and lounges, designed to reflect the organization’s brand and heritage and to support hybrid programming between staff and public‑facing spaces [11].

## Ownership and operations

The headquarters complex is owned by the National Geographic Society, a private nonprofit organization whose headquarters address is 1145 17th Street NW [1][3]. The complex accommodates the Society’s administrative, programmatic, and creative operations, with office modernization completed and public‑facing spaces under active redevelopment during 2024–2026 [11][13][16][23]. 

## Recognition and preservation

- District of Columbia Inventory of Historic Sites: The National Geographic Society Headquarters was designated on March 24, 2022, by the D.C. Historic Preservation Review Board, with a recommended period of significance from 1904 to 1964 [5].

- National Register of Historic Places: The property was listed on June 5, 2023, recognizing Hubbard Hall (1904), the Administration Building (1912–13; 1931), and the Edward Durell Stone Building (1964) as contributing buildings within the headquarters complex [2][3].

## Transportation

Metrorail access. The campus is within walking distance of three Metrorail stations: Dupont Circle (Red Line), Farragut North (Red Line), and Farragut West (Orange, Silver, and Blue Lines) [6][7][8][22]. WMATA’s “Farragut Crossing” provides an out‑of‑system transfer between Farragut North and Farragut West, facilitating movement between the Red Line and the Orange/Silver/Blue Lines near the campus [9].

## Notable events and cultural significance

The headquarters has hosted a wide range of public talks, performances, and exhibitions. National Public Radio broadcast programs from the Grosvenor Auditorium, including a December 12, 2011 Talk of the Nation broadcast on water resources and science‑focused programming in March 2012 [17][22]. The Washington Post regularly listed lectures and series such as National Geographic Live! at the auditorium, including science and cultural talks in 1992 and 2007 [25 in body; see numbering as [18] and [19] below]. The New York Times noted concerts presented there, including a 2008 performance by Italian singer‑songwriter Carmen Consoli [24]. Exhibitions at the museum have included the 2013 “Women of Vision” photography show and the 2019 “Queens of Egypt” exhibition, both covered by The Washington Post [19][18].
