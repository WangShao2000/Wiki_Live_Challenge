# Ring of Nestor

The Ring of Nestor is a large gold signet ring of Aegean Bronze Age type, first published and named by Sir Arthur Evans in 1925 and now in the Ashmolean Museum, Oxford (accession AN1938.1130). Evans attributed the ring to the Late Minoan I period and associated its alleged findspot with the Mycenaean tholos tombs at Kakovatos near Pylos (southwest Greece). The ring’s elaborate engraved imagery and its modern naming after the Homeric figure Nestor have attracted sustained scholarly attention. Its authenticity and exact origin have been much debated, with arguments both for and against ancient manufacture; the Ashmolean catalogues it as a signet ring and records its period as Late Minoan I, while recent scholarly assessments discuss technical features and iconographic comparanda that bear on the question of authenticity. The name “Ring of Nestor” is modern and conventional; the artifact is not directly connected to the Homeric hero and should not be confused with the Mycenaean gold vessel commonly called “Nestor’s Cup” from Grave Circle A at Mycenae. [21][8][4][36][12]

## Identification and naming

- The name “Ring of Nestor” originates with Arthur Evans, who published the object under that title in 1925 and interpreted its imagery as a window onto conceptions of the “Minoan after-world.” A contemporaneous review by V. Gordon Childe in Nature identifies the publication and title as Evans’s work. [8][4]
- The Ashmolean Museum’s image library records the object under accession AN1938.1130, with “Signet ring” as the preferred descriptive label and “The Ring of Nestor” noted as a popular name; the museum attributes the ring to the Late Minoan I period. [21]
- The association with the Homeric Nestor is modern and conventional; it reflects Evans’s naming rather than an ancient inscription or secure historical linkage. [8][4]

## Discovery and initial publication

- Evans reported that the ring was shown in Athens before he acquired it; he linked it to an alleged findspot at Kakovatos near Pylos and published it in 1925 as “The Ring of Nestor.” His detailed study appeared as a Macmillan monograph and in the Journal of Hellenic Studies. [8][5][13]
- Later summaries of the scholarly debate note claims that the ring was initially rejected as a modern forgery by a committee at the National Archaeological Museum in Athens, before Evans purchased and published it; the alleged findspot is a tholos (“beehive”) tomb at Kakovatos near Pylos. These details are discussed in modern analyses of the ring’s scholarly biography and authenticity debate. [27]

## Description

- Material and manufacture: The ring is gold, with technical analyses cited in recent scholarship indicating high gold purity (around 97%) and construction techniques consistent with ancient Aegean practice. Ultrasonic examination has been reported to confirm that the bezel is hollow, matching known Bronze Age signet ring construction. [28]
- Dimensions: Recent scholarly treatment records the bezel size at approximately 3.29 cm by 2.18 cm. [28]
- Form and technique: The piece is a signet ring—an engraved ring used to impress designs into soft materials—as part of Aegean glyptic (seal carving) traditions. The engraved bezel bears a complex composition organized by a central vertical element often interpreted as a trunk or pillar with a horizontal divider, separating distinct figural groupings; the hoop is decorated, and the ring shows features of ancient wear noted in scholarly discussions. [34][8][28]
- Iconography: Evans described multiple scenes and figures arranged around what he interpreted as a “world tree” motif, including:
  - A lion reclining on a platform with three supports visible.
  - A grouping with a griffin and approaching female figures.
  - Human pairs and processions, and insect forms (such as butterflies/chrysalises) that Evans connected to soul symbolism.
  Later analyses have proposed alternative readings of specific elements: for example, the platform beneath the lion has been reinterpreted as a construction resting on incurved altars, with comparanda from the Theran frescoes at Akrotiri (Xeste 3), rather than a mere three-legged table. [8][26]
- Inscriptions: There are no known inscriptions on the ring; its imagery is executed in engraved relief typical of Aegean glyptic. [8][34]

## Dating and cultural context

- Museum attribution and Evans’s dating place the ring in the Late Minoan I period (roughly the 17th–15th centuries BCE). This corresponds to a phase of intense interaction between Crete (Minoan civilization) and mainland Greece (emergent Mycenaean society), reflected in glyptic iconography and craftsmanship. [21][8]
- The ring’s engraved imagery—goddess-centered ritual scenes, animal guardians such as lions and griffins, and tree/pillar motifs—fits within the broader corpus of Aegean iconography known from seals, sealings, and frescoes. The Corpus der Minoischen und Mykenischen Siegel (CMS), which catalogues Aegean glyptic, includes the Ashmolean’s seal collection and discusses rings with similar technique and iconography; the Ring of Nestor is catalogued in CMS VI, with modern scholarship citing a CMS catalogue number for the piece (CMS VI no. 277). [36][26]
- Later archaeological finds, such as the four gold signet rings with Minoan-style ritual imagery from the “Griffin Warrior” tomb near the Palace of Nestor at Pylos (dated c. 1450 BCE), have expanded the comparanda for interpreting elite rings and their iconography in the early Mycenaean period. [6][7]

## Function and use

- As a signet ring, the object belongs to Aegean glyptic (the art of carving seals). Seals and signet rings could impress designs into clay or wax for administrative, ritual, or personal purposes; their iconography also communicated elite identities and affiliations. The CMS project outlines the systematic documentation of such seals and sealings across the Aegean Bronze Age. [34]
- Scholarly discussions of the Ring of Nestor have emphasized its elite status, elaborate iconography, and technical construction; wear noted in detailed studies has been cited as consistent with prolonged use. [28]

## Provenance and current location

- The ring is in the Ashmolean Museum (University of Oxford), accession number AN1938.1130; the museum’s image library records the object under the popular title “The Ring of Nestor” and dates it to Late Minoan I. [21]
- The ring entered the Ashmolean’s holdings by 1938 (as indicated by the accession number). A scholarly note records that the ring returned to public display in the Ashmolean in 1995, after a period off view. [21][31]

## Scholarship and debates

- Initial interpretation: Evans read the composition as an elaborate scene of admission to the realms of bliss in a Minoan “after-world,” drawing cross-cultural parallels to ancient eschatological motifs; Childe’s 1925 review summarized Evans’s framing. [8][4]
- Skepticism and alternative readings: Early and later scholars questioned various aspects of Evans’s iconographic interpretation and the ring’s authenticity. Martin P. Nilsson’s comprehensive study of Minoan–Mycenaean religion (1927) considered funerary and afterworld themes but later grouped certain artifacts among “suspect objects,” reflecting a growing awareness of modern forgeries in Aegean glyptic; other scholars criticized specific iconographic elements (e.g., the butterflies as soul symbols). [45][27]
- Forgery hypothesis: Nanno Marinatos and Briana Jackson argued in 2011 that the ring is a modern fabrication, possibly tailored to Evans’s expectations by drawing on Egyptian iconography and then “Minoanizing” it; they highlighted an unverifiable chain of transmission and suggested specific modern sources for elements in the design. Their article outlines this hypothesis and situates the “Ring of Nestor” within a wider modern forgery problem in Aegean glyptic. [23][24]
- Arguments for ancient manufacture: Technical and iconographic studies have presented evidence consistent with Bronze Age workmanship. Recent synthetic treatments report:
  - The ring’s gold purity (about 97%) and microscopic engraving/manufacture consistent with ancient techniques. [28]
  - Ultrasound examination confirming a hollow bezel construction characteristic of ancient signet rings. [28]
  - Iconographic parallels unavailable to a would‑be forger in the 1920s, such as platforms on incurved altars known from Akrotiri frescoes (published later), supporting the plausibility of the ring’s ancient iconographic logic. [26]
  - Comparative iconography on securely excavated early Mycenaean rings (e.g., the Griffin Warrior rings from Pylos) referencing goddess-centered rituals, bull symbolism, and elaborate multi-figure compositions, which add context for interpreting the Ashmolean ring’s complex imagery. [6][7]
- Synthetic assessments: Relational archaeology perspectives have traced how authenticity arguments evolve as methods and comparanda change, emphasizing that contested artifacts like the Ring of Nestor acquire scholarly biographies that themselves illuminate the discipline’s history; such studies collate and evaluate both skeptical and affirmative lines of evidence. [27]
- Cataloguing and reassessment: The Ashmolean’s Aegean seals were fully published in CMS VI (2009) with re-examination of doubtful items; CMS serves as a principal scholarly reference for Aegean glyptic, including rings such as this one. [36]

## Reception and legacy

- The ring’s publication and the subsequent debate have influenced discussions of Aegean Bronze Age art, iconography, and the modern problem of forgeries. Childe’s 1925 review placed Evans’s work within a broader acknowledgment of Minoan and Mycenaean roots of later Greek civilization. [4]
- The Ashmolean’s cataloguing and the CMS volumes’ comprehensive coverage have ensured the ring’s continued presence in scholarly reference works on Aegean glyptic. [21][36]
- The discovery of elite rings with rich figurative scenes in the Griffin Warrior tomb near the Palace of Nestor has broadened the baseline for interpreting early Mycenaean use of Minoan-style religious imagery in signet rings, often cited in discussions of comparative iconography that touch on the Ring of Nestor’s imagery. [6][7]

## Other uses and clarification of ambiguity

- The phrase “Ring of Nestor” in reliable literature primarily refers to the Ashmolean ring described above. Other notable artifacts with Homeric associations include:
  - Nestor’s Cup at Mycenae: a gold goblet found by Heinrich Schliemann in Grave Circle A (Shaft Grave IV) and named for its resemblance to Homer’s description. This is a separate, securely excavated artifact housed in the National Archaeological Museum in Athens. The cup should not be confused with the Ashmolean ring. [12]
- Modern scholarship and museum publications do not use “Ring of Nestor” for the four gold signet rings found in 2015 in the Griffin Warrior tomb at Pylos; those rings serve as comparanda for iconographic motifs but retain their own archaeological nomenclature and context. [6][7]

## Notes on terminology

- Signet ring: a finger ring with an engraved bezel used to impress a design into clay or wax (part of Aegean glyptic, i.e., the art of seal carving and sealing). [34]
- Glyptic: the domain of carved seals, sealings, and engraved rings in the Aegean Bronze Age; CMS is the principal systematic catalogue of such materials. [34]
