# Chapel of St. Cornelius the Centurion

The **Chapel of St. Cornelius the Centurion** is a historic Episcopal religious structure located on Governors Island in New York Harbor, New York City. It is a part of the parish of Trinity Church Wall Street in Lower Manhattan. Dedicated to St. Cornelius the Centurion, traditionally considered the first Gentile convert to Christianity, the current limestone edifice was consecrated in 1906. It replaced an earlier wooden chapel built on the site in 1847.

Designed by the architect Charles C. Haight in the English Gothic style, the chapel served for nearly a century as the primary place of worship for military personnel stationed at the post, including the United States Army and later the United States Coast Guard. Following the closure of the federal base on Governors Island in 1996, ownership of the property reverted to Trinity Church. In the 21st century, the building has ceased regular religious services and is utilized primarily as a venue for cultural events and public art installations.

## History

### Establishment and First Chapel (1844–1905)
In 1844, the Reverend John McVickar, a professor at Columbia College, was appointed as the post chaplain for Fort Columbus (later Fort Jay) on Governors Island [1]. At the time of his appointment, the military post lacked a dedicated religious facility, and services were conducted in administrative offices or outdoors depending on the weather [1][2].

McVickar initiated a campaign to construct a permanent house of worship for the garrison. Although the U.S. government provided the land—a plot approximately 150 feet square on the south side of the island—it did not appropriate funds for the construction [2]. McVickar secured donations from private citizens and the corporation of Trinity Church to fund the project [1][2].

The first chapel was a wooden structure designed by McVickar himself [1]. It was described as a "neat and tasteful church-like building" and cost approximately $2,500 to construct [2][3]. The building was consecrated on April 19, 1847, by Bishop William H. DeLancey of the Episcopal Diocese of Western New York [1][3]. It was named for Cornelius the Centurion, a Roman officer described in the Acts of the Apostles (Chapter 10) as the first Gentile to convert to the Christian faith [3][4].

### Integration with Trinity Parish
The relationship between the chapel and Trinity Church deepened in the mid-19th century. Between 1846 and 1855, Trinity Church provided an annual appropriation to support the chapel's operations [2]. In 1866, the War Department ceased funding for chaplains at Governors Island [5]. To ensure the continuation of religious services for the soldiers, Trinity Church offered to maintain a chaplain at its own expense, provided the Army agreed to place the chapel under the parish's control [5].

This arrangement was formalized in 1868, making the Chapel of St. Cornelius a "free chapel" within the parish of Trinity Church [2][5]. Under this agreement, the civilian chaplains appointed by Trinity were often designated as honorary vicars [5].

### Construction of the Current Chapel (1905–1906)
By the turn of the 20th century, the original 1847 wooden structure had fallen into disrepair and was condemned [1][2]. Trinity Church proposed replacing the deteriorating building with a new, permanent stone structure. The War Department accepted the offer in 1905 [2].

The new chapel was designed by Charles C. Haight, a prominent architect known for his collegiate and ecclesiastical buildings, including the campus of the General Theological Seminary and buildings at Yale University [6][7]. Haight had a personal connection to the military, having served as a captain in the Union Army during the American Civil War, and was the son of the Reverend Benjamin I. Haight, an assistant minister at Trinity Church [1][3].

The cornerstone for the new building was laid on October 27, 1905, by Bishop Coadjutor David H. Greer [1][3]. Construction proceeded rapidly, and the chapel was consecrated just one year later, on October 19, 1906, in a ceremony attended by military officials and Trinity clergy [1][2].

### Military Use (1906–1996)
Throughout the 20th century, the chapel served the spiritual needs of the garrison. In 1924, the agreement between the military and the church was modified; the Army assumed responsibility for the chaplain's salary, while Trinity Church continued to maintain the physical structure [5].

Following the transfer of Governors Island from the Army to the United States Coast Guard in 1966, the chapel continued to function as the post's religious center [2][5]. During the Coast Guard era, chaplains were supplied by the United States Navy Chaplain Corps, who served as honorary vicars under the existing agreement with Trinity [1].

In 1986, Trinity Church donated the chapel building to the United States government. The deed of gift included a reversion clause stipulating that if the government ever ceased to own the land or stopped using the building as a military chapel, ownership would revert to Trinity Church [5].

### Post-Military Era (1996–Present)
The Coast Guard ceased operations on Governors Island in 1996, closing the base [1][5]. In accordance with the 1986 agreement, ownership of the Chapel of St. Cornelius returned to Trinity Church [1][5].

Since the closure of the base, the chapel has not held regular religious services [1]. The island was opened to the public in 2005, and the chapel has subsequently been used as a venue for arts and cultural organizations [1]. In 2018, the chapel hosted *The Eclipse*, a major art installation by Jacob Hashimoto featuring thousands of rice paper kites suspended from the ceiling [8][9].

## Architecture

### Exterior
The Chapel of St. Cornelius the Centurion is constructed in the English Gothic style, specifically drawing influence from 14th-century designs [3][10]. The structure is cruciform in plan, measuring approximately 106 feet (32 meters) in length and 70 feet (21 meters) at its widest point [1][3].

The exterior walls are clad in coursed and rusticated buff Indiana limestone [1][2]. The roof is sheathed in slate and features copper finishings, including rain gutters [1]. A prominent feature of the design is the massive square tower located at the northwest corner of the building [1][2]. The tower contains the main entrance at the ground level and is capped by a parapet with four turrets [1].

### Interior
The interior features a nave, transepts, a chancel, and a side chapel dedicated to St. Alban [2][3]. The walls are finished in white stone [1]. The chancel is separated from the nave by the transept, and the clergy and choir sacristies are located on the south side of the chancel [2].

The chapel contains several memorials to former chaplains. Three sedilia (seats for clergy) are located in the sanctuary; the central one is dedicated to the founder, Reverend John McVickar, while the others memorialize vicars Davidson and La Tourette [1][3].

Historically, the chapel interior displayed significant military artifacts, including battle flags from the Mexican–American War and other campaigns, which were hung from the walls [1][2].

### Stained Glass
The building features 35 stained glass windows, many of which are lancet-shaped [1]. Notable among these is a window dedicated to the memory of Major General Winfield Scott Hancock, who commanded the Department of the East from Governors Island and ran for U.S. President in 1880 [6]. Another window depicts the Archangel Uriel [11].

### Organ
The chapel has housed several organs throughout its history. The original instrument for the 1906 building was built by the Hook & Hastings Company of Boston [2]. In 1954, this was replaced by a "Double Artiste" pipe organ manufactured by M.P. Möller, installed in a chamber on the right side of the chancel, though the facade of the original Hook & Hastings instrument was retained [2]. In the 1980s, the pipe organ was replaced by a two-manual electronic instrument built by Rodgers Instruments [2].
