# La Poupée (Bellmer)

**La Poupée** (English: *The Doll*; German: *Die Puppe*) refers to a multidisciplinary artistic project created by the German artist Hans Bellmer between the early 1930s and the late 1940s. The project encompasses the construction of articulated, life-sized female mannequins, as well as the subsequent production of photographs, drawings, and publications featuring these figures. Associated with the Surrealist movement, the work is noted for its exploration of the uncanny, the deconstruction of the human form, and the relationship between desire and violence [1], [2].

Bellmer began the project in Berlin in 1933, shortly after the Nazi Party rose to power. The work is frequently interpreted by art historians as an act of political defiance against the state's aesthetic ideals of the perfect body, as well as a psychological response to the artist's personal history [3], [4]. The project resulted in several key publications, including the privately printed *Die Puppe* (1934), the French edition *La Poupée* (1936), and the collaborative portfolio *Les Jeux de la poupée* (1949) with poet Paul Éluard [5], [6].

## Background and Inspiration

### Personal and Psychological Factors
Hans Bellmer's creation of the doll is often attributed to a convergence of personal events and psychological motivations. Born in Kattowitz (now Katowice, Poland) in 1902, Bellmer had a strained relationship with his father, a strict authority figure. Bellmer later described his artistic rebellion as a rejection of paternal authority [7], [8].

In the early 1930s, Bellmer's mother sent him a crate of his childhood toys, which reportedly reconnected him with the objects of his youth [6]. Around this time, he also became infatuated with his fifteen-year-old cousin, Ursula Naguschewski, who moved to Berlin in 1932. This unrequited attraction is cited by biographers as a primary catalyst for the construction of an "artificial girl" [9], [7].

### Cultural Influences
Bellmer was influenced by the literary and artistic climate of the era. He was inspired by a performance of Jacques Offenbach's opera *The Tales of Hoffmann*, which he attended in 1932. The opera features the character Olympia, a mechanical doll with whom the protagonist falls in love [1], [10]. Bellmer was also aware of the work of Oskar Kokoschka, specifically the Austrian artist's commission of a life-sized doll in 1918 intended to replace a former lover; Kokoschka's letters regarding this project were published as *Der Fetisch* in 1925 [7], [11]. Additionally, Bellmer had encountered the work of dollmaker Lotte Pritzel [6].

### Political Context
The project began in 1933, coinciding with the establishment of the Nazi regime in Germany. Bellmer declared that he would not create work that supported the state, renouncing his employment in commercial advertising [12], [8]. Art historians such as Hal Foster and Sue Taylor have interpreted the doll's mutated and fragmented form as a direct opposition to the fascist neoclassical ideal of the healthy, athletic Aryan body promoted by the Nazi government [1], [4].

## The First Doll (1933–1934)

### Construction
Bellmer constructed the first doll in 1933 with the assistance of his brother, Fritz, who was an engineer [1]. This initial figure was a static, mannequin-like structure. It consisted of a molded torso made from flax fiber, glue, and plaster, built over a wood and metal skeleton [1], [10]. The head was mask-like, featuring glass eyes and a disheveled wig. The legs were constructed from broomsticks or dowel rods, with one leg encased in plaster to resemble a realistic limb while the other remained a wooden stick [1], [7]. The figure did not have arms, although a loose wooden hand sometimes appeared in the compositions [11].

### *Die Puppe* (1934)
Bellmer photographed the doll in various staged "tableaux vivants" within his parents' apartment. In 1934, he privately published a small book titled *Die Puppe* (The Doll) in Karlsruhe [13], [14]. The publication contained ten black-and-white gelatin silver prints pasted onto yellow cardboard, accompanied by a text written by Bellmer titled "Erinnerungen zum Thema Puppe" (Memories on the Theme of the Doll) [14]. The edition was limited and produced anonymously to avoid attention from Nazi authorities, who later labeled his work "degenerate art" [2], [7].

### Reception by Surrealists
Photographs of the first doll were sent to Paris, where Bellmer's cousin Ursula showed them to André Breton, the leader of the Surrealist movement [6]. The images were received enthusiastically by the group, who viewed the doll as a quintessential Surrealist object embodying the uncanny and subversive sexuality [9]. Eighteen of these photographs were published in the December 1934 issue of the Surrealist journal *Minotaure*, marking Bellmer's introduction to the international art world [1], [9].

## The Second Doll (1935–1938)

### Construction and Articulation
Dissatisfied with the rigidity of the first model, Bellmer began constructing a second doll in 1935 [1]. This version was designed to be fully articulated, influenced by a pair of 16th-century wooden dolls with ball joints that Bellmer had observed at the Kaiser-Friedrich-Museum in Berlin [7], [11].

The second doll was constructed around a central spherical joint representing the abdomen (sometimes described as the stomach). This design allowed for a high degree of flexibility and anatomical reconfiguration [1], [15]. The figure included two pelvises, multiple sets of legs, and four spherical breasts, allowing the body to be assembled in non-naturalistic ways, such as a torso consisting of two pelvises or a pair of legs attached directly to a head [15], [16].

### *La Poupée* (1936)
In 1936, a French edition of Bellmer's work was published in Paris by Éditions GLM under the title *La Poupée* [6]. This edition included a translation of Bellmer's text by Robert Valençay and featured ten mounted gelatin silver photographs of the doll [6], [17]. The publication coincided with the inclusion of Bellmer's work in the *Exposición Surrealista* in Tenerife and a special issue of *Cahiers d'art* devoted to Surrealist objects [6], [2].

## *Les Jeux de la poupée* (1949)

Following the death of his wife Margarete in 1938, Bellmer moved to Paris [7]. Before leaving Germany, he had continued to photograph the second doll in various settings, including domestic interiors and forests [18].

In Paris, Bellmer collaborated with the poet Paul Éluard on a new publication titled *Les Jeux de la poupée* (The Games of the Doll). Éluard wrote fourteen prose poems in 1938 to accompany a selection of Bellmer's hand-colored photographs [19], [20]. The project was interrupted by the outbreak of World War II, during which Bellmer was interned as an enemy alien at the Camp des Milles [10], [11].

The book was finally published in 1949 by Éditions Premières [5]. It was released in a limited edition of roughly 136 to 142 copies [5], [19]. The volume contained fifteen hand-colored gelatin silver prints mounted on cardstock. The photographs in this series are noted for their use of aniline dyes to add unnatural hues—such as pinks and yellows—to the black-and-white prints, enhancing the artificial and dreamlike quality of the images [19], [20].

## Themes and Analysis

### The Uncanny and Surrealism
Bellmer's dolls are frequently discussed in relation to Sigmund Freud's concept of the "uncanny" (*das Unheimliche*), which describes the cognitive dissonance caused by objects that are simultaneously familiar and strange, such as lifelike automatons [2], [21]. The Surrealists embraced the doll for its ability to blur the line between the animate and inanimate [15].

### The Body as Anagram
Bellmer described the body as being like a sentence that could be dismantled and rearranged into an anagram [2], [15]. By using ball joints and interchangeable parts, he sought to liberate the anatomy from its physical limitations and functional logic. This "physical anagram" allowed for the expression of unconscious desires and the visualization of the "physics of passion" [15], [2].

### The Gaze and Fetishism
Scholars have analyzed the doll series through the lens of psychoanalytic theory, focusing on scopophilia (the pleasure of looking) and fetishism. The dolls are often posed to obscure their eyes or are headless, which some critics, such as Sue Taylor, argue serves to subvert the female gaze, leaving the figure as a passive object of the artist's vision [1]. Others view the fragmentation of the body as a representation of castration anxiety or a mechanism to master trauma through the repetition of dismantling and reassembling the form [22], [23].

## Legacy and Collections

Hans Bellmer's *La Poupée* remains a significant work within the history of Surrealism and photography. The original dolls have not survived; the first doll was made of ephemeral materials, and the second was eventually discarded or lost, though parts may have been reused [7], [11]. The work exists primarily through the photographic record and the published books.

Original prints and editions of *Die Puppe* and *Les Jeux de la poupée* are held in major museum collections, including:
*   The Museum of Modern Art (MoMA), New York [9]
*   The Tate, London [23]
*   The Centre Pompidou, Paris [7]
*   The Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York [21]
*   The Art Institute of Chicago [12]
*   The J. Paul Getty Museum, Los Angeles [24]
