# Centre for Minority Rights Development (Kenya) and Minority Rights Group International on behalf of Endorois Welfare Council v. Kenya

**Centre for Minority Rights Development (Kenya) and Minority Rights Group International on behalf of Endorois Welfare Council v. Kenya** (Communication 276/2003), commonly known as the **Endorois case**, is a landmark decision issued by the African Commission on Human and Peoples' Rights (ACHPR) in 2010 [1][2]. The ruling addressed the expulsion of the Endorois people, an indigenous pastoralist community, from their ancestral lands around Lake Bogoria in Kenya to create a game reserve [1][3].

The decision is legally significant for two primary reasons: it was the first time the African Commission recognized the existence of indigenous peoples in Africa with rights over traditionally owned land, and it was the first ruling by an international tribunal to find a violation of the right to development [3][4]. The Commission found the Government of Kenya in violation of the African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights, specifically regarding the rights to religious practice, property, culture, the free disposition of natural resources, and development [4][5]. Despite the ruling being adopted by the African Union in 2010, implementation of the Commission's recommendations remains limited as of 2025 [6][7].

## Background

### The Endorois People
The Endorois are a semi-nomadic, pastoralist indigenous community of approximately 60,000 people who have historically inhabited the Lake Bogoria area in the Rift Valley Province of Kenya [1][8]. They are often classified as a sub-group of the Tugen, who are part of the larger Kalenjin ethnic group, though they maintain a distinct cultural identity [9][10]. Their livelihood relies heavily on livestock herding, and their culture is inextricably linked to the land surrounding Lake Bogoria, which they consider sacred [1][11]. The community utilizes the lake's hot springs for religious ceremonies and the surrounding areas for grazing and traditional herbal medicine [4][11].

### Eviction and Domestic Litigation
In 1973, the Kenyan government established the Lake Hannington Game Reserve (renamed Lake Bogoria Game Reserve in 1978) [1][9]. To facilitate this, the government evicted the Endorois community from their ancestral lands [4][8]. The eviction process began in the 1970s and continued into the 1980s [2][9].

The Endorois alleged that these evictions occurred without proper prior consultation or adequate compensation [1][12]. The displacement forced the community into semi-arid areas, resulting in the loss of livestock and disrupting their traditional way of life [3][7]. The community was also denied access to the game reserve for grazing or religious purposes, except at the discretion of the Game Reserve Authority [4].

In the 1990s, the Endorois Welfare Council (EWC) attempted to seek redress through the Kenyan domestic legal system [2][9]. They filed a case in the High Court of Kenya, arguing that the eviction was illegal and violated their constitutional rights. However, the High Court dismissed the case in 2002 [2][9]. The court ruled that the land was "Trust Land" managed by the County Council for the benefit of the residents, but that the creation of the game reserve superseded the community's customary rights [2][9]. The court further stated that Kenyan law did not recognize collective land rights based on cultural claims [2]. Following the exhaustion of domestic remedies, the Centre for Minority Rights Development (CEMIRIDE) and Minority Rights Group International (MRG) filed a complaint with the ACHPR on behalf of the Endorois in 2003 [1][12].

## Proceedings before the African Commission

The communication (complaint) was declared admissible by the African Commission in 2005 [2]. The complainants argued that the expulsion of the Endorois from their land violated the African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights, specifically:
*   **Article 8:** Right to practice religion.
*   **Article 14:** Right to property.
*   **Article 17:** Right to culture.
*   **Article 21:** Right to free disposition of natural resources.
*   **Article 22:** Right to development [5][13].

The Centre on Housing Rights and Evictions (COHRE) submitted an *amicus curiae* brief in support of the Endorois [1][13].

The Respondent State (Kenya) argued that the Endorois were not a distinct indigenous group but part of the larger Kalenjin community, and therefore not entitled to special protection as a "people" under the Charter [3][13]. Kenya further contended that the creation of the game reserve was for the broader public interest, specifically tourism and wildlife conservation, and that the Trust Land system provided adequate legal administration for the land [2][13].

## Judgment

In November 2009, during its 46th Ordinary Session, the African Commission issued its ruling, finding the Government of Kenya in violation of Articles 1, 8, 14, 17, 21, and 22 of the African Charter [5]. The decision was formally adopted by the African Union Heads of State Summit in February 2010 [7][14].

### Recognition of Indigenous Status
A central aspect of the ruling was the Commission's determination that the Endorois constitute a distinct "people" and an indigenous community [2][3]. The Commission applied criteria including self-identification, distinct cultural patterns, and a special attachment to ancestral land [15]. This rejected the Kenyan government's argument that all Africans are indigenous and that the Endorois were merely a sub-group without distinct collective rights [3]. This finding established a precedent for the protection of indigenous rights within the African human rights system [4][6].

### Right to Property (Article 14)
The Commission ruled that the Endorois' traditional possession of the land was equivalent to state-granted full property title [10][15]. It found that the "Trust Land" system, which allowed local councils to hold land on behalf of residents, had failed to protect the community's rights [4]. The Commission stated that the encroachment on Endorois land was disproportionate to the public need for a game reserve and that the community had not been adequately compensated [3][12].

### Right to Development (Article 22)
The *Endorois case* is widely cited as the first international jurisprudence to find a violation of the Right to Development [2][3][16]. The Commission established that development processes must include the "active, free and meaningful participation" of the affected community [13][15]. It ruled that the Kenyan government failed to obtain the "free, prior, and informed consent" (FPIC) of the Endorois before evicting them [2][15]. Furthermore, the Commission noted that development should result in the improvement of the community's well-being, whereas the eviction had led to a decline in their welfare [16].

### Religious and Cultural Rights (Articles 8 and 17)
The ruling recognized that the Endorois' spiritual beliefs and ceremonial practices constituted a religion protected under the Charter [10][17]. By denying the community unrestricted access to Lake Bogoria (a sacred site) and surrounding areas for religious rituals, the state had violated their freedom of religion [4][17]. The Commission dismissed the state's argument that access could be regulated or restricted for conservation purposes, stating that the Endorois' presence was not incompatible with conservation [17].

### Recommendations
The African Commission issued several recommendations to the Government of Kenya to remedy the violations:
1.  **Restitution:** Recognize the Endorois' rights of ownership and return their ancestral land [5].
2.  **Access:** Ensure unrestricted access to Lake Bogoria and surrounding sites for religious rites and grazing [5].
3.  **Compensation:** Pay adequate compensation for all losses suffered [5].
4.  **Royalties:** Pay royalties from existing economic activities (tourism and mining) and ensure employment opportunities within the reserve [5].
5.  **Registration:** Grant registration to the Endorois Welfare Council [5].
6.  **Dialogue:** Engage in dialogue with the complainants to implement the recommendations [5].

## Implementation

Following the 2010 ruling, implementation of the Commission's recommendations has been slow and contentious.

### Initial Response and Task Force
The Kenyan government initially expressed a willingness to comply, and the Endorois Welfare Council was successfully registered, fulfilling one of the recommendations [7][18]. In 2014, the government established a task force with a one-year mandate to study the decision and guide its implementation [7][19]. However, the task force's term expired without the release of a public report or significant progress on the ground [7][20].

### Status as of 2024–2025
As of 2025, the core recommendations regarding land restitution and compensation remain largely unimplemented [6][7].
*   **Land Rights:** The Endorois have not been granted legal title to their ancestral lands, and the community continues to face restrictions on access [2][6].
*   **Compensation:** No comprehensive compensation package has been provided for the historical losses [6][14].
*   **Continued Displacement:** Reports indicate that members of the Endorois community have faced further displacement risks from related areas, such as the Mochongoi Forest and Mukutani Forest, due to security operations and conservation measures [9].

In February 2024, the Endorois Welfare Council, alongside the Ogiek community (who won a similar case at the African Court on Human and Peoples' Rights), delivered call-to-action letters to the Kenyan Attorney General demanding immediate implementation of the rulings [7][14]. Civil society organizations, including Minority Rights Group International and ESCR-Net, continue to advocate for the government to honor its obligations under the African Charter [7][19].

## Significance
The *Endorois case* is considered a cornerstone of indigenous rights jurisprudence in Africa [3][21]. It clarified the definition of "peoples" under the African Charter to explicitly include indigenous populations, distinguishing them from the broader population of a state [2][15].

The case also set a global precedent regarding the Right to Development. By linking development to the requirement of "free, prior, and informed consent," the Commission established that state development projects cannot legally proceed at the expense of the fundamental rights and well-being of indigenous communities [15][16]. The ruling has influenced subsequent litigation, including the *Ogiek case* (*African Commission on Human and Peoples' Rights v. Kenya*), where the African Court relied on principles established in the Endorois decision [2][7].
