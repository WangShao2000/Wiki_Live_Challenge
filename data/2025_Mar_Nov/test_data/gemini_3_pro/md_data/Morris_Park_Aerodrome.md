# Morris Park Aerodrome

The **Morris Park Aerodrome** was a pioneering aviation facility located in the Bronx, New York, operational from 1908 to 1910. Established on the grounds of the defunct Morris Park Racecourse, it was the first airfield in the United States specifically designated for the development and exhibition of heavier-than-air flying machines [1]. The aerodrome was established and operated by the Aeronautical Society of New York (ASNY), an organization formed to promote practical experimentation in aviation [2].

During its brief existence, the aerodrome hosted the first public air meet in the United States and was the site of the first airplane flight over New York City [2][3]. It served as a testing ground for early aviation innovators, including Glenn Curtiss, who demonstrated and sold his *Golden Flyer* biplane at the site—a transaction regarded as the first commercial sale of an airplane in the U.S. [4][5]. The facility closed in 1910 following a fire and the subsequent auctioning of the land for residential development, after which the Aeronautical Society relocated its operations to Long Island [6][7].

## History

### Origins and Establishment
The site of the aerodrome was originally the Morris Park Racecourse, a thoroughbred horse racing track built by John Albert Morris in 1889 [6]. The racetrack, which hosted major events such as the Belmont Stakes and the Preakness Stakes, ceased racing operations in 1904 [6][7]. Following the closure of the track, the City of New York acquired the property in 1907 [7].

In 1908, the newly formed Aeronautical Society of New York sought a location to conduct practical flight experiments. The Society, which included members dissatisfied with the lack of practical testing facilities provided by other organizations, leased the idle racetrack from the city [2]. The site was selected for its open spaces, although modifications were required to make it suitable for aviation; organizers removed fences surrounding the old track to create a straightway of approximately one kilometer to facilitate takeoffs and landings [2].

### Facilities and Operations
The Morris Park Aerodrome functioned as an "open-air laboratory" for aviation enthusiasts and inventors [8]. The Society utilized the existing infrastructure of the racetrack, including the grandstands and stables, which were converted into hangars and workshops for the construction of aircraft [9].

Because the field lacked a significant hill to assist with glider launches, the Society constructed a tower to provide initial impetus for gravity-powered craft [8]. The grounds were used for testing a wide variety of experimental machines, including gliders, ornithopters, and "wind wagons"—vehicles powered by aerial propellers intended to test engine efficiency and propeller design [10].

## Major Exhibitions

### Election Day Air Meet (1908)
On November 3, 1908, the Aeronautical Society hosted a major exhibition at the aerodrome, which is cited by some historians as the first public air meet in the United States [11][10]. The event attracted a crowd estimated at 20,000 spectators, who paid an entrance fee to witness the display of aviation technology [10].

The program included competitions for gliders, kite flying, and demonstrations of wind wagons [10]. A highlight of the event was the flight of 16-year-old Laurence Lesh, a glider pilot. Lesh's glider was towed by a Thomas Flyer automobile, a vehicle famous for winning the 1908 New York to Paris Race [10]. During one of the flights, Lesh crashed and suffered a broken ankle, an incident that underscored the dangers of early aviation experimentation [10].

The event was attended by Rudolph Dirks, the cartoonist known for *The Katzenjammer Kids*. Inspired by the scene of the crowds and the flying machines, Dirks painted an oil work titled *The Fledglings*, which depicted the atmosphere of the meet [10][10].

### Exhibition of June 1909
A second significant exhibition took place in June 1909. This event featured Glenn Curtiss, a prominent aviation pioneer and engine manufacturer. Curtiss piloted his biplane, the *Golden Flyer* (also known as the *Gold Bug*), at the aerodrome [9].

On June 26, 1909, Curtiss flew the *Golden Flyer* in a demonstration that marked the first airplane flight over the city of New York [3]. During this period, the Aeronautical Society of New York purchased the *Golden Flyer* from Curtiss for $5,000 [4]. This transaction is historically significant as the first sale of a commercial airplane in the United States [5]. The purchase agreement included a provision for Curtiss to instruct two members of the Society in piloting the aircraft [8].

The exhibition also served as a venue for the ongoing "patent wars" between early aviators. Following the publicity of the *Golden Flyer* at Morris Park, Wilbur Wright filed a patent-infringement lawsuit against the Aeronautical Society to prevent them from exhibiting the plane, escalating the legal conflict between the Wright brothers and the Curtiss-Herring interests [4].

## Closure and Legacy

The Morris Park Aerodrome had a short operational life. By 1910, the property was slated for redevelopment. A fire in 1910 destroyed some of the structures on the grounds, and the land was subsequently divided into over 3,000 lots and auctioned for residential use [6][7].

Following the closure of the Morris Park facility, the Aeronautical Society of New York moved its operations to the Hempstead Plains near Mineola on Long Island [12]. This new location, which offered more open space and fewer obstructions than the Bronx racecourse, eventually developed into Roosevelt Field, a major hub for American aviation in the early 20th century [1][13].

Today, the site of the former aerodrome is a residential neighborhood known as Morris Park. The history of the site is commemorated by local landmarks, such as Loreto Playground, which sits on a portion of the former racetrack grounds [7]. Local historians and organizations, including the Morris Park Business Improvement District, have sought to honor the neighborhood's aviation heritage through murals and proposed historical markers [6][9][2].
