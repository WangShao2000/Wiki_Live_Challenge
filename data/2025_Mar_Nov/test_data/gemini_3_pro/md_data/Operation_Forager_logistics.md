# Operation Forager logistics

**Operation Forager logistics** refers to the complex planning, transportation, supply, and engineering support operations required to sustain the United States military's invasion of the Mariana Islands (Saipan, Tinian, and Guam) between June and August 1944 during World War II. The campaign, executed by the United States Fifth Fleet and the V Amphibious Corps, represented the largest amphibious operation in the Pacific theater up to that date [1]. The operation was characterized by the immense distances involved—staging occurred over 1,000 miles (1,600 km) away at Eniwetok and 3,500 miles (5,600 km) from Pearl Harbor—and the requirement to sustain hundreds of thousands of personnel and hundreds of ships at sea for months without a large, established land base nearby [2].

Key logistical challenges included a chronic shortage of shipping tonnage, the need to transfer cargo across coral reefs, and the disruption of supply lines caused by the Japanese fleet's intervention during the Battle of the Philippine Sea [3], [1]. The operation saw significant logistical innovations, including the extensive use of Service Squadron 10 (ServRon 10) as a mobile floating base to repair and resupply the fleet at sea, and the widespread adoption of palletized cargo to expedite unloading [1], [1]. The successful logistical support of Operation Forager allowed for the seizure of the Marianas, which were subsequently developed by Navy Seabees and Army engineers into massive airbases for B-29 Superfortress strategic bombing campaigns against the Japanese home islands [4], [5].

## Background and planning

The strategic objective of the Mariana Islands campaign was to breach the Japanese inner defense ring and secure airfields that would place Tokyo within the range of the new B-29 Superfortress bombers [2]. Additionally, the capture of the islands was intended to sever Japanese lines of communication to the south and draw the Imperial Japanese Navy into a decisive engagement [6].

### Distance and staging
The logistical requirements for Operation Forager were unprecedented in the Central Pacific. Planners were tasked with transporting and landing three divisions of men and equipment on Saipan, followed by forces for Guam and Tinian [2]. The primary staging areas were Pearl Harbor in Hawaii and Guadalcanal in the Solomon Islands. The distance from Pearl Harbor to Saipan is approximately 3,500 miles (5,600 km), while the forward staging base at Eniwetok in the Marshall Islands—captured only months prior in February 1944—was over 1,000 miles (1,600 km) away [2], [7].

Because base facilities in Hawaii were insufficient to handle the volume of supplies required for mounting simultaneous operations against Saipan and Guam, direct resupply from the United States West Coast was necessary [1]. The capture of Eniwetok allowed for the forward marshaling of the Joint Expeditionary Forces, where troops and supplies could be staged prior to the final movement to the Marianas [4].

### Shipping shortages
A critical constraint during the planning phase was the availability of shipping. A staff study by Captain John F. Rees, chief of the transportation section for Admiral Chester W. Nimitz, estimated that the operation would require 120 cargo ships and 58 troop transports. However, in April 1944, only 18 cargo ships and 22 transports were available [1].

Ultimately, 110 transport vessels were marshaled for the invasion of Saipan, including 37 attack transports (APA), 11 attack cargo ships (AKA), 47 tank landing ships (LST), and various other auxiliary vessels [1]. The shortage of cargo space forced commanders to leave significant amounts of unit equipment and vehicles behind. In some instances, units were transported on different ships than their equipment, complicating the reorganization of forces upon landing [1].

## Fleet logistics and Service Squadron 10

To sustain the Fifth Fleet and the expeditionary forces at sea for extended periods, the U.S. Navy relied heavily on **Service Squadron 10** (ServRon 10), commanded by Commodore Worrall Reed Carter [1]. This unit functioned as a mobile, floating naval base, providing fuel, ammunition, food, water, and repairs to the combatant ships [8].

### The floating base concept
Unlike previous campaigns where fleets returned to major bases like Pearl Harbor for resupply and repair, the distance of the Marianas necessitated that the base come to the fleet. Service Squadron 10 operated primarily out of Eniwetok during the early phases of the operation [1]. The squadron included repair ships (*Vestal*, *Ajax*, *Hector*), destroyer tenders (*Piedmont*, *Cascade*), floating dry docks, and barges for fuel and water [1].

This mobile logistics capability allowed the fleet to remain at sea for over four months. When the fleet moved forward, ServRon 10 displaced forward as well, eventually establishing a major anchorage at Ulithi later in the war [9]. The squadron's ability to repair battle damage forward was critical; for example, the repair ships at Eniwetok addressed damage sustained by warships during the campaign [1].

### Refueling at sea
Fuel logistics were managed by task units consisting of oilers escorted by destroyers. Eleven designated refueling areas were established for Operation Forager, each named after a major oil company [1]. Ships would detach from their task groups to rendezvous with oilers in these areas.

During the campaign, the Imperial Japanese Navy targeted U.S. logistical ships for the first time in the Central Pacific. On June 18, 1944, Japanese aircraft attacked a refueling group, damaging the oilers USS *Saranac*, USS *Neshanic*, and USS *Saugatuck*. *Saranac* suffered heavy casualties and was forced to return to the U.S. for repairs, while the other two were repaired by ServRon 10 at Eniwetok [1].

### Ammunition and provisions
The intensity of the fighting ashore and the naval engagements at sea resulted in ammunition consumption rates that far exceeded planning estimates. The fleet required "prodigious" amounts of naval gunfire support ammunition [1]. Most resupply ships were not "vertically loaded" (a method where all types of supplies are accessible without moving others), which made retrieving specific types of urgently needed ammunition difficult [1].

To address critical shortages, the ammunition ship USS *Mazama* was ordered to move forward to the transport area off Saipan while the battle was still raging. This was considered a high-risk maneuver due to daily Japanese air attacks. Between June 21 and July 7, *Mazama* issued over 3,400 tons of ammunition to the fleet [1].

Provisions were also a challenge. With the fleet at sea for months, fresh food ran low. General stores issue ships were employed to replenish thousands of items, ranging from food to toilet paper [1]. Refrigerated cargo ships carried fresh produce, but because they were not organized for selective discharge and Saipan lacked warehouse facilities until August, distribution was inefficient. Despite this, the Service Force managed to supply fresh produce to combatant ships five days out of six [1].

## Amphibious assault logistics

The landings on Saipan, Guam, and Tinian presented distinct hydrographic and tactical challenges that required specific logistical solutions.

### Saipan
The invasion of Saipan began on June 15, 1944 (D-Day). The logistical plan involved landing 20,000 combat troops by sunset on the first day [2].

**Beach conditions and supply buildup:**
Supplies were initially dumped on the beaches by Landing Vehicles, Tracked (LVTs) and Landing Craft, Tank (LCTs). However, the beaches were under Japanese artillery and mortar fire for the first four days, frequently halting unloading operations [1]. Congestion became a severe problem; supplies intended for the 2nd Marine Division were sometimes landed on 4th Marine Division beaches, and the arrival of the reserve 27th Infantry Division further complicated the situation as no specific landing plans or unloading areas had been designated for them [3].

**Impact of the Battle of the Philippine Sea:**
On June 16, Admiral Raymond Spruance received intelligence that the Japanese fleet was approaching. To protect the transports, they were ordered to withdraw to the east, away from the island [10]. This withdrawal suspended unloading operations for extended periods during the critical early days of the invasion, causing shortages of food, water, and ammunition for the troops ashore [3]. The 27th Infantry Division, having arrived late, had little time to unload equipment before the transports departed [3].

**Water shortages:**
Potable water was in critically short supply during the first week. Troops relied on captured Japanese cisterns or water transported ashore. One veteran recalled that the water available often tasted like gasoline [11].

### Guam
The invasion of Guam was originally scheduled for June 18 but was postponed to July 21 due to the extended battle on Saipan and the naval engagement in the Philippine Sea [12].

**Reef transfer operations:**
Guam is surrounded by a wide coral reef that prevented standard landing craft from reaching the beach. Logistics planners had to implement a transfer system where cargo was moved from landing craft to LVTs or DUKWs (amphibious trucks) at the reef line to be ferried to the shore [1], [12].

**Equipment shortages:**
The delay in the Guam operation allowed for the inclusion of the Army's 77th Infantry Division. However, the 77th lacked sufficient LVTs for its landing. Consequently, many soldiers had to wade ashore from the reef edge, and the division relied heavily on DUKWs for supply [13], [14]. The loss of LVTs during the initial assaults hampered the logistics chain, delaying the buildup of supplies ashore [12].

### Tinian
The invasion of Tinian (July 24, 1944) was a "shore-to-shore" operation, with forces and supplies moving directly from Saipan, which is located only a few miles away [12]. This proximity allowed U.S. forces to use Saipan as a forward logistics base and fire support base, with artillery on Saipan firing across the channel to support the Tinian landings [15]. The operation is often cited as the "perfect amphibious operation" due to the effective application of lessons learned from Saipan and the logistical advantages of the nearby base [12].

## Cargo handling innovations

### Palletization
Operation Forager marked a significant shift in cargo handling methods with the widespread use of **wooden pallets**. Before this, cargo was often handled "break-bulk" (piece by piece). The V Amphibious Corps ordered that 25 to 50 percent of all supplies and significant portions of ammunition be loaded on pallets [1].

Palletization allowed for the use of forklifts and cranes to move larger units of supply, drastically reducing the time required to unload ships. A ship that might take days to unload using traditional methods could be turned around much faster, mitigating the critical shortage of shipping bottoms [16]. However, not all ships were equipped to handle pallets efficiently, and space constraints on some vessels limited their use [1].

### LVT and DUKW usage
The campaign demonstrated the essential role of amphibious vehicles not just for assault, but for logistics. The LVT (Landing Vehicle, Tracked) was crucial for crossing the coral reefs prevalent in the Marianas. The DUKW (amphibious truck) also proved vital, particularly on Guam where they were used to ferry artillery and supplies from the reef to inland dumps [1]. When a typhoon struck Guam on July 29, destroying causeways and grounding landing craft, DUKWs were the only vehicles capable of navigating the rough seas to keep supplies flowing [1].

## Medical logistics

The medical support system for Operation Forager involved a multi-layered chain of evacuation utilizing field hospitals, hospital ships, and air evacuation.

### Casualties and treatment
The campaign resulted in high casualties; the U.S. suffered approximately 26,000 casualties on Saipan alone [2]. Medical supplies were carried in general stores ships, and assault troops carried 30 days' worth of medical supplies [1].

### Hospital ships
Four dedicated hospital ships supported the operation: USS *Relief*, USS *Solace*, USS *Bountiful*, and USS *Samaritan* [1]. These ships arrived off the beaches to take on casualties directly from landing craft or via transfer boats. For example, *Solace* and *Bountiful* arrived off Saipan on June 18 and quickly filled to capacity, departing for rear-area hospitals in the Solomons or Marshalls [1]. Additionally, three LSTs were converted into makeshift hospital ships to handle the overflow of wounded during the initial days of the invasion [1].

### Air evacuation
Operation Forager saw the extensive use of air evacuation for casualties. Seaplanes (PBM Mariners) from rescue squadrons landed in the lagoons to pick up urgent cases. Once Isely Field (formerly Aslito Airfield) on Saipan was secured and repaired, transport planes began evacuating wounded personnel to the Marshall Islands and Hawaii [1]. Between June 24 and the end of the operation, thousands of casualties were evacuated by air [1].

## Construction and base development

Following the combat phase, the logistical focus shifted to transforming the Marianas into a massive forward base for the strategic bombing of Japan. This effort was led by the **Naval Construction Battalions (Seabees)** and Army engineers.

### Airfield construction
*   **Saipan:** Seabees landed with the assault waves. Aslito Airfield was captured on June 18 and renamed Isely Field. Seabees repaired the bomb-damaged runways, filling craters with crushed coral and laying Marston matting. By June 22, the field was operational for U.S. aircraft [17], [1].
*   **Tinian:** Following the capture of Tinian, 15,000 Seabees constructed the world's largest airbase complex at the time. This included **North Field** and **West Field**. The construction involved blasting and moving millions of cubic yards of coral to build four parallel 8,500-foot (2,600 m) runways at North Field alone [5]. These fields hosted the B-29s of the Twentieth Air Force, including the *Enola Gay* and *Bockscar*, which launched the atomic bomb attacks on Hiroshima and Nagasaki [5].
*   **Guam:** Seabees and Army engineers built major airbases and the headquarters for the Pacific Fleet. Guam became a primary supply hub, with extensive harbor improvements at Apra Harbor [18].

### Infrastructure
Beyond airfields, the logistical development included building tank farms for fuel storage, ammunition depots, hospitals, and roads. On Saipan, Seabees repaired the island's narrow-gauge railroad to facilitate the movement of supplies from the harbor to the airfields [1]. By 1945, the Marianas had become a fully developed logistical hub capable of supporting the final invasion of Japan.
