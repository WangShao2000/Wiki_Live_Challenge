# Central Africa Regiment

The **Central Africa Regiment** (CAR) was a British colonial infantry unit raised in the British Central Africa Protectorate (modern-day Malawi). Formed in 1896 and initially designated as the **Central African Rifles**, the regiment played a significant role in the consolidation of British colonial rule in Central Africa and participated in several overseas campaigns, including the War of the Golden Stool in Ashanti (modern Ghana) and the Somaliland Campaign. The regiment was characterized by its composition of African enlisted soldiers, Sikh non-commissioned officers, and British officers. In 1902, the unit was amalgamated with other colonial forces to form the King's African Rifles (KAR), becoming the 1st and 2nd (Nyasaland) Battalions of the new regiment.

## History

### Formation and Early Operations
The regiment traces its origins to the irregular forces raised to suppress the slave trade in the region during the early 1890s. In 1896, the colonial administration formalized these forces into a unit originally titled the **British Central African Rifles** [1]. The primary objective of the unit was to police the protectorate, suppress slave raiding, and counter resistance from local groups such as the Ngoni and Yao [2].

In 1898, the unit was renamed the **Central African Rifles**, dropping the word "British" from its title [1]. By 1899, the unit was re-designated as the **Central Africa Regiment** [1][3]. During this period, the regiment was expanded to two battalions. The 1st Battalion was primarily tasked with internal security and garrison duties within the protectorate, while the 2nd Battalion was raised specifically for overseas service to assist in other British colonial conflicts [2].

### Internal Security and the Angoni Rebellion
Throughout the late 1890s, the regiment was engaged in various punitive expeditions within Central Africa. A notable operation occurred in 1898, known as the Angoni Rebellion. The Central African Rifles were deployed to assist the British South Africa Company in Northern Rhodesia (modern Zambia) near Fort Jameson (now Chipata). The operation was launched in response to threats against European settlers and disputes over land and taxation involving the Ngoni people under Chief Mpezeni and his son Singu [2]. The regiment, equipped with Maxim guns and 7-pounder field guns, suppressed the resistance [2].

## Organization and Composition

### Command Structure
Like many colonial units of the era, the Central Africa Regiment was commanded by officers seconded from the British Army. The regiment relied heavily on Sikh soldiers from India, who served as drill instructors, artillerymen, and non-commissioned officers (NCOs) [2][3]. The Sikhs were viewed by the colonial authorities as a disciplined core that could effectively train and stiffen the resolve of the locally recruited African troops [4].

### African Recruitment
The rank and file of the regiment were recruited from various ethnic groups within the protectorate. The British preferred specific groups they considered to have "martial races" qualities. Initially, the Atonga (Tonga), Yao, and Marimba people were the primary sources of recruits [2]. The Ngoni, despite their military reputation, were initially not recruited due to their resistance to British rule, although this policy later changed [2].

### Uniforms
The regiment's uniform evolved over its short history. On active service, soldiers typically wore khaki drill uniforms. A distinctive feature of the regimental band was their bright yellow trousers, which earned them the nickname "Canary Birds" [5].

## Overseas Campaigns

The Central Africa Regiment was notable among East African colonial forces for its extensive service outside its home territory.

### Mauritius Garrison
In 1899, following the formation of the 2nd Battalion, elements of the regiment were deployed to Mauritius to serve as a garrison force [6]. This deployment allowed for the relief of British regular troops for service elsewhere, particularly as tensions rose in South Africa prior to the Second Boer War [2].

### War of the Golden Stool (1900)
In 1900, the 2nd Battalion of the Central Africa Regiment was deployed to the Gold Coast (modern Ghana) to take part in the War of the Golden Stool (also known as the Ashanti War). Commanded by Lieutenant-Colonel H.E.J. Brake, the battalion was part of the relief force sent to suppress the uprising led by Yaa Asantewaa [4][4].

The regiment, accompanied by Sikh detachments, participated in heavy fighting in the dense forests of the Ashanti region. The unit was involved in the relief of Kumasi and actions at Kokofu [7]. The performance of the Central Africa Regiment in this campaign was highly regarded by colonial commanders, with the unit earning the "Ashanti 1900" medal [4][4].

### The Gambia Expedition (1901)
Following their service in Ashanti, a contingent of the 2nd Battalion was diverted to the Gambia Colony and Protectorate in 1901 to suppress a local insurgency. The expedition was launched in response to the killing of two British travelling commissioners at Sankandi in June 1900 [8].

The expeditionary force, known as the Gambia Field Force, was commanded by Lieutenant-Colonel Brake and consisted of four companies of the 2nd Central Africa Regiment and four companies of the 3rd West India Regiment [9][8]. The troops arrived off Bathurst (now Banjul) in January 1901. To maintain the element of surprise, the force bypassed the capital and landed at Tendaba, marching inland to capture the towns of Dumbutu and Sankandi [9][8]. The operation resulted in the suppression of the insurgency and the execution or deportation of the ringleaders [8].

### Somaliland Campaign
Elements of the regiment, specifically the 2nd Battalion, were deployed to British Somaliland to participate in operations against the Dervish state led by Mohammed Abdullah Hassan (referred to by the British as the "Mad Mullah") [6]. The regiment participated in the early phases of the campaign in 1901 and 1902.

During the fighting at Erego on October 6, 1902, Lieutenant-Colonel Alexander Stanhope Cobbe, a British officer serving with the regiment (by then in the process of becoming the King's African Rifles), performed actions for which he was awarded the Victoria Cross [10][10]. Cobbe rescued a wounded orderly under heavy fire and worked a Maxim gun single-handedly at a critical moment during the engagement [10][10].

## Amalgamation

On January 1, 1902, the Central Africa Regiment was amalgamated with the Uganda Rifles and the East Africa Rifles to form a unified colonial force known as the **King's African Rifles** (KAR) [1][11].

Upon amalgamation:
*   The 1st Battalion, Central Africa Regiment became the **1st (Nyasaland) Battalion, King's African Rifles** [1][12].
*   The 2nd Battalion, Central Africa Regiment became the **2nd (Nyasaland) Battalion, King's African Rifles** [1][12].

These battalions continued to serve with distinction under their new titles throughout the colonial period, seeing action in both World War I and World War II [11]. The lineage of the Central Africa Regiment is preserved in the military history of Malawi.

## Notable Personnel

*   **Lieutenant-Colonel Alexander Stanhope Cobbe VC, GCB, KCSI, DSO:** Served with the regiment and was awarded the Victoria Cross for gallantry at Erego, Somaliland, in 1902 [10][13].
*   **Lieutenant-Colonel H.E.J. Brake CB, DSO:** Commanded the 2nd Battalion during the Ashanti War and the Gambia Expedition [4][8].
*   **Colonel Willoughby Verner:** A notable officer associated with the early command of the regiment [14].
