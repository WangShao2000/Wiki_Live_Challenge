# Parasitic ant

**Parasitic ants** are a diverse group of ant species that exhibit **social parasitism**, a life history strategy in which a parasite exploits the labor, resources, and social organization of another ant colony (the host) for its own survival and reproduction. Unlike individual parasitism, which targets a single organism, social parasitism targets the entire eusocial colony structure. Approximately 230 to 400 species of ants are known to be social parasites, representing a significant evolutionary phenomenon within the family Formicidae [1][2][3].

These ants have evolved various strategies to infiltrate host colonies, ranging from temporary parasitism, where a queen kills the host queen and usurps the nest, to **dulosis** (slave-making), where workers raid other nests to steal brood. The most specialized form is **inquilinism**, where the parasite lives permanently within the host nest, often without a worker caste of its own, relying entirely on the host workers to rear its offspring [1][4].

The evolution of parasitic ants is frequently described by **Emery's rule**, which posits that social parasites and their hosts are often closely related phylogenetically, typically belonging to the same genus or sister genera [5][6]. To overcome the sophisticated recognition systems of host colonies, parasitic ants employ complex chemical and acoustic mimicry to integrate themselves into the host society [7][8].

## Classification and life history strategies

Social parasitism in ants is generally classified into three to four major types based on the level of dependence on the host and the behavior of the parasite: temporary social parasitism, dulosis (slave-making), inquilinism (permanent parasitism), and xenobiosis (guest ants) [2][9].

### Temporary social parasitism
In temporary social parasitism, the parasitic queen is unable to found a new colony independently. Instead, she invades an established colony of a host species, often killing or driving away the resident queen [1][10]. The host workers then care for the parasite queen and her brood. As the host workers die of natural causes and are replaced by the parasite's offspring, the colony population eventually shifts to consist entirely of the parasitic species [2].

This strategy is common in the genus *Lasius* (e.g., *Lasius umbratus*) and *Formica* (e.g., the *Formica rufa* group) [2][11]. Phylogenetic studies suggest that temporary social parasitism is often an evolutionary intermediate step between independent colony foundation and more specialized forms of parasitism like dulosis or inquilinism [1][11].

### Dulosis (slave-making)
Dulosis, or slave-making, involves parasitic ants raiding the nests of other ant species to capture brood (usually pupae). The captured brood is brought back to the parasite's nest, where it hatches and contributes to the workforce of the parasite colony, performing tasks such as foraging, nest maintenance, and brood care [12][13]. The enslaved ants function as if they were in their own colony, often unaware of their captive status due to olfactory imprinting [13].

Dulotic species are categorized by their level of dependence on slaves:
*   **Facultative slave-makers:** Species such as *Formica sanguinea* can survive and maintain a colony without slaves, although they often conduct raids to augment their workforce [12][14].
*   **Obligate slave-makers:** Species such as *Polyergus* (Amazon ants) are fully dependent on slaves. Their workers often possess specialized mandibles adapted for combat but are incapable of feeding themselves or caring for brood without assistance from enslaved workers [12][15].
*   **Degenerate slave-makers:** Some species, such as those in the genus *Strongylognathus* or *Temnothorax* (formerly *Myrmoxenus*), have reduced worker castes or have lost the ability to conduct effective raids. In these cases, the queen may kill the host queen (similar to temporary parasites) but the colony remains dependent on the remnant host workforce [12].

### Inquilinism (permanent parasitism)
Inquilinism is considered the most derived and specialized form of social parasitism. Inquiline species are permanent residents of the host nest and typically lack a worker caste entirely, producing only reproductive queens and males [1][4]. Unlike temporary parasites, inquiline queens usually coexist with the host queen (tolerance) rather than killing her, although exceptions exist where the host queen is eliminated [7][12].

Because they lack workers, inquilines are obligately dependent on the host colony for all labor, including food gathering and brood rearing. Inquiline queens often possess morphological adaptations such as reduced size or specialized attachment structures to cling to the host queen [16]. An extreme example is *Tetramorium inquilinum*, which spends much of its life riding on the back of the host queen [16][17].

### Xenobiosis (guest ants)
Xenobiosis involves one species living within the nest walls or in close proximity to another species. Unlike inquilines, "guest ants" keep their brood separate from the host's brood. They typically obtain food by soliciting regurgitation (trophallaxis) from host workers or by intercepting food being returned to the host nest [2]. An example is *Formicoxenus nitidulus*, which lives in the nests of *Formica* wood ants [2].

## Evolution

The evolution of social parasitism in ants has occurred independently at least 60 times, resulting in a high diversity of parasitic species [1][3]. The genus *Formica* alone contains species representing all major classes of social parasitism, making it a key model for studying these evolutionary transitions [1][11].

### Emery's rule
A central concept in the study of social parasitism is **Emery's rule**, named after the entomologist Carlo Emery who first formulated the generalization in 1909. The rule states that social parasites and their hosts share a common ancestry and are therefore closely related, often belonging to the same genus [5][6].

The rule is interpreted in two ways:
*   **Strict interpretation:** The parasite is the sister species of the host. This supports the hypothesis of **sympatric speciation**, where the parasite evolves directly from the host lineage within the same geographic population [1][18].
*   **Loose interpretation:** The parasite is a close relative (e.g., a congener) but not necessarily the sister species. This supports **allopatric speciation**, where the parasite and host lineages diverged in separate geographic areas before coming into secondary contact [1][19].

Phylogenetic analyses have provided support for both interpretations depending on the specific lineage. For example, in *Acromyrmex* leaf-cutting ants, the inquiline *Acromyrmex insinuator* appears to be the sister species of its host, supporting the strict version [18]. Conversely, in the genus *Formica*, some permanent social parasites appear to have evolved via the allopatric route, consistent with the loose interpretation [19].

### Evolutionary pathways
Research suggests that inquilinism and dulosis may have evolved through different pathways. In *Formica*, dulosis and inquilinism both appear to have evolved from ancestors that practiced temporary social parasitism [11]. However, in other groups like *Acromyrmex* and *Myrmica*, inquilinism may have evolved directly from free-living ancestors or via different mechanisms, potentially involving intraspecific parasitism (parasitism within the same species) as a precursor [11].

## Mechanisms of integration

To successfully infiltrate and survive within a host colony, parasitic ants must overcome the host's nestmate recognition system, which is primarily based on cuticular hydrocarbons (CHCs)—chemical cues on the insect's exoskeleton that define colony identity [7][8].

### Chemical deception
Parasites employ three main chemical strategies:
1.  **Chemical insignificance:** Newly emerged or invading parasites often possess very low quantities of recognition cues on their cuticle, making them effectively "invisible" to host workers. This allows them to enter the colony without triggering aggression [8][20][21].
2.  **Chemical camouflage:** The parasite acquires the colony odor of the host by physical contact, grooming, or rubbing against nest material. This allows the parasite to blend in with the host workforce [7][22].
3.  **Chemical mimicry:** The parasite actively biosynthesizes cuticular hydrocarbons that mimic the profile of the host species. This is a more specialized adaptation often seen in highly integrated inquilines [7][8].

### Acoustic mimicry
In addition to chemical cues, some social parasites exploit acoustic communication. Queens of certain ant species produce distinctive sounds (stridulations) that elicit benevolent care from workers. Some parasites, such as the inquiline ant *Myrmica karavajevi*, utilize vibroacoustic signals that mimic the host queen, allowing them to achieve high social status within the colony hierarchy and receive preferential treatment from host workers [7]. While this behavior is famously documented in the parasitic larvae of *Maculinea* butterflies, it is also a strategy employed by parasitic ants [23][24].

## Notable species

### *Tetramorium inquilinum*
Formerly known as *Teleutomyrmex schneideri*, *Tetramorium inquilinum* is often cited as the "ultimate" parasitic ant due to its extreme morphological adaptations. It is an inquiline parasite of *Tetramorium* pavement ants. The queens are ectoparasitic, meaning they spend most of their lives clinging to the back of the host queen [16][17]. They have evolved a concave abdomen and specialized tarsal claws to grip the host. They lack a worker caste and rely entirely on food regurgitated by host workers [16][17].

### *Solenopsis daguerrei*
*Solenopsis daguerrei* is a workerless inquiline parasite of fire ants, including the red imported fire ant (*Solenopsis invicta*). Native to South America, young queens of *S. daguerrei* invade fire ant colonies and attach themselves to the host queen. They produce pheromones that mimic the host queen, causing host workers to feed and care for them and their brood, often at the expense of the host's own offspring [25][26]. Because *S. daguerrei* can inhibit the growth of host colonies, it has been studied as a potential biological control agent for invasive fire ants [25][27].

### *Polyergus* (Amazon ants)
The genus *Polyergus* comprises obligate slave-making ants. Workers of this genus are specialized for raiding; they possess sharp, sickle-shaped mandibles designed for piercing the exoskeletons of defending ants but are ineffective for foraging or brood care [12][15]. A *Polyergus* colony is completely dependent on enslaved workers (typically *Formica* species) for all nest maintenance and feeding [12].

## Ecological impact

Social parasitism imposes significant costs on host colonies. In the case of temporary parasitism and degenerate slave-making, the host queen is killed, leading to the eventual genetic death of the host colony [1][12]. In dulotic systems, raids can result in the loss of large numbers of host brood and adult workers [15].

Even in inquiline systems where the host queen survives, the presence of parasites drains resources. Parasitized colonies often show reduced production of host workers and sexuals (alates) [26][28]. For example, fire ant colonies parasitized by *Solenopsis daguerrei* have significantly fewer host queens and less worker brood compared to unparasitized colonies [26]. However, the impact varies; some inquilines may have a negligible effect on host fitness if their populations remain low, while others can effectively castrate the host colony by diverting all resources to the parasite's reproduction [28][29].
