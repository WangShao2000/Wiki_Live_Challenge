# Natural Dis-tinction Un-natural Selection

**Natural Dis-tinction Un-natural Selection** is the Spring/Summer 2009 womenswear collection by British fashion designer Alexander McQueen. Presented on October 3, 2008, at Le Centquatre in Paris, the collection is noted for its thematic focus on the natural world, the theory of evolution, and the impact of industrialization on the environment. The collection is widely recognized in fashion history for its pioneering application of digitally engineered prints, a technique where textile patterns are computer-manipulated to fit the specific geometry of a garment's pattern pieces. This collection preceded McQueen's final completed runway show, *Plato's Atlantis* (Spring/Summer 2010), and is considered a significant precursor to the digital aesthetic that defined his late career.

## Background and Concept

The collection was the thirty-third designed by McQueen for his eponymous label [1]. The title, *Natural Dis-tinction Un-natural Selection*, serves as a wordplay on "natural selection," the process of evolution described by naturalist Charles Darwin. The program notes for the show indicated that McQueen drew direct inspiration from Darwin's 1859 text, *On the Origin of Species* [1] [2].

The conceptual framework of the collection explored the tension between the organic beauty of the natural world and the intervention of humanity through technology and industry [1]. McQueen intended to comment on the "deleterious results of industrialization on the natural world" and the concept of survival of the fittest [3]. Despite the gravity of the environmental themes—addressing issues such as the destruction of nature by greed—McQueen stated that his intention was "not to preach," but rather to present a narrative through clothing [2] [4].

The collection is often categorized by fashion historians as the first in a trilogy of shows addressing environmental and existential themes, followed by *The Horn of Plenty* (Autumn/Winter 2009), which critiqued consumerism, and *Plato's Atlantis* (Spring/Summer 2010), which envisioned a post-apocalyptic underwater future [5] [1].

## Runway Show

### Setting and Atmosphere
The runway presentation took place at Le Centquatre (104), a cultural center in Paris that had formerly served as a municipal undertaking establishment and mortuary [1] [6]. This choice of venue aligned with McQueen's frequent exploration of macabre themes and mortality.

The set design featured a "cave of Victorian taxidermy," creating a museum-like atmosphere reminiscent of a cabinet of curiosities [7] [7]. The runway was lined with a menagerie of stuffed animals, including a polar bear, a giraffe, a zebra, and a tiger [2] [4]. At the rear of the stage, a large circular screen projected images of a rotating Earth, which transitioned into a burning sun, a silver moon, and finally a close-up of McQueen's own eyeball, suggesting the designer's role as an observer of the unfolding narrative [1] [7] [4].

### Soundtrack
The auditory landscape of the show mirrored the thematic progression of the collection. The presentation began with a soundscape of natural noises, including animal calls, roaring, and hoofbeats, accompanied by steel drums and tribal chants [1] [2]. As the show transitioned from the "natural" to the "un-natural" segments, the music shifted to electronic dance music, reflecting the encroachment of modernity and industry [1].

## Design and Aesthetics

The collection was visually divided into two distinct phases: the first representing the natural world and the second representing the modern, industrial world [1] [2].

### Phase I: The Natural
The first segment of the show focused on organic imagery and soft silhouettes. Garments were constructed from natural fabrics and featured prints derived from the earth's raw materials, such as wood grain, granite, and meadow flowers [6] [4]. The color palette in this section was characterized by soft, earthy tones and neutrals.

A key silhouette introduced in this phase was the "Bell Jar" dress. These garments featured rounded skirts sculpted over the hips to mimic the shape of a Victorian glass specimen jar, referencing the collection of biological specimens by scientists like Darwin [8]. One notable example is a dress featuring silk flowers trapped between layers of tulle, simulating the preservation of botanical samples [2] [4].

### Phase II: The Un-natural
The second half of the show shifted toward a harder, more synthetic aesthetic, representing the "un-natural selection" of human industry. The designs incorporated sharp, exaggerated shoulders and angular silhouettes [9]. The prints in this section, while still derived from natural forms like crystals and bones, were digitally manipulated to appear symmetrical, kaleidoscopic, and artificial [1] [4].

This section featured the use of Swarovski crystals to create "mirror-ball" dresses and bodysuits that encased the body in a hard, reflective armor [10]. One of the most documented pieces from this segment is a bell-jar shaped dress constructed from a net base densely covered in Swarovski crystals, described by conservators as "more crystal than textile" [8] [11]. Other prints included images of industrial structures, such as the Eiffel Tower, and diamond patterns that morphed into human skeletons [7] [6].

### Engineered Prints
*Natural Dis-tinction Un-natural Selection* is recognized for its pioneering use of "engineered prints" [12] [13]. Unlike traditional textile design, where a pattern repeats across a roll of fabric and is cut randomly, McQueen and his design team used digital technology to map images specifically to the garment's pattern pieces [12]. This ensured that the image—whether a wood grain or a crystal fracture—flowed seamlessly across the seams and complemented the shape of the body [12].

This technique allowed for "hyper-real" visuals and optical illusions, such as a dress printed with a rainbow moth pattern that utilized the symmetry of the insect to accentuate the female form [14] [15]. Fashion critics and curators have noted that this collection significantly contributed to the widespread adoption of digital printing in the fashion industry [12] [1].

## Reception
Contemporary critical reception to the collection was generally positive, though some critics noted a departure from the theatrical performance art that characterized McQueen's earlier shows. Sarah Mower, writing for *Vogue*, opened her review with the rhetorical question, "Alexander McQueen, environmentalist?" expressing surprise at the designer's direct engagement with ecological politics [16] [16]. Mower noted that despite the serious underlying themes, the collection possessed a "lighter mood" than previous seasons and praised the commercial viability of the wood grain prints [16].

Reviewers highlighted the technical precision of the tailoring and the successful juxtaposition of the natural and the technological [9] [6]. The "Bell Jar" silhouette and the crystal-encrusted evening wear were frequently cited as standouts [17] [7].

## Legacy
The collection is viewed as a critical bridge in McQueen's design evolution, foreshadowing the aesthetic of *Plato's Atlantis* (Spring/Summer 2010), which pushed the concept of digitally engineered prints and post-human silhouettes to their extreme [18] [1].

Garments from *Natural Dis-tinction Un-natural Selection* have been acquired by major museums, including the Victoria and Albert Museum (V&A) in London, the Los Angeles County Museum of Art (LACMA), and the National Gallery of Victoria (NGV) in Melbourne [19] [12] [8]. Pieces from the collection were featured prominently in the retrospective exhibition *Alexander McQueen: Savage Beauty*, which appeared at the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York (2011) and the V&A (2015) [14] [8]. The "Bell Jar" dress was specifically highlighted in the V&A exhibition for its craftsmanship and thematic relevance to the "Cabinet of Curiosities" section [17] [8].
