# 2025 Pennsylvania Supreme Court election

The **2025 Pennsylvania Supreme Court election** was a statewide retention election held on November 4, 2025. Voters determined whether three incumbent justices of the Supreme Court of Pennsylvania—Christine Donohue, Kevin Dougherty, and David Wecht—should serve additional ten-year terms. All three justices, who were originally elected as Democrats in 2015, were retained by margins of approximately 23 percentage points, securing more than 60% of the "Yes" vote [1][2][3].

The election was characterized by record-breaking spending for a judicial retention race in Pennsylvania, with total expenditures estimated between $15 million and $20 million [4][5]. While retention elections are historically low-profile and nonpartisan in nature, the 2025 cycle drew significant attention due to the court's 5–2 Democratic majority and its pivotal role in adjudicating issues regarding voting rights, redistricting, and reproductive healthcare [4][6][7].

The retention of Justices Donohue, Dougherty, and Wecht preserved the Democratic majority on the court. Had any of the justices been rejected by voters, Governor Josh Shapiro would have been tasked with appointing interim replacements, subject to confirmation by a two-thirds vote of the Republican-controlled Pennsylvania State Senate [2][4].

## Background

### Electoral System
The Supreme Court of Pennsylvania is the state's court of last resort. It consists of seven justices who are elected to ten-year terms. Pennsylvania utilizes a hybrid method for judicial selection. Justices are initially chosen in partisan elections. At the conclusion of their ten-year term, they must face a nonpartisan retention election to remain on the bench. In these elections, voters cast a simple "Yes" or "No" vote on whether the justice should serve an additional term. If a justice is retained, they serve another ten years or until they reach the mandatory retirement age of 75 [1][6].

Since the establishment of the retention system in 1968, only one Pennsylvania Supreme Court justice, Russell Nigro in 2005, has failed to win retention. That defeat occurred amidst public backlash regarding a legislative pay raise scandal [1][8].

### Court Composition
Prior to the 2025 election, the court held a 5–2 Democratic majority. This majority was established in 2015 when Democrats swept three open seats, electing Donohue, Dougherty, and Wecht [1][3]. The 2025 election was viewed by political analysts as a critical moment for the court's ideological balance. If the justices had lost their retention bids, the court could have faced a deadlock or a shift in power depending on the outcome of interim appointments and subsequent special elections [4][9].

### Incumbents
Three justices faced retention in 2025:

*   **Christine Donohue:** First elected in 2015. She previously served on the Superior Court of Pennsylvania. Donohue authored the majority opinion in 2022 upholding Act 77, the state's no-excuse mail-in voting law [2][3]. Due to the mandatory retirement age of 75, Donohue is eligible to serve only until 2027, regardless of the 2025 retention result [3][10].
*   **Kevin Dougherty:** First elected in 2015. He previously served as the Administrative Judge of the Family Court in Philadelphia and on the Philadelphia Court of Common Pleas [10][11].
*   **David Wecht:** First elected in 2015. He previously served on the Superior Court of Pennsylvania and the Allegheny County Court of Common Pleas. Wecht has authored concurring opinions on issues regarding reproductive rights and the Environmental Rights Amendment [6][11].

## Campaign

The 2025 retention election was noted for being unusually contentious and expensive compared to historical norms for judicial retention races in the state [3][12]. While justices are prohibited from directly campaigning on political issues or promising specific rulings, outside interest groups invested heavily in the race [1][13].

### Pro-Retention Campaign
Supporters of the justices, organized largely under the political action committee (PAC) **Pennsylvanians for Judicial Fairness**, framed the election as a defense of judicial independence and fundamental rights [13][14]. This coalition included labor unions, trial lawyers, and the Democratic National Committee (DNC) [1][5].

Key themes of the pro-retention campaign included:
*   **Reproductive Rights:** Advertisements highlighted the court's role in protecting abortion access in Pennsylvania, particularly following the U.S. Supreme Court's overturning of *Roe v. Wade* in 2022. Groups such as Planned Parenthood Votes and Reproductive Freedom for All spent nearly $500,000 combined in support of retention [14][15].
*   **Voting Rights:** Proponents emphasized the justices' decisions to uphold mail-in voting and reject challenges to the 2020 presidential election results [6][8].
*   **Judicial Independence:** The campaign argued that removing the justices would invite political chaos and allow "billionaires" to buy the court [8][15].

The American Civil Liberties Union (ACLU) spent approximately $900,000 on an awareness campaign regarding the election [8][16]. The Pennsylvania Bar Association (PBA) recommended all three justices for retention, describing them as "fair," "well-prepared," and "logical" [3][17].

### Anti-Retention Campaign
Opposition to the justices was led by conservative groups, primarily the **Commonwealth Leaders Fund** and **Citizens for Term Limits**, which are associated with Jeffrey Yass, a prominent donor and co-founder of Susquehanna International Group [8][14][18]. Other opposition groups included **Fair Courts America**, funded by Richard Uihlein [19][20].

Key themes of the anti-retention campaign included:
*   **"Soft on Crime":** Opponents criticized the justices for procedural rulings that they argued hindered law enforcement, including the decision to overturn the conviction of Bill Cosby on due process grounds [3][21].
*   **Gerrymandering Accusations:** The Commonwealth Leaders Fund distributed mailers accusing the "liberal Supreme Court" of gerrymandering congressional districts to benefit Democrats. These mailers were criticized by nonpartisan groups like Fair Districts PA for being misleading, as the map featured in the mailers was actually a Republican-drawn map that the court had struck down in 2018 as an unconstitutional partisan gerrymander [2][7][18].
*   **Term Limits:** Some messaging urged voters to vote "No" to impose term limits, framing the retention vote as a mechanism to rotate power [7][22].

### Fundraising and Spending
The election was estimated to be the second-most expensive judicial retention race in U.S. history [2][8]. By mid-October 2025, the three justices and their direct supporters had raised over $14 million [8][16].

*   **Pennsylvanians for Judicial Fairness** reported spending over $3.4 million on advertisements and mailers supporting the justices [5][13].
*   **Commonwealth Partners** and affiliated groups spent over $3 million opposing retention [14].
*   **Citizens for Term Limits** spent approximately $1.8 million on opposition efforts [14][16].

## Results

On November 4, 2025, voters retained all three justices. The results were called by the Associated Press shortly before 10:00 p.m. EST [3]. Each justice received approximately 61% of the vote, with margins of victory around 27 percentage points [2][3].

The justices performed well in Democratic strongholds and maintained sufficient support in suburban areas. Reports indicated they were retained even in over a dozen counties that were won by Donald Trump in the 2024 presidential election [2].

## Aftermath and Analysis

The retention of Justices Donohue, Dougherty, and Wecht ensured that the Democratic Party maintained its 5–2 majority on the Pennsylvania Supreme Court [12]. Political commentators noted that the results signaled voter support for the court's recent direction, particularly regarding election administration and reproductive rights [12][23].

The victory avoided a potential constitutional crisis. Had the justices been removed, Governor Shapiro would have needed to nominate replacements requiring a two-thirds confirmation vote in the State Senate. Given the partisan divide, analysts predicted this could have led to prolonged vacancies or a deadlocked court [4][9].

Despite the victory, the composition of the court is scheduled to change in the near future. Justice Christine Donohue will reach the mandatory retirement age of 75 in 2027, triggering a partisan election for her seat that year [3][10].
