# Red and Purple Modernization Project

The **Red and Purple Modernization (RPM) Project** is a major capital improvement program undertaken by the Chicago Transit Authority (CTA) to rebuild and modernize the infrastructure of the Red Line and Purple Line on the North Side of Chicago, Illinois. The project encompasses a 9.6-mile (15.4 km) corridor extending from the Belmont station in the Lakeview neighborhood to the Linden terminal in Wilmette. It is the largest capital project in the history of the CTA [1][2][3].

The project is designed to replace rail infrastructure constructed in the early 20th century, much of which had reached the end of its useful life. The primary goals of the RPM program are to expand capacity, increase train speeds, improve service reliability, and ensure compliance with the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) at stations that were previously inaccessible [4][5]. The initiative is divided into multiple phases. Phase One, with a budget of approximately $2.1 billion, began construction in 2019 and concluded its primary components in July 2025 [6][7]. This phase included the construction of the Red-Purple Bypass (commonly known as the Belmont Flyover) and the reconstruction of four stations and track structures between Lawrence and Bryn Mawr avenues [8][9].

## Background and History

The Red and Purple lines on Chicago's North Side operate on elevated structures and embankments originally built between 1900 and 1920 [4][6]. By the early 21st century, the infrastructure was over 100 years old, leading to frequent maintenance needs, slow zones, and capacity constraints. The corridor serves some of the most densely populated neighborhoods in Chicago and carries a significant portion of the CTA's daily ridership [5][10].

Planning for the modernization began in 2009 with a vision study to assess the condition of the corridor. In 2011, the CTA and the Federal Transit Administration (FTA) initiated the environmental review process [6][11]. Due to the scale and cost of modernizing the entire 9.6-mile corridor simultaneously, the CTA opted in 2014 to implement the project in phases [11].

## Phase One

Phase One of the RPM project focused on two specific areas within the corridor: the junction north of the Belmont station and the segment between the Lawrence and Bryn Mawr stations. The total cost for this phase was approximately $2.1 billion [6][7]. The CTA selected the Walsh-Fluor Design-Build Team, a joint venture between the Walsh Group and Fluor Corporation, as the general contractor in December 2018 [6][12]. Stantec served as the lead designer, with EXP acting as a design consultant [1][13].

### Red-Purple Bypass
A major component of Phase One was the construction of the Red-Purple Bypass, often referred to as the Belmont Flyover. Located north of the Belmont station, this structure replaced a flat rail junction (Clark Junction) where the Red, Purple, and Brown lines intersected [8][14].

Prior to the bypass, northbound Brown Line trains had to cross the four tracks used by the Red and Purple lines at grade level. This configuration created a bottleneck, requiring Red and Purple line trains to stop while Brown Line trains crossed, which limited the number of trains the CTA could run during rush hours [12][14]. The new bypass, a concrete flyover structure, lifts northbound Brown Line trains over the Red and Purple line tracks, allowing for uninterrupted movement.

Construction on the bypass began in October 2019 [12]. The structure was completed and opened to service on November 19, 2021 [1][15]. The CTA stated that the bypass allows for the addition of up to eight more Red Line trains per hour during rush periods and increases train speeds through the intersection by 60 percent [1][16]. Following the opening of the bypass, crews reconstructed the Red and Purple line tracks underneath the new structure, a process completed in May 2025 [8].

### Lawrence to Bryn Mawr Modernization
The second major component of Phase One involved the complete reconstruction of approximately 1.3 miles (2.1 km) of track and embankment, as well as four stations: Lawrence, Argyle, Berwyn, and Bryn Mawr [7][17].

The original embankment, which was over a century old, was demolished and replaced with a new aerial structure. This modernization allowed for the straightening of curves to improve train speeds and the widening of the right-of-way [18]. The four reconstructed stations were designed to be fully accessible to people with disabilities, featuring elevators, escalators, and wider platforms. Previously, these stations were only accessible via stairs [7][17].

Construction was staged to maintain service during the project. Stage A (2021–2023) involved rebuilding the two northbound tracks, while Stage B (2023–2025) focused on the southbound tracks [19][20]. The four new stations and the completed track structure opened to the public on July 20, 2025 [7][9].

### Corridor Signal Improvements
Phase One also included the installation of a new signal system along 23 track miles between the Belmont and Howard stations. This upgrade was designed to improve train flow and service reliability, similar to modernizing traffic signals on a roadway [19][21].

### Under 'L' Placemaking
The replacement of the solid earthen embankment with a modern open structure created approximately 10 blocks of new open space beneath the tracks between Lawrence and Ardmore avenues [22]. The CTA engaged in a community planning process to determine the use of this space. Final designs unveiled in February 2025 included plans for pedestrian trails, dog parks, playgrounds, and flexible plaza space. Construction of these community amenities is scheduled to take place between 2026 and 2027 [22][23].

## Funding

The $2.1 billion cost of Phase One was funded through a combination of federal and local sources.

### Federal Funding
The project received significant funding from the Federal Transit Administration (FTA). In January 2017, the CTA and FTA signed a Full Funding Grant Agreement (FFGA) that provided approximately $956.6 million through the Core Capacity grant program [6][10]. This program is designed specifically for capital projects that expand capacity in existing, heavily used transit corridors. Additionally, the project received funding from the Congestion Mitigation and Air Quality (CMAQ) Improvement Program [10].

### Local Funding and Transit TIF
To provide the required local matching funds, the City of Chicago utilized a funding mechanism known as a Transit Tax Increment Financing (TIF) district. In 2016, the Illinois General Assembly passed legislation authorizing the creation of Transit TIFs, which differ from traditional TIFs by focusing on transit improvement rather than general economic development [24].

The Red-Purple Modernization Phase One Project TIF was designated by the Chicago City Council in November 2016 [6][25]. This district encompasses properties generally within a half-mile of the corridor. Revenue generated from the growth in property values within this district is used to repay a federal loan (TIFIA) and CTA bonds issued to finance the project [3][25]. The Transit TIF was projected to generate approximately $625 million toward the project costs [3].

## Community Impact and Controversies

### Opposition to the Belmont Flyover
The proposal for the Red-Purple Bypass faced significant opposition from local residents and business owners in the Lakeview neighborhood. The "Coalition to Stop the Belmont Flyover" argued that the structure would be an eyesore, negatively impact property values, and damage the character of the neighborhood [26][27].

A primary point of contention was the necessity of acquiring and demolishing private property to accommodate the new structure. The project required the demolition of approximately 16 buildings, including residential and commercial properties [14][28]. Opponents disputed the CTA's data regarding train delays, suggesting that the benefits of the flyover did not justify the displacement and cost [27].

One historic structure, the Vautravers Building at 947-949 W. Newport Avenue, was preserved. In August 2021, the three-story brick apartment building, weighing approximately 1,000 tons, was moved 30 feet west to clear the path for the new tracks while avoiding demolition [29].

### Construction Quality Issues
In 2022, less than a year after the Red-Purple Bypass opened, reports emerged of concrete cracking and falling from the new structure. The CTA acknowledged the issue, attributing it to a quality control failure by the contractor, Walsh-Fluor, involving insufficient grout in certain areas that allowed water to penetrate and freeze [30][31]. The CTA stated that the defects were cosmetic rather than structural and that the contractor would repair the damage at their own expense [30].

## Future Phases

The RPM project is a multi-phase program, with Phase One covering only a portion of the 9.6-mile corridor. Future phases are intended to address the remaining segments, including the track and stations from Addison to Sheridan, the segment from Thorndale to Howard, and the Purple Line Evanston branch [32].

The CTA initiated the "RPM Next Phases Planning Study" in 2021 to evaluate alternatives and determine the sequencing for subsequent work. This study involved public engagement meetings in 2023 and 2024 [32][33]. As of late 2025, a specific timeline for the start of construction for Phase Two had not been established, as the project is dependent on securing future federal and local funding [6][32].
