# Dulles International Airport Main Terminal

The **Dulles International Airport Main Terminal** is the primary terminal building of Washington Dulles International Airport (IAD) in Dulles, Virginia. Designed by Finnish-American architect Eero Saarinen and opened in 1962, the structure is regarded as a significant example of mid-century modern architecture and a landmark of the Jet Age [1][2]. The terminal is distinct for its suspended catenary roof, which hangs between angled concrete columns, creating a large, column-free interior space [1][3].

Originally measuring 600 feet (180 m) in length, the terminal was expanded to 1,240 feet (380 m) in 1996 based on Saarinen's original modular design specifications [1][4]. The building houses the airport's ticketing counters, baggage claim, security checkpoints, and the "Z" Gates, while also serving as the central hub for transportation to the airport's remote midfield concourses via the AeroTrain system and mobile lounges [3][5]. The terminal was recognized by the American Institute of Architects (AIA) with an Honor Award in 1966 and was determined eligible for the National Register of Historic Places in 1978 [6][7].

## History

### Planning and Construction
Following World War II, the Civil Aeronautics Administration (CAA) identified a need for a second major airport to serve the Washington, D.C. metropolitan area, as Washington National Airport was reaching capacity [3]. In 1958, President Dwight D. Eisenhower selected a site in Chantilly, Virginia, displacing the community of Willard [3][6]. The engineering firm Ammann & Whitney was selected as the lead contractor, with Eero Saarinen and Associates chosen as the architect for the terminal building and control tower [8].

Saarinen aimed to create a structure that captured the "soul of the airport" and the dynamic nature of flight [8]. His design team, including consultant Charles Landrum, analyzed passenger movements at other airports and determined that walking distances were becoming excessive as terminals expanded [1][9]. To address this, Saarinen proposed a compact terminal design that utilized "mobile lounges"—large, bus-like vehicles—to transport passengers from the terminal directly to aircraft parked on remote ramps, thereby eliminating the need for long "finger" piers or concourses attached to the main building [10][11].

Construction began in 1958 [8]. The terminal was dedicated on November 17, 1962, by President John F. Kennedy and former President Eisenhower [1][7]. Saarinen died in 1961, a year before the building's completion [12].

### Expansion and Renovation
The terminal was designed with future expansion in mind. Saarinen's original plans allowed for the building to be extended on both ends while maintaining its architectural profile [8].

*   **1970s and 1980s:** As passenger traffic grew, the airport underwent several modifications. In 1978, Hellmuth, Obata & Kassabaum (HOK) designed a 50-foot corridor addition to the length of the terminal [7]. In 1982, new waiting areas were added to the upper level, and baggage facilities were expanded below [8].
*   **1990s Expansion:** In 1991, a separate International Arrivals Building (IAB) was constructed 300 feet west of the Main Terminal [7]. Between 1993 and 1996, the Main Terminal underwent a major expansion designed by Skidmore, Owings & Merrill (SOM). This project extended the terminal's length from 600 feet to 1,240 feet, effectively doubling its size and connecting it to the IAB [1][13]. The expansion replicated Saarinen's original catenary structure, concrete finishes, and terrazzo flooring to ensure visual continuity [13].
*   **21st Century:** In 2010, the AeroTrain system opened, replacing many mobile lounge routes. The Main Terminal station was constructed entirely underground to preserve the sightlines of the historic building [13]. In 2022, the terminal was connected to the Washington Metro Silver Line via an underground pedestrian tunnel equipped with moving walkways [14][15].

In March 2025, the Metropolitan Washington Airports Authority (MWAA) selected HOK to design a further expansion of the airport's facilities, including a new "Tier 2" concourse and potential modifications to handle increased passenger capacity, although the focus of this expansion is largely on the airfield concourses rather than the footprint of the Main Terminal itself [16][17].

## Architecture and Design

### Structure and Roof
The Main Terminal is defined by its suspended roof, which Saarinen described as a "huge continuous hammock suspended between concrete trees" [1][2]. The roof is supported by a catenary system of heavy suspension cables strung between two rows of outward-leaning concrete pylons [1][12].
*   **Columns:** The colonnade on the approach (north) side features columns that are 65 feet (20 m) high, while the columns on the airfield (south) side are 40 feet (12 m) high [2][18]. The piers slant outward to counteract the tension of the cables [2].
*   **Roof Panels:** Precast concrete panels sit atop the suspension cables to form the roof deck [1].
*   **Interior:** The suspension design allows for a column-free interior, providing an open, unobstructed space for passenger processing [3][19]. The interior layout was originally organized into 40-foot (12 m) wide bays, each containing ticket counters and loading areas for mobile lounges [1][18].

### Mobile Lounges
The architecture of the Main Terminal was intrinsically linked to the concept of the mobile lounge (also known as the Passenger Transfer Vehicle or PTV). Because the terminal did not originally have gates or jet bridges attached to it, passengers boarded these elevated vehicles directly from the terminal's airfield side [10][11]. The lounges would then drive across the apron to the aircraft. This system allowed the terminal to remain physically smaller than contemporary airports of similar capacity [1]. While the mobile lounge system has been largely supplanted by the AeroTrain and fixed concourses, the vehicles remain in use for transport to the International Arrivals Building and Concourse D [10][20].

## Facilities and Operations

The Main Terminal is a two-level structure with a basement level for automated baggage handling and transportation connections [1][21].

### Levels
*   **Upper Level (Departures):** This level houses four main ticketing areas, security checkpoints (East, West, and TSA PreCheck), and concession stands [5][21]. The open architectural volume allows departing passengers to see the sweeping ceiling structure.
*   **Lower Level (Arrivals):** This level contains the baggage claim carousels, U.S. Customs and Border Protection facilities, and ground transportation access [3][21].
*   **Ground Transportation Level:** A lower level provides access to parking shuttles, taxis, and ride-share services [5].

### Z Gates
While most aircraft gates are located in the remote midfield concourses (A, B, C, and D), the Main Terminal contains a small set of gates known as the **Z Gates** (specifically Z6 through Z10) [3][19]. These gates are connected directly to the main building and primarily serve domestic flights for airlines such as Air Canada and United Express [19][22].

### Transportation Connections
The Main Terminal serves as the intermodal hub for the airport:
*   **AeroTrain:** An underground automated people mover system connects the Main Terminal to Concourses A, B, and C. The station is located beneath the terminal's suspended structure [5][13].
*   **Silver Line Metro:** The Washington Dulles International Airport station, part of the Washington Metro Silver Line, is connected to the Main Terminal via an underground pedestrian tunnel [14][15].
*   **Mobile Lounges:** Shuttle vehicles continue to operate from the Main Terminal to transport passengers to Concourse D and to ferry international arrivals to the International Arrivals Building [5][20].

## Recognition and Significance

The Dulles Main Terminal is widely considered a masterpiece of modern architecture.
*   **Awards:** In 1966, the terminal received the Honor Award from the American Institute of Architects (AIA). The jury cited the building for its "vigorous, free and graceful movement which we associate with flight" [6][7]. It also received the AIA Twenty-five Year Award [1].
*   **Historic Status:** The terminal was determined eligible for the National Register of Historic Places in 1978, only 16 years after its completion, due to its exceptional architectural significance [6][7].
*   **Cultural Impact:** The terminal's futuristic design has made it a popular location for film and television, appearing in movies such as *Die Hard 2* and *Body of Lies* [6].
*   **Stamps:** The building was featured on a U.S. Postal Service stamp in the 1980s as part of a series honoring American architecture [18].
