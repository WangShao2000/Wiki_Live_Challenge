# Assassination of George Lincoln Rockwell

On August 25, 1967, George Lincoln Rockwell, the founder and leader of the American Nazi Party (later renamed the National Socialist White People's Party), was assassinated in Arlington, Virginia. Rockwell was shot and killed while leaving a laundromat by John Patler, a former captain in the party who had been expelled months earlier for alleged ideological deviations and internal disputes.

The assassination marked a significant turning point for the neo-Nazi movement in the United States. Following a highly publicized controversy regarding the military's refusal to allow a burial with Nazi honors at a national cemetery, Rockwell's body was cremated. Patler was convicted of first-degree murder in December 1967 and sentenced to 20 years in prison. The event led to the fracturing of the American Nazi Party and the emergence of various splinter groups that influenced the trajectory of white supremacist organizations in the late 20th century.

## Background

### George Lincoln Rockwell and the American Nazi Party
George Lincoln Rockwell, a veteran of the United States Navy who served during World War II and the Korean War, founded the American Nazi Party (ANP) in 1959 [1][2]. Establishing the party's headquarters in Arlington, Virginia, Rockwell sought to promote National Socialism in the United States, advocating for white supremacy, antisemitism, and racial segregation [2][3]. He utilized provocative tactics to garner media attention, such as picketing civil rights marches and giving inflammatory speeches at universities [2][4].

In January 1967, in an attempt to broaden the movement's appeal and move toward "Phase Two" of his political strategy, Rockwell renamed the organization the National Socialist White People's Party (NSWPP) [4][5]. He also coined the slogan "White Power" and began distancing the group's public image from some of the traditional German Nazi iconography, although the ideology remained rooted in National Socialism [1][3].

### John Patler
The perpetrator, John Patler, was born John Christ Patsalos in New York City on January 6, 1938, to Greek-American parents [6][7]. His childhood was marked by tragedy; when he was five years old, his father shot and killed his mother [7][8]. Patler served in the United States Marine Corps from 1958 to 1960 but received a discharge on grounds of "unsuitability" after being arrested at a Nazi rally [6][8].

Patler joined the American Nazi Party in 1960 and legally changed his surname from Patsalos to Patler, a name chosen for its phonetic resemblance to "Hitler" [8][9]. He rose through the ranks to become a captain and served as the editor and cartoonist for the party's publication, *The Stormtrooper* [6][10]. Patler and Rockwell shared a close, almost familial relationship for several years, with Patler later stating he loved Rockwell "like a father" [8].

### Internal Conflicts and Expulsion
Despite their close relationship, tensions arose between Patler and the party leadership. Patler's "swarthy" Greek heritage was a point of contention among some members who adhered to a strict "Nordic" definition of the Aryan race, leading to friction with other high-ranking members such as Matt Koehl and William Luther Pierce [7][10][11]. Rockwell had defended Patler against these internal attacks, arguing for a definition of "White" that included Southern Europeans [10].

However, ideological and personal disputes eventually caused a rift. In March 1967, Rockwell expelled Patler from the party [10]. The official reasons cited included "Bolshevik leanings"—specifically an alleged attempt to introduce Marxist doctrine into the party platform—and causing dissension within the ranks [4][6][12]. Patler was also accused of "abandoning his post" and "gross insubordination" [10]. Following his expulsion, Patler remained in the Arlington area but was isolated from the movement that had defined his adult life.

### June 1967 Assassination Attempt
Two months prior to his death, Rockwell was the target of a failed assassination attempt. On June 28, 1967, as Rockwell returned to the party's headquarters (known as the "barracks") on Wilson Boulevard, he found the driveway blocked by a felled tree and brush [4][13]. Assuming it was a prank by local teenagers, Rockwell exited his vehicle to clear the obstruction.

As a young party member assisted him, two shots were fired from behind a brick driveway pillar. One bullet ricocheted off Rockwell's car, narrowly missing his head [10][13]. Rockwell pursued the shooter, who escaped. Although the gunman was not immediately caught, prosecutors later alleged that Patler was the perpetrator of this initial attempt [10]. Following this incident, Rockwell petitioned the Arlington County Circuit Court for a gun permit, but no action was taken on the request before his death [4][13].

## The Assassination

### August 25, 1967
On the morning of Friday, August 25, 1967, Rockwell was at the party headquarters in Arlington. Shortly before noon, he drove his blue-and-white 1958 Chevrolet to the Dominion Hills Shopping Center, located in the 6000 block of Wilson Boulevard, to do laundry at the Econ-o-Wash laundromat [9][13][14]. This was a routine domestic task for the party leader, who lived at the headquarters.

After placing his clothes in a washing machine, Rockwell realized he had forgotten bleach. He purchased a box of Ivory Snow detergent and a copy of the *New York Daily News* before returning to his car to drive home and retrieve the bleach [9][14][15].

### The Shooting
At approximately 11:58 a.m. to 12:00 p.m., as Rockwell sat in his vehicle in the parking lot, shots rang out from the roof of a nearby building, which housed a beauty salon and barber shop [12][14][16]. The shooter used a 7.63mm Mauser "Broomhandle" semi-automatic pistol [8][10].

Two bullets pierced the windshield of Rockwell's Chevrolet. One shot missed or passed through the seat, but the second struck Rockwell in the chest, penetrating his heart [10][14][15]. The car rolled backward and collided with another vehicle. Rockwell managed to stagger out of the passenger side door, pointed toward the roof of the shopping center, and collapsed on the pavement [5][13][15]. He was pronounced dead at the scene at 12:02 p.m. [10].

### Arrest of John Patler
Witnesses at the shopping center reacted immediately. Tom Blankeney, the owner of a barbershop in the center, heard the shots and saw Rockwell collapse. He ran toward the direction of the shooter and called the police [17].

Arlington police responded rapidly. Approximately 30 to 45 minutes after the shooting, a patrolman arrested John Patler at a bus stop on Washington Boulevard, about a mile from the crime scene [10][12][13]. Patler was out of breath and his trousers were wet, suggesting he had fled through the nearby Four Mile Run creek [14][17]. The murder weapon, the Mauser pistol, was subsequently recovered from the creek [14][17].

## Legal Proceedings

### Investigation and Indictment
Following his arrest, Patler was charged with the murder of George Lincoln Rockwell. The investigation was led by the Arlington County Police, with Commonwealth's Attorney William J. Hassan handling the prosecution [14][18]. The evidence against Patler was primarily circumstantial, as no witnesses directly identified him as the shooter on the roof, though witnesses did identify him fleeing the area [10][14].

A grand jury indicted Patler on charges of first-degree murder in September 1967 [12]. He pleaded not guilty.

### Trial
The trial began on November 27, 1967, in the Arlington County Circuit Court, presided over by Judge Charles S. Russell [12][14]. The proceedings were held under tight security due to the high profile of the victim and the potential for violence involving neo-Nazi supporters [12].

**Prosecution Case:**
Prosecutor William J. Hassan sought the death penalty [14]. The prosecution argued that Patler was motivated by revenge following his expulsion from the party and his personal grievances with Rockwell [10]. They presented evidence regarding the June assassination attempt, suggesting a pattern of premeditated intent [10]. Physical evidence included the wet clothing worn by Patler at the time of his arrest and the recovery of the murder weapon, which prosecutors linked to Patler through test firings conducted on his father-in-law's property [14].

**Defense Case:**
Patler was represented by attorneys Helen Lane, Thomas Harrigan, and Thomas Morris [10][14]. The defense argued that the evidence was entirely circumstantial and that Patler was not the only person with a motive to kill Rockwell. They suggested that other party members, specifically Matt Koehl, stood to gain from Rockwell's death and knew of his movements that day [10][14]. Patler testified in his own defense, claiming he had been at the bus stop following a family dispute and denied involvement in the shooting [10][14].

### Verdict and Sentencing
On December 15, 1967, after four hours of deliberation, the jury of ten men and two women found John Patler guilty of first-degree murder [9][14]. Despite the prosecution's request for capital punishment, the jury recommended a sentence of 20 years in prison, which was the minimum sentence for first-degree murder in Virginia at the time [10][12].

Judge Russell formally sentenced Patler to 20 years in prison on February 23, 1968 [12].

### Appeals
Patler appealed his conviction to the Supreme Court of Virginia, which upheld the verdict and sentence on November 30, 1970 [12][13]. He subsequently appealed to the Supreme Court of the United States, which unanimously rejected his appeal in May 1972 [10][12].

## Funeral Controversy

The disposition of George Lincoln Rockwell's remains became a source of significant public and legal controversy immediately following his death.

### National Cemetery Request
As an honorably discharged veteran who had achieved the rank of Commander in the U.S. Navy, Rockwell was legally eligible for burial in a national cemetery [1][14]. The Department of Defense initially approved a request for his burial at Culpeper National Cemetery in Virginia [19][20].

However, the approval came with strict conditions. Army officials, citing regulations, ruled that while Rockwell could be buried in his uniform, no swastikas, Nazi flags, or other political insignia would be permitted at the grave site or during the ceremony [20].

### The Standoff at Culpeper
On August 29, 1967, a funeral procession organized by the NSWPP arrived at the gates of Culpeper National Cemetery. The hearse was accompanied by approximately 50 party members, many wearing Nazi uniforms and swastika armbands [19][20]. Matt Koehl, Rockwell's successor, rode in the hearse clutching a copy of *Mein Kampf* [19].

When the procession attempted to enter, they were stopped by U.S. military police and the cemetery superintendent, who enforced the ban on Nazi insignia. The party members refused to remove their armbands, leading to a tense five-hour standoff at the cemetery gates [19]. During the confrontation, the Nazis attempted to force their way in, resulting in the arrest of three party members [20].

Major General Carl Turner, Provost Marshal General of the Army, subsequently canceled the approval for the burial due to the group's refusal to comply with the regulations [20]. The hearse was forced to turn back, and Rockwell's body was returned to Arlington.

### Cremation
Following the failed burial attempt, the American Civil Liberties Union (ACLU) briefly represented the party in challenging the Army's decision, but the legal battle was abandoned [21]. On August 30, 1967, Rockwell's body was secretly cremated at a funeral home [10][19]. A memorial service was held at the party headquarters. The ashes were taken into the custody of Matt Koehl and the NSWPP [19].

## Aftermath and Legacy

### Fate of the Party
Rockwell's death left a power vacuum within the American neo-Nazi movement. Matt Koehl succeeded Rockwell as the commander of the NSWPP [1][5]. Under Koehl's leadership, the party moved in a more esoteric and quasi-religious direction, eventually renaming itself the "New Order" [1][5].

The loss of Rockwell's charismatic leadership style led to significant fracturing within the movement. Prominent members, dissatisfied with Koehl's direction, split off to form their own organizations. Most notably, William Luther Pierce left to found the National Alliance, which became one of the most active white supremacist groups in the United States in the following decades [1][2].

### John Patler's Later Years
John Patler served nearly eight years of his sentence. He was paroled in August 1975 [6][12]. However, he violated the terms of his parole in 1976 and was returned to prison to serve an additional six years [6][12].

Following his final release, Patler legally reverted to his birth name, John Patsalos [9][12]. He largely disappeared from the public eye, working as a commercial artist and attempting to distance himself from his neo-Nazi past [22].

### Cultural Impact
The assassination of George Lincoln Rockwell remains a significant event in the history of American political extremism. Rockwell is often regarded as a martyr by white supremacists, and his writings, particularly his book *White Power* (published posthumously), continue to influence neo-Nazi ideology [1][3]. The assassination demonstrated the volatility and internal violence often present within fringe political movements.
