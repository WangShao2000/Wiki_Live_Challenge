# Edwardsville Amazon warehouse collapse

**Key Points**
*   **Event:** On December 10, 2021, an EF3 tornado struck an Amazon delivery station (DLI4) in Edwardsville, Illinois, causing a partial structural collapse.
*   **Casualties:** Six people were killed, and several others were injured. The victims were primarily contracted delivery drivers.
*   **Investigation:** The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) investigated the incident. While Amazon was not fined for the collapse itself, OSHA issued a Hazard Alert Letter recommending improvements to severe weather emergency procedures.
*   **Structural Factors:** The building utilized "tilt-up" concrete wall construction. Investigations raised questions regarding the anchoring of support columns, though the building met the codes in effect at the time of construction.
*   **Legislative Outcome:** In response to the disaster, Illinois passed the **Warehouse Safety Tornado Preparedness Act** in 2025, mandating tornado safety plans and requiring storm shelters in newly constructed warehouses.
*   **Status:** The facility was rebuilt and reopened in September 2024.


The **Edwardsville Amazon warehouse collapse** was a structural failure resulting from a severe weather event that occurred on the night of December 10, 2021, in Edwardsville, Illinois. An EF3 tornado struck the Amazon delivery station known as DLI4, causing a significant portion of the facility's roof and concrete walls to collapse [1][2]. The incident resulted in the deaths of six workers and injuries to several others [1][3].

The collapse drew national attention to warehouse safety standards, the structural integrity of "tilt-up" construction methods, and the emergency protocols of major corporations during severe weather events [4][5]. Following the disaster, the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) conducted an investigation, which concluded without issuing citations for the collapse itself but resulted in a Hazard Alert Letter recommending improvements to Amazon's emergency action plans [6][7].

In the years following the event, the victims' families filed multiple wrongful death lawsuits against Amazon and the construction firms involved [8][9]. The tragedy also spurred legislative action in Illinois, leading to the enactment of the Warehouse Safety Tornado Preparedness Act in August 2025, which introduced stricter safety requirements for warehouse operators across the state [10]. The damaged facility was rebuilt and resumed operations in September 2024 [11].

## Background

### Facility Details
The facility, identified as **DLI4**, was an Amazon delivery station located at 3077 Gateway Commerce Center Drive East in Edwardsville, Illinois, approximately 25 miles east of St. Louis [1][2]. Delivery stations serve as the final node in Amazon's logistics network, where packages are sorted and loaded onto vans for "last-mile" delivery to customers. The building was part of the Lakeview Commerce Center and was leased by Amazon. It opened around 2016 or 2017 as part of an expansion that brought approximately 1,000 jobs to the region [12].

### Construction
The warehouse was constructed using a method known as **tilt-up construction**. In this method, large concrete wall panels are cast horizontally on the ground and then lifted ("tilted") into a vertical position by cranes to form the building's exterior walls [5][13]. This technique is widely used for warehouses and distribution centers due to its cost-effectiveness and speed of construction [14]. The DLI4 facility covered approximately 1.1 million square feet [15].

### Employment
At the time of the collapse, the facility was staffed by a mix of direct Amazon employees and contracted delivery drivers. Many of the workers present on the evening of December 10 were independent contractors working for Delivery Service Partners (DSPs) such as Xseed Delivery, AB&C D.A.D Inc., and Boxify Logistics [2][7]. The workforce was operating during the peak holiday season, processing a high volume of packages.

## Event Timeline

### Meteorological Context
On the evening of December 10, 2021, a significant tornado outbreak affected the central and southern United States. The National Weather Service (NWS) issued a tornado warning for the Edwardsville area at approximately 8:06 p.m. CST, as severe storms moved through the region [2][16].

### The Collapse
At approximately 8:27 p.m. CST, an EF3 tornado struck the DLI4 facility [1][6]. The tornado, packing winds estimated at up to 150 mph, caused catastrophic damage to the structure [4][17]. A section of the building approximately 150 yards long collapsed [1].

The impact caused the roof to fail, which in turn destabilized the concrete walls. The walls on the west side of the building collapsed inward. Reports indicated that the columns supporting the roof were lifted out of the floor, described by a fire marshal as resembling "a peg coming out of a hole," leading to the structural failure [4].

### Emergency Response
When the tornado warning was issued, workers were directed to shelter in designated areas, which were the restrooms located on the north and south sides of the building. The north side of the building remained largely intact, and workers sheltering there survived. However, the south side of the building, where other workers had taken shelter in a bathroom, took a direct hit and suffered the most severe collapse [2][6].

Emergency responders from Edwardsville and surrounding agencies in the St. Louis region arrived shortly after the collapse. The response initially focused on search and rescue, with crews working through unstable debris to locate survivors. Forty-five people were rescued from the building, and one person was airlifted to a hospital [3][18]. By the following day, operations transitioned from rescue to recovery as authorities determined no further survivors were likely to be found [1].

## Casualties

Six individuals were killed in the collapse. The victims ranged in age from 26 to 62 and included both Amazon employees and contracted delivery drivers. The Madison County Coroner identified the victims as:

*   **Clayton Lynn Cope**, 29, of Alton, Illinois (Maintenance worker) [1][19]
*   **Kevin D. Dickey**, 62, of Carlyle, Illinois (Dispatcher) [1][3]
*   **Etheria S. Hebb**, 34, of St. Louis, Missouri (Delivery driver) [1][17]
*   **Austin J. McEwen**, 26, of Edwardsville, Illinois (Delivery driver) [1][3]
*   **Deandre S. Morrow**, 28, of St. Louis, Missouri (Delivery driver) [1][3]
*   **Larry E. Virden**, 46, of Collinsville, Illinois (Delivery driver) [1][3]

Austin McEwen was reported to have died while trying to shelter in the bathroom with colleagues [3]. Clayton Cope had reportedly warned others to seek shelter before the storm hit [19].

## Investigations

### OSHA Investigation
The U.S. Department of Labor's Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) opened an investigation into the collapse immediately following the incident. The investigation concluded on April 26, 2022 [6][7].

**Findings:**
OSHA did not issue any citations or fines to Amazon regarding the structural collapse itself, determining that the company's severe weather emergency procedures met minimal federal safety guidelines [6][7]. However, OSHA issued a **Hazard Alert Letter** to Amazon, identifying several areas of concern and recommending voluntary improvements.

**Key Issues Identified:**
1.  **Megaphone Inaccessibility:** A megaphone intended to be used to communicate emergency instructions was locked in a cage and inaccessible during the event [2].
2.  **Shelter Confusion:** Some employees were unsure of the location of the designated shelter areas. The investigation noted that the facility did not have a purpose-built storm shelter; rather, restrooms were designated as "shelter-in-place" locations [6][20].
3.  **Drill Participation:** Investigators found that some workers, particularly independent contractors, did not recall participating in severe weather drills or were not fully aware of the emergency action plan [6][21].

OSHA recommended that Amazon ensure all employees, including contractors, participate in drills and that audible warning devices be readily accessible [7].

### Structural Analysis
Following the collapse, questions were raised regarding the building's construction. A local fire marshal noted that the support columns appeared to be unanchored to the floor, relying on gravity and the weight of the roof to stay in place. This observation led to criticism that the "tilt-up" construction method, while code-compliant at the time, lacked sufficient resilience against significant uplift forces generated by a tornado [4].

Engineers noted that while the building met the 2006 International Building Code (IBC) standards applicable when it was built, it was not designed to withstand the wind loads of an EF3 tornado, nor was it required to be [2][5].

## Legal Proceedings

### Wrongful Death Lawsuits
In January 2022, the family of Austin McEwen filed the first wrongful death lawsuit against Amazon, Contegra Construction (the builder), and other entities involved in the facility's development [8][22]. The lawsuit alleged that Amazon failed to evacuate workers or provide a safe shelter, prioritizing profits over safety. It specifically claimed that Amazon directed workers to shelter in a restroom that the company knew or should have known was unsafe [8].

Subsequent lawsuits were filed by the families of other victims, including Deandre Morrow and Clayton Cope [23][24]. These suits generally alleged negligence in the building's construction and Amazon's emergency response protocols.

### Litigation Status
As of late 2022, some lawsuits against architecture and engineering firms (Stock & Associates Consulting Engineers Inc. and Gray Design Group Inc.) were voluntarily dismissed by the plaintiffs, though litigation continued against Amazon and Contegra Construction [25]. The cases were consolidated in Madison County circuit court [25].

### Regulatory Settlements
In a separate but related regulatory development, Amazon reached a settlement with OSHA in December 2024 regarding ergonomic safety violations at multiple facilities, including sites in Illinois. While not directly stemming from the tornado collapse, this settlement of $145,000 highlighted ongoing scrutiny of Amazon's workplace safety practices [26][27].

## Legislative Response

The collapse served as a catalyst for legislative reform regarding warehouse safety in Illinois.

### Warehouse Safety Tornado Preparedness Act
In response to the tragedy, Illinois lawmakers introduced and passed **House Bill 2987**, known as the **Warehouse Safety Tornado Preparedness Act**. The bill was signed into law by Governor J.B. Pritzker on August 15, 2025 [10].

**Key Provisions:**
*   **Mandatory Safety Plans:** All warehouse operators in Illinois are required to develop a specific tornado safety plan. Existing warehouses had 120 days from the law's effective date to comply [10][28].
*   **Shelter Requirements:** Newly constructed warehouses are required to include a designated storm shelter or "safe room" capable of housing the facility's population [10][29].
*   **Inspector Certification:** The law mandates that by January 1, 2027, city and county building inspectors must hold certifications from the International Code Council (ICC) [10][30].
*   **Communication:** Plans must include protocols for communicating with workers and local emergency services [10].

The legislation was advocated for by the families of the victims, particularly the Cope family, who participated in task force meetings to shape the bill [29].

### Other Legislation
In 2025, Governor Pritzker vetoed a separate bill (HB 2547) that aimed to limit productivity quotas in warehouses, a measure supported by unions but opposed by industry groups. This bill was distinct from the tornado safety legislation [31][32].

## Reconstruction and Reopening

Following the investigation, the owner of the property applied to rebuild the facility. The City of Edwardsville mandated that the reconstruction adhere to the 2015 International Building Code, an update from the 2006 code used for the original structure [2].

The warehouse was rebuilt to "pre-loss conditions" regarding its general layout, a decision that initially drew criticism from federal lawmakers, including Senator Elizabeth Warren and Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, who questioned why a dedicated storm shelter was not being added during the rebuild [20].

**Reopening:**
The facility officially reopened on September 4, 2024, marking exactly 1,000 days since the tornado [2][11].

**Safety Upgrades:**
*   **Wind Resistance:** The rebuilt structure was designed to withstand wind gusts of up to 114 mph, exceeding the previous specification of 90 mph [2][11].
*   **Sheltering:** While a dedicated FEMA-rated storm bunker was not constructed, Amazon stated that it worked with third-party engineers to identify the "optimal refuge locations" within the facility. These areas were reinforced to meet safety guidance [11][33].
*   **Procedures:** Amazon implemented new safety measures, including providing employees with lanyards containing emergency information and hiring meteorologists to monitor weather threats [11].

## Aftermath and Legacy

### Community Response
The Edwardsville community and Amazon established various forms of support for the victims' families. Amazon donated $1 million to the Edwardsville Community Foundation and provided funds to local public bodies for emergency equipment [15][17]. Scholarships were established in the names of the victims, including a donation to Southern Illinois University Edwardsville [17].

### Criticism
Amazon faced criticism immediately following the event. Founder Jeff Bezos was criticized for attending a Blue Origin rocket launch and posting about it on social media shortly after the collapse, before publicly addressing the tragedy in Edwardsville [2]. Labor unions and safety advocates used the incident to argue that Amazon's policies, such as restrictions on personal mobile phones (which workers argued prevented them from receiving weather alerts), compromised safety. Amazon clarified that the phone ban had been suspended during the pandemic and was not in effect at the time of the collapse [3].

### Memorials
On the first anniversary of the collapse in 2022, families and community members gathered to honor the victims. A permanent memorial has been discussed by the company and local officials [17].
