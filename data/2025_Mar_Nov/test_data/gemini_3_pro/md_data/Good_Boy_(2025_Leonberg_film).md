# Good Boy (2025 Leonberg film)

**Good Boy** is a 2025 American supernatural horror film directed by Ben Leonberg. The film stars Leonberg's own Nova Scotia Duck Tolling Retriever, Indy, in the lead role, alongside human actors Shane Jensen, Arielle Friedman, and Larry Fessenden. The narrative follows a loyal dog named Indy and his owner, Todd, who move into a rural family home where the dog perceives a malevolent presence that his owner cannot see.

The film is notable for being presented primarily from the perspective of the dog, utilizing low camera angles and limited dialogue to simulate a canine point of view. Leonberg co-wrote the screenplay with Alex Cannon and produced the film with his wife, Kari Fischer, under their production company, What's Wrong With Your Dog? Filming took place over three years in Harding Township, New Jersey, on a micro-budget of approximately $70,000.

*Good Boy* premiered at the South by Southwest (SXSW) Film & TV Festival on March 8, 2025. It was released theatrically in the United States on October 3, 2025, by IFC Films, followed by a streaming release on Shudder. The film was a commercial success relative to its budget, grossing over $8.7 million worldwide. It received generally positive reviews from critics, who praised the innovative perspective, the technical craftsmanship, and Indy's performance, though some reviewers critiqued the thinness of the narrative.

## Plot

Todd, a young man suffering from a chronic and debilitating lung disease, relocates from New York City to his late grandfather's uninhabited rural home in the woods. He is accompanied by his supportive and loyal dog, a Nova Scotia Duck Tolling Retriever named Indy. Todd's sister, Vera, expresses concern regarding Todd's decision to isolate himself, citing family rumors that the house is haunted and may have contributed to their grandfather's mysterious death.

Upon arriving, Todd attempts to settle in, using a generator supplied by a neighbor, Richard, to power the dilapidated house. While Todd occupies himself with watching old home videos of his grandfather, Indy immediately senses a hostile presence. The dog observes supernatural manifestations that Todd is unable to perceive, including shifting shadows and a dark, skeletal figure often drenched in mud. Indy also experiences nightmares involving Bandit, the Golden Retriever that belonged to Todd's grandfather, which appear to serve as spectral warnings.

As Todd's health deteriorates, he becomes increasingly oblivious to the danger surrounding them. Indy attempts to alert Todd to the presence of the entity through barking and whining, but his warnings go unheeded or are misinterpreted as behavioral issues. The supernatural force grows more aggressive, tormenting Indy and drawing closer to the weakened Todd.

In the film's climax, the dark entity manifests in Todd's bedroom while he is in a fragile physical state. Indy attempts to comfort and protect his owner. Todd eventually succumbs to the entity; realizing he has died, he is dragged by the figure through the house and toward the basement. Indy chases them into the cellar in a final attempt to rescue his master. Todd, accepting his fate, tells Indy he is a "good boy" before being pulled into a dark tunnel. The following morning, Vera arrives at the house to find Todd's lifeless body and Indy sitting alone at the entrance of the cellar, having survived the ordeal.

## Production

### Development
Director Ben Leonberg conceived the idea for *Good Boy* after re-watching the 1982 film *Poltergeist*. He noted that the family dog in that film sensed the ghosts before the human characters did, prompting him to consider a haunted house narrative told entirely from a dog's perspective [1][2]. Leonberg, a dog owner himself, was interested in the "painful space between knowing and powerlessness" that pets experience when they sense danger but cannot communicate it to their owners [2].

Leonberg co-wrote the script with Alex Cannon beginning in 2017 [1]. Because the protagonist was a dog, the writers could not rely on traditional dialogue to advance the plot. Instead, they focused on action, sensory details, and perspective to tell the story [1]. The script was designed to emphasize the limitations of a household pet, avoiding "superpowers" or voice-over narration in favor of a realistic canine experience [2].

### Filming
The film was produced on a micro-budget of approximately $70,000 [1][3][4]. Principal photography took place in a house in Harding Township, New Jersey, over a period of three years, totaling roughly 400 shooting days [1][4][5].

The production crew was minimal, often consisting only of Leonberg, his wife and producer Kari Fischer, and the dog, Indy [1][6]. This small footprint allowed the team to work around the dog's schedule and mood without the pressure of a large crew [6]. Leonberg and Fischer lived in the house during production [1]. To assist with framing and lighting setups without exhausting the animal actor, a stuffed replica of the dog, nicknamed "Findy" (Fake Indy), was used as a stand-in [1].

### Cinematography
To simulate the dog's perspective, Leonberg (who also served as cinematographer) utilized specific visual techniques. The camera was frequently placed at a low angle to match Indy's eye level [7][8]. Consequently, human characters are often framed from the waist down or obscured by shadows, with their faces rarely shown unless Indy looks up at them [6][9][10]. Wide-angle lenses were employed to capture the dog's surroundings, creating a distorted, vast look that emphasized the animal's small stature within the environment [2][10].

### Animal training
Indy, the director's real-life pet, had no formal acting training prior to the film [1][7]. Leonberg and Fischer trained Indy over the course of the three-year production [11]. The performance relied on capturing natural behaviors rather than complex tricks, with Leonberg noting that the dog's "intense, unblinking stare" and expressive movements were integral to the characterization [1]. No computer-generated imagery (CGI) was used to enhance or alter Indy's performance [8][12].

## Release

*Good Boy* had its world premiere at the South by Southwest (SXSW) Film & TV Festival on March 8, 2025, as part of the "Midnighters" section [1][13][14]. It subsequently screened at the Overlook Film Festival, the Calgary Underground Film Festival, and the Fantasia International Film Festival [1][2][5].

The film was distributed in the United States by IFC Films and its horror streaming division, Shudder [15][16]. Although initially planned for a limited release, the film's trailer and poster went viral, generating significant online interest and speculation regarding the dog's fate [10]. This popularity led to a wider theatrical release on October 3, 2025 [1][17].

The film was released on video-on-demand (VOD) platforms on October 23, 2025, and became available for streaming on Shudder on November 21, 2025 [1][16]. In the United Kingdom and Ireland, the film was released by Vertigo Releasing on October 10, 2025 [1].

## Reception

### Box office
*Good Boy* was a commercial success, particularly given its low production costs. In its opening weekend in the United States, the film grossed $2.3 million from 1,650 theaters [1][4]. This marked the third-largest opening weekend in the history of IFC Films, behind *Late Night with the Devil* and *Clown in a Cornfield* [4][12].

The film continued to perform well, earning $5.59 million domestically by its third weekend [1]. As of December 2025, *Good Boy* has grossed approximately $8.7 million worldwide [1][18]. Reports indicate that the film recouped its production budget of $70,000 shortly after its release [3][4].

### Critical response
On the review aggregator website Rotten Tomatoes, *Good Boy* holds an approval rating of 89% based on reviews from critics [3][16].

Critics generally praised the film's unique premise and technical execution. Writing for *RogerEbert.com*, a reviewer described the film as an "ingenious canine horror film" and a "transportive work," highlighting a dream sequence that pulls the viewer into the dog's mind [19]. *Flickering Myth* commended the film for avoiding the "gimmick" trap, stating that Indy delivers a "legitimately emotive and often striking performance" [11]. *Screen Rant* noted that the film "breaks away from traditional genre tropes" and offers a "haunting and boldly imaginative experience" [16].

The decision to limit the human perspective was frequently highlighted as a strength. *Bloody Disgusting* (via *ScreenAnarchy*) noted that the film feels "almost like a first-person shooter" due to the camera angles, creating a unique viewing experience [20]. *PopHorror* praised the film for effectively showcasing the helplessness of the dog, serving as a metaphor for the owner's illness [21].

However, some critics felt the narrative was slight. A review in *RogerEbert.com* noted that the film "could've been equally as effective as a 40-minute short," suggesting that the scares became repetitive over the 73-minute runtime [19]. Similarly, *Cinelinx* stated that while the film is a "fun experiment," the story "feels a little thin" [22].

Indy's performance was universally acclaimed, with *The Rolling Tape* calling it "one of the most deeply moving performances of the entire year" [7].
