# Conscription in Rhodesia

**Conscription in Rhodesia** was a mandatory military service system utilized by the government of Southern Rhodesia (later Rhodesia and Zimbabwe Rhodesia) to maintain its security forces. While forms of conscription existed from the 1920s, the system was significantly expanded following the Unilateral Declaration of Independence (UDI) in 1965 and the subsequent escalation of the Rhodesian Bush War. Initially applied only to white males, the conscription apparatus became increasingly aggressive as the conflict intensified, extending service durations, widening age brackets to include men up to age 60 (often referred to as "Dad's Army"), and eventually incorporating Asian, Coloured, and black Rhodesians.

The conscription system was a critical component of the white minority government's strategy to counter the insurgency led by the Zimbabwe African National Liberation Army (ZANLA) and the Zimbabwe People's Revolutionary Army (ZIPRA). By the late 1970s, the demands of the war caused severe economic disruption and contributed to high rates of white emigration. The system was characterized by a complex structure of "National Service" for young men and periodic "camps" for reservists, creating a society that was heavily militarized. Despite these measures, manpower shortages remained a chronic issue for the Rhodesian Security Forces until the country's transition to independence as Zimbabwe in 1980.

## History and Legislation

### Early Legislation (1926–1955)
Compulsory military service in the territory was first established under the **Defence Act of 1926**. This legislation stipulated that all European males aged 18 and older were liable for military call-up [1]. The Act provided for the creation of a permanent territorial force, which included two battalions and a cadet corps [1].

During World War II, the Southern Rhodesian government passed the **National Services (Armed Forces) Act of 1940**. This legislation allowed authorities to conscript any male British subject of European ancestry aged between 19 and 25 who had resided in the colony for at least six months. In 1942, the minimum age was lowered to 18 [2]. Additionally, part-time training became compulsory for all white males between the ages of 18 and 55, with exceptions only for those in essential "reserved occupations" [2]. While black Rhodesians were recruited into the Rhodesian African Rifles (RAR) during the war, this was nominally voluntary, although conscription was technically extended to black men in 1940 for specific service [3].

Following World War II, conscription was suspended but was reintroduced in 1955 (implemented in 1957) under a new **Defence Act**. This required all young white men to undergo a period of National Service, initially set at four and a half months of basic training [3][4].

### Post-UDI Escalation (1965–1972)
Following the Unilateral Declaration of Independence in 1965, the Rhodesian government tightened conscription laws to secure manpower for the growing counter-insurgency operations. In 1966, the government altered the liability criteria, basing conscription primarily on residence rather than citizenship. This change meant that British subjects and South Africans living in Rhodesia were required to register for service alongside Rhodesian citizens [3].

The duration of National Service was progressively extended. By 1966, the full-time service period was increased to 35 weeks [3]. By 1972, as the Bush War entered a more intense phase, all white men aged 18 to 25 were required to undertake 12 months of continuous training and service [5][6].

## Structure of Service

The Rhodesian conscription system was divided into two main categories: initial **National Service** for young men and ongoing **Territorial Force** obligations for older reservists.

### National Service
Young men, typically upon leaving school at age 18, were drafted for a continuous period of full-time military duty. This intake was the primary source of manpower for the regular army and police units.
*   **Duration:** The period of service rose from 4.5 months in the 1960s to 12 months in 1972 [3][5]. In 1976, the mandatory period was extended further to 18 months to cope with the intensifying war [5][7].
*   **Deployment:** Conscripts were deployed into various units, including the Rhodesia Regiment, the British South Africa Police (BSAP), and eventually specialized units. By 1976, the government banned boys aged 16 and over from leaving the country to ensure they would be available for the draft [3][6].

### Reserve Obligations
After completing their National Service, men were transferred to the Territorial Force or Reserve, where they remained liable for call-ups for several years.
*   **Camp System:** Reservists were subject to periodic "call-ups" or "camps." Initially, this involved a few weeks per year, but as the security situation deteriorated, the frequency and duration increased.
*   **Continuous Call-up:** In 1976, the system of "continuous call-up" was introduced. Men aged 25 to 38 were required to serve up to 190 days per year, often in rotating blocks (e.g., six weeks on, six weeks off) [5][7]. Men aged 38 to 50 were liable for up to 70 days of service annually [5][7].

### "Dad's Army"
As manpower reserves dwindled, the government extended conscription to older age groups, leading to the formation of units colloquially known as "Dad's Army."
*   **38–50 Age Group:** In 1977, conscription was extended to men up to age 50. These men were often drafted into the Police Reserve or static guard duties to release younger men for combat roles [6][8].
*   **50–60 Age Group:** In January 1979, the liability was further extended to men aged 50 to 59. These conscripts, sometimes referred to as "Mashford's Militia" (after a local funeral parlor), were required to perform six weeks of service per year, typically guarding urban installations or agricultural assets [3][5].

## Expansion to Non-White Populations

For the majority of the Rhodesian Bush War, conscription applied primarily to the white minority. However, as the insurgency grew, the government sought to tap into other demographic groups.

### Asian and Coloured Conscripts
Asian and Coloured (mixed-race) men were liable for conscription under the same general terms as whites, although they were often segregated into specific units.
*   **Roles:** Historically, during World War II, Coloured units served in transport and logistics roles [9]. During the Bush War, they were frequently assigned to the **Rhodesia Defence Regiment (RDR)** or protection companies, tasked with guarding convoys and infrastructure [3][10].
*   **Integration:** While the regular army had black battalions (such as the Rhodesian African Rifles), the conscripted elements of the Asian and Coloured communities were often utilized in support roles to free up white infantry for frontline operations [3].

### Conscription of Black Rhodesians
The question of conscripting black Rhodesians—who made up the vast majority of the population—was politically sensitive. For most of the war, black soldiers in the Rhodesian Security Forces were volunteers, comprising a significant portion of the regular army [3][11].

*   **1978 Policy Change:** In late 1978, facing critical manpower shortages, the Transitional Government announced that black Rhodesians would be drafted. The initial call-up targeted educated black men aged 18 to 25 (those with at least three years of secondary education) [3][5].
*   **Implementation and Resistance:** The registration deadline was set for December 1978, with service to begin in January 1979. The measure faced widespread opposition. Protests broke out in Salisbury (now Harare) and Bulawayo, with demonstrators burning registration papers [12].
*   **Failure:** The call-up was largely ineffective. Of the approximately 25,000 black men estimated to be eligible, a significant number ignored the call-up. In the first intake of January 1979, only 300 reported out of 1,544 called [3].

## Socio-Economic Impact

The aggressive conscription policies had profound effects on Rhodesian society and its economy.

### Economic Disruption
The "continuous call-up" system severely disrupted the economy. With men aged 18 to 38 absent from their civilian jobs for up to six months a year, businesses faced chronic staff shortages [10][13]. The burden fell disproportionately on the small white population, which provided the bulk of the skilled workforce and managerial class. The constant rotation of men between civilian life and the bush destabilized industries and strained the financial resources of families [3][10].

### Emigration
Conscription was a major driver of white emigration, often referred to as the "Chicken Run."
*   **Statistics:** Net emigration of whites accelerated in the late 1970s. In the first nine months of 1978 alone, over 11,000 whites emigrated [14].
*   **Youth Exodus:** To prevent young men from fleeing to avoid the draft, the government imposed strict travel restrictions. From 1974, men aged 18 to 25 were barred from leaving the country without completing their service [3][6]. Nevertheless, many young men left, and the inability to retain manpower became a vicious cycle that further weakened the state's capacity to wage war [15].

## Evasion and Objection

### Draft Evasion
As the war dragged on and the political future of Rhodesia became uncertain, draft evasion increased. By 1973, it was reported that nearly half of the eligible men called up managed to avoid service through various means [3]. By the late 1970s, the administrative machinery of the state struggled to enforce call-ups, particularly among the newly liable black population, where the boycott was widespread [3].

### Conscientious Objection
Rhodesia had limited provisions for conscientious objection. The most prominent group to refuse service on religious grounds were **Jehovah's Witnesses**.
*   **Persecution:** Jehovah's Witnesses refused to participate in military service or political activities due to their doctrine of neutrality. Consequently, they faced imprisonment and detention. Many were held in military detention barracks, where they were often subjected to harsh treatment [16][17].
*   **Legal Status:** Unlike some Western nations that offered alternative civilian service, the Rhodesian government generally viewed refusal to serve as a criminal offense. Objectors were often sentenced to repeat terms of imprisonment if they continued to refuse call-ups after their release [17].
