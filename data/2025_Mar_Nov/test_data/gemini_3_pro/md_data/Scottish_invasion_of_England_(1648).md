# Scottish invasion of England (1648)

The **Scottish invasion of England** in 1648 was a military campaign undertaken by the Kingdom of Scotland on behalf of King Charles I during the Second English Civil War. The invasion was initiated by a faction of the Scottish Covenanters known as the "Engagers," led by James Hamilton, 1st Duke of Hamilton. The campaign aimed to restore the King to the English throne following his defeat in the First English Civil War.

The invasion force crossed the border in July 1648 but was hampered by poor logistics, internal political dissent within Scotland, and adverse weather conditions. The campaign culminated in the Battle of Preston (17–19 August 1648), where the Engager army was decisively defeated by the New Model Army under the command of Oliver Cromwell and John Lambert. The defeat resulted in the surrender of the Scottish forces, the execution of Charles I and the Duke of Hamilton, and a shift in power within Scotland from the Engagers to the radical Kirk Party.

## Background

### The Engagement
Following his defeat in the First English Civil War, King Charles I was held prisoner by the English Parliament. While in custody at Carisbrooke Castle on the Isle of Wight, Charles negotiated secretly with a faction of the Scottish Covenanters [1]. On 26 December 1647, the King signed a secret treaty known as "The Engagement" [2][3].

Under the terms of this agreement, Charles promised to establish Presbyterianism in England for a trial period of three years and to suppress the Independent sectarians, who were dominant in the English New Model Army [1][2]. In return, the Scottish signatories promised to raise an army to invade England and restore the King to his throne [4][5].

### Internal Scottish Divisions
The Engagement caused a deep schism within Scotland. The Covenanter movement split into two primary factions: the **Engagers**, who supported the treaty and the restoration of the King, and the **Kirk Party** (or strict Covenanters), led by Archibald Campbell, 1st Marquess of Argyll [6][7].

The Kirk Party and the General Assembly of the Church of Scotland (the Kirk) denounced the Engagement as "sinful" because Charles I refused to take the Covenant himself [1][8]. Consequently, many experienced Scottish commanders, including Alexander Leslie, Earl of Leven, and David Leslie, refused to serve in the new army [8][9]. This forced the Engagers to appoint the Duke of Hamilton as general, despite his limited military experience compared to the veteran Covenanter generals [8].

## Prelude

### Mobilization
The levy of troops in Scotland was slow and difficult due to the opposition of the Kirk, which preached against the war [10][11]. The resulting army was ill-equipped, poorly trained, and lacked experienced officers [8][12]. The force was further complicated by the arrival of "New Scots" veterans from Ulster under Major General George Munro, who refused to accept subordination to Hamilton, leading to a disjointed command structure [13].

### Invasion
On 8 July 1648, the Scottish army crossed the border into England near Carlisle [13]. They were joined by English Royalist forces commanded by Sir Marmaduke Langdale, who had already seized Berwick and Carlisle in late April to facilitate the invasion [11][14].

The combined force is estimated by historians to have numbered between 18,000 and 24,000 men, although the effective fighting force was likely smaller due to the separation of Munro's corps and the straggling nature of the march [12][15]. The army advanced slowly through the wet summer weather, moving south through Lancashire rather than taking the route through Yorkshire [11].

## Campaign

### Parliamentarian Response
The English Parliamentarian response was initially led by Major General John Lambert, who commanded a small force of cavalry and dragoons. Lambert shadowed the advancing Scots, harassing their rearguard and gathering intelligence while avoiding a pitched battle against superior numbers [13][16].

Meanwhile, Lieutenant General Oliver Cromwell was engaged in suppressing a Royalist rebellion in South Wales. After the surrender of Pembroke Castle in mid-July, Cromwell marched his troops rapidly across England to join Lambert [11]. Despite his men being worn out and lacking shoes, they were re-equipped in the Midlands before rendezvousing with Lambert near Wetherby [16][17].

### Maneuvers
By mid-August, Hamilton's army was strung out over a distance of nearly 50 miles (80 km) along the line of march, with the cavalry often far separated from the infantry [12]. Hamilton was reportedly unaware of the close proximity of Cromwell's main force, believing the Parliamentarians were still at a distance [18].

Cromwell, having combined his forces with Lambert's, chose to cross the Pennines and attack the Scots from the flank rather than blocking their advance from the south [13][15]. This aggressive maneuver placed the Parliamentarian army on the Scottish line of communication and retreat.

## Battle of Preston

The decisive engagement of the invasion took place between 17 and 19 August 1648, around the town of Preston in Lancashire.

### 17 August: Preston Moor
On the morning of 17 August, Cromwell's forces encountered the English Royalist contingent under Marmaduke Langdale at Preston Moor [17]. Langdale's force, numbering approximately 3,500 men, attempted to hold their ground behind hedges and in enclosed fields [15][17]. Langdale sent urgent requests for support to Hamilton, but the Duke, believing the attack to be a feint, ordered the main body of the Scottish infantry to continue crossing the River Ribble, leaving Langdale isolated [15][19].

Despite fierce resistance, Langdale's forces were eventually overwhelmed by the veteran New Model Army infantry. The Parliamentarians then turned their attention to the Scottish forces remaining north of the river, driving them through the streets of Preston and seizing the bridge over the Ribble [15][19].

### 18–19 August: Retreat and Winwick
The Scottish army, now cut off from their base in Scotland, attempted to retreat south towards Wigan and Warrington. The retreat was conducted in torrential rain, which rendered much of the Scots' gunpowder useless and made movement difficult [17][19]. Folklore of the time associated the heavy rains with St. Swithin's Day (15 July), claiming that rain on that day predicted forty days of wet weather [20][21].

On 19 August, the pursuing Parliamentarians caught the Scottish rearguard at the Battle of Winwick. The Scottish infantry made a stand but were defeated after hours of fighting [13][22]. The remaining infantry surrendered at Warrington, while the cavalry, including the Duke of Hamilton, fled towards Uttoxeter [15][17].

## Surrender and Aftermath

### Capitulation
The Duke of Hamilton surrendered at Uttoxeter on 25 August 1648 [11][19]. The invasion force was effectively annihilated; estimates suggest approximately 2,000 Royalists were killed and up to 9,000 captured during the campaign [13][16].

### Fate of Prisoners
The treatment of the captured soldiers was harsh. Thousands of Scottish prisoners were sent to the English colonies in the Americas, specifically Barbados and Virginia, to work as indentured servants [12]. Others were conscripted into service for foreign powers, such as the Republic of Venice [13][19].

### Political Consequences in England
The defeat of the Engager invasion ended the Second English Civil War. The English Army leadership, exasperated by Charles I's attempts to provoke a new war while negotiating, resolved to bring the King to trial [23]. This led to Pride's Purge of the English Parliament, the trial of the King, and his execution on 30 January 1649 [12][13]. The Duke of Hamilton was also tried for treason and beheaded in March 1649 [13].

### Political Consequences in Scotland
The destruction of the Engager army fundamentally altered the balance of power in Scotland. The anti-Engagement faction, the Kirk Party, launched the "Whiggamore Raid" in September 1648 [9][24]. This was a march of Covenanters from the western shires to Edinburgh, which resulted in the seizure of the government [24].

The remaining Engager forces under the Earl of Lanark (Hamilton's brother) and George Munro briefly opposed the Whiggamores at the Battle of Stirling in September 1648 [6]. However, realizing their position was untenable, they agreed to the Treaty of Stirling on 27 September 1648 [25][26]. This treaty mandated the resignation of all Engagers from public office and established the dominance of the Kirk Party, which then opened friendly negotiations with Oliver Cromwell [9][24].
