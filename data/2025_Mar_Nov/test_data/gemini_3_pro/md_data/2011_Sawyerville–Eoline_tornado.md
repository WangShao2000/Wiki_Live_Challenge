# 2011 Sawyerville–Eoline tornado

The **2011 Sawyerville–Eoline tornado** was a long-tracked and destructive tornado that struck Central Alabama on the afternoon of April 27, 2011. Occurring as part of the 2011 Super Outbreak, the largest tornado outbreak in United States history, this storm was rated EF3 on the Enhanced Fujita scale. The tornado tracked for over 72 miles (116 km) through Greene, Hale, and Bibb counties, reaching a maximum path width of one mile (1.6 km) and estimated peak wind speeds of 145 mph (233 km/h). The storm resulted in seven fatalities and at least 50 injuries, causing extensive structural damage to homes, businesses, and the Eoline Volunteer Fire Department [1][2].

## Meteorological synopsis

The tornado occurred during the April 25–28, 2011 tornado outbreak, a historic severe weather event that produced 362 confirmed tornadoes across the southern, midwestern, and northeastern United States. April 27 was the peak of the outbreak, characterized by a volatile atmospheric environment with high levels of wind shear and instability [3][4].

The Sawyerville–Eoline tornado was one of 29 confirmed tornadoes to impact Central Alabama on that day. It touched down at approximately 5:30 p.m. CDT (22:30 UTC) and remained on the ground for nearly one hour and a half, dissipating at 6:55 p.m. CDT (23:55 UTC). The storm was one of several long-track supercells that traversed the state during the afternoon and evening "second wave" of the outbreak [1][2].

## Storm path and impact

The tornado followed a northeasterly path, affecting rural communities and forested areas across three counties. The National Weather Service (NWS) in Birmingham conducted damage surveys to determine the track and intensity of the storm [1].

### Greene County
The tornado first touched down in extreme southwest Greene County, near the Tombigbee River, west of the Tishabee community. The initial damage occurred along County Road 69 near Head Drive. In this early stage, the tornado was rated EF1 but quickly intensified to EF2 status as it moved northeast across rural areas north of Forkland. Damage in Greene County included the destruction or damage of at least four mobile homes, three outbuildings, and two churches. Numerous trees were also downed in this area. Two minor injuries were reported in the county before the tornado crossed the Black Warrior River and entered Hale County [1].

### Hale County
Upon entering Hale County west of Sawyerville, the tornado intensified significantly. As it crossed Alabama State Route 14 just north of Sawyerville, the tornado reached EF3 intensity with winds estimated at 145 mph. The storm continued northeast, crossing County Roads 18 and 21, Alabama State Route 69 south of Harper Hill, and County Road 29 east of Ingram [1].

This segment of the path experienced the most severe impacts in terms of casualties. Extensive structural damage occurred, with numerous mobile homes and single-family residences destroyed. One church was also destroyed in this area. Thousands of trees were snapped or uprooted along the path. Six of the seven fatalities associated with this tornado occurred in Hale County, along with at least 40 injuries [1][5].

### Bibb County and Eoline
The tornado moved from Hale County into the Talladega National Forest near Ingram, maintaining its strength as it crossed into Bibb County. It caused significant damage to timber within the national forest before approaching the community of Eoline.

In Eoline, the tornado inflicted significant structural damage consistent with an EF3 rating. Approximately one dozen mobile homes and single-family houses were destroyed, and numerous others sustained varying degrees of damage. The Eoline Volunteer Fire Department building was destroyed by the tornado. Twelve people survived the direct hit by taking shelter in the hallway of the fire station. However, one fatality occurred in Eoline involving a person in a vehicle near the destroyed fire department. At least 10 other injuries were noted in this area [1][6].

The tornado continued northeast, crossing Alabama State Route 5 south of West Blocton. By this point, the storm had weakened slightly to an EF2 rating with winds estimated at 125 mph. Several mobile homes and site-built homes near County Road 26 were destroyed or heavily damaged. The tornado continued to down thousands of trees as it weakened further, eventually lifting just northeast of the Marvel community near the Shelby County and Bibb County line [1].

## Casualties and damage summary

The National Weather Service confirmed seven fatalities and 50 injuries directly attributed to the tornado. Six deaths occurred in Hale County, and one death occurred in Bibb County [1][2].

**Summary of Statistics:**
*   **EF Rating:** EF3
*   **Estimated Peak Winds:** 145 mph (233 km/h)
*   **Path Length:** 72.13 miles (116.08 km)
*   **Maximum Width:** 1,760 yards (1.0 mile)
*   **Fatalities:** 7
*   **Injuries:** 50

The storm destroyed the Eoline Volunteer Fire Department and another business in Eoline. Across the entire path, hundreds of structures were impacted, and thousands of trees were downed, contributing to power outages and road closures in the region [1].

## Aftermath and recovery

Following the tornado, relief efforts were coordinated by local emergency management agencies and community organizations. The Greensboro Recreational Center in Greensboro, Alabama, served as a distribution point for food, clothing, and household items for victims [6].

The destruction caused by the 2011 outbreak, including the Sawyerville–Eoline tornado, highlighted the vulnerability of rural communities in the region, particularly those relying on mobile homes. In the years following the disaster, local officials and federal representatives prioritized the construction of community storm shelters.

In 2023 and 2024, Hale County secured federal grant money, facilitated by U.S. Representative Terri Sewell, to construct new community storm shelters. These efforts were driven by the history of severe weather in the area, including the 2011 tornado and subsequent storms in 2022 and 2023. Four new shelters were planned for the communities of Akron, Greensboro, Moundville, and Sawyerville to provide safe refuge for residents during future severe weather events [7][8][9][10]. By September 2024, shelters in Akron and Sawyerville were operational, with others in Moundville and Greensboro in various stages of completion [8].
