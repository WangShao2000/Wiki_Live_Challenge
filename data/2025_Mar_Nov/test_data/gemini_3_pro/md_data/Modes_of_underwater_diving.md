# Modes of underwater diving

**Modes of underwater diving** are the various methods and techniques used to descend below the water's surface. These modes are primarily distinguished by the type of breathing apparatus employed, the specific equipment configurations, and the physiological conditions to which the diver is exposed [1][2]. The fundamental classification divides diving into two categories: ambient pressure diving, where the diver is exposed to the direct pressure of the surrounding water, and atmospheric pressure diving, where the diver is isolated from the water pressure within a rigid suit or vehicle [1][2].

The choice of diving mode is determined by the purpose of the dive, such as recreational, commercial, scientific, or military applications, as well as the depth and duration required [3][4]. Ambient pressure modes include freediving, which relies on breath-holding, and compressed-gas diving, which utilizes equipment such as scuba or surface-supplied systems to deliver breathing gas [2]. Saturation diving is a specialized technique within ambient pressure diving designed to allow for extended work at great depths by eliminating the need for repetitive decompression [5]. Atmospheric pressure diving utilizes articulated suits, known as Atmospheric Diving Suits (ADS), which maintain an internal pressure of one atmosphere, thereby negating the physiological risks associated with high pressure, such as decompression sickness and nitrogen narcosis [6][7].

## Ambient pressure diving

In ambient pressure diving, the diver's body is exposed to the pressure of the surrounding water, which increases with depth [2]. To breathe effectively, the gas supplied to the diver must be at the same pressure as the surrounding environment [8]. This exposure necessitates the management of physiological effects such as gas absorption by body tissues, which can lead to decompression sickness if ascent is too rapid [9].

### Freediving
Freediving, also known as breath-hold diving or apnea, is the practice of diving without the use of external breathing apparatus [2]. It relies entirely on the diver's ability to hold their breath until resurfacing [1]. Freediving is considered the original form of diving and is utilized in activities ranging from recreational exploration and spearfishing to competitive sports [2][3].

In competitive freediving, various disciplines exist, including Constant Weight and Free Immersion [10]. The discipline known as "No Limit" apnea is considered the deepest and most extreme; it involves the use of a weighted sled to descend and a buoyancy device to ascend [11]. The current world record for a No Limit freedive is 253 meters (830 ft), set by Herbert Nitsch in 2012 [12]. However, this discipline carries significant risks, and Nitsch suffered severe decompression sickness following his record-breaking dive [12][13]. Due to the high level of danger, including fatalities, the discipline is not currently recognized by some governing bodies like AIDA [11].

### Scuba diving
Scuba diving (Self-Contained Underwater Breathing Apparatus) is a mode in which the diver carries their own source of breathing gas, typically compressed air or specialized gas mixtures, allowing for greater mobility and autonomy than surface-tethered systems [9][14]. Scuba is widely used for recreational, scientific, and military purposes [4].

Scuba equipment is generally categorized into open-circuit and closed-circuit systems. In open-circuit scuba, the diver inhales gas from a cylinder and exhales it into the water as bubbles [1]. Closed-circuit rebreathers recycle the diver's exhaled gas by removing carbon dioxide and replenishing oxygen, allowing for longer dive times and silent operation [15].

**Depth records**
While recreational scuba diving is typically limited to depths of 40 meters (130 ft) [2], technical divers utilize mixed gases (such as trimix) to reach significantly greater depths [16]. The world record for the deepest open-circuit scuba dive is 332.35 meters (1,090 ft), achieved by Ahmed Gabr in the Red Sea in 2014 [17][18]. The descent took approximately 15 minutes, while the ascent required over 13 hours of decompression to safely eliminate inert gases from the body [19][20].

### Surface-supplied diving
Surface-supplied diving (SSD) involves delivering breathing gas to the diver through an umbilical hose connected to a supply on the surface, such as a compressor or high-pressure storage tanks [14][21]. The umbilical often includes lines for communications, depth monitoring (pneumofathometer), and hot water for thermal protection [21].

This mode is the standard for commercial and military diving operations because it provides a virtually unlimited gas supply and increased safety through surface monitoring and hard-line communications [14][22]. Unlike scuba, where a diver's gas supply is finite and carried on their back, surface-supplied divers are tethered, which restricts mobility but enhances safety for stationary or localized work [14].

**Hookah diving**
A variation of surface-supplied diving known as "hookah" or "airline" diving uses a simple low-pressure compressor on the surface to supply air to a diver via a hose and a standard demand regulator [14]. This method is often used for shallow water activities such as boat hull cleaning, light commercial work, or recreational diving in limited depths [23][24]. Hookah systems are distinct from full commercial surface-supplied setups as they typically lack voice communications and emergency gas bailout systems [25].

### Saturation diving
Saturation diving is a specialized technique used for deep commercial and industrial work, such as offshore oil and gas construction [5]. It is based on the principle that after a certain period under pressure (typically 24 hours), a diver's tissues become fully saturated with inert gas [26]. Once saturated, the time required for decompression remains constant regardless of how much longer the diver stays at that depth [5][27].

In this mode, divers live in a pressurized habitat (saturation system) on a surface vessel or platform for days or weeks [5][28]. They transfer to the underwater work site via a pressurized diving bell [29]. This allows them to work at depths greater than 50 meters (160 ft) for extended periods without needing to decompress after every shift [5]. Decompression occurs only once at the end of the entire mission, which may last weeks [27].

**Records**
The deepest simulated saturation dive was the *Hydra 10* experiment conducted by Comex in 1992. During this onshore hyperbaric chamber experiment, diver Théo Mavrostomos reached a simulated depth of 701 meters (2,300 ft) using a breathing mixture known as hydreliox (hydrogen, helium, and oxygen) [30][31].

## Atmospheric pressure diving

Atmospheric pressure diving isolates the diver from the surrounding water pressure, maintaining an internal environment of one atmosphere (surface pressure) [6]. This is achieved using an Atmospheric Diving Suit (ADS), which is essentially a one-person articulated submersible resembling a suit of armor [7][32].

### Atmospheric Diving Suits (ADS)
An ADS is constructed with pressure-resistant materials and features elaborate rotary joints that allow the operator to move their limbs and perform tasks underwater while remaining at surface pressure [6][33]. Because the occupant is not exposed to increased ambient pressure, there is no risk of decompression sickness or nitrogen narcosis, and no need for slow decompression ascents [7][32]. This allows for rapid deployment and return from great depths [6].

**Notable systems**
*   **JIM suit:** An early successful ADS developed in the 1970s, capable of operating at depths up to 460 meters (1,500 ft) [6].
*   **Newtsuit:** Developed by Phil Nuytten in 1987, this suit utilizes rotary joints and is rated for depths of 300 meters (1,000 ft) [6].
*   **Exosuit:** A modern evolution of the Newtsuit, the Exosuit is rated to 300 meters (1,000 ft) and features thrusters for propulsion, allowing the pilot to maneuver mid-water [34][35]. It includes advanced telemetry and communication systems [33].
*   **ADS 2000:** Developed jointly with the US Navy, this suit was designed to meet submarine rescue requirements [36]. In 2006, Chief Navy Diver Daniel Jackson set a record by submerging to 610 meters (2,000 ft) in the ADS 2000 [37][38].

## Experimental modes

### Liquid breathing
Liquid breathing is a theoretical and experimental mode of diving in which the lungs are filled with an oxygen-rich liquid, such as a perfluorocarbon (PFC), rather than gas [39]. Because liquids are virtually incompressible, filling the lungs with fluid would theoretically allow a diver to withstand extreme ambient pressures without the need for high-pressure gases, thereby eliminating the risks of decompression sickness and nitrogen narcosis [40][41].

While the concept was popularized in science fiction, such as the film *The Abyss*, practical application in diving remains experimental [40][42]. Challenges include the high density of the liquid, which makes the mechanical work of breathing difficult, and the efficient removal of carbon dioxide [41]. Current research has focused primarily on medical applications, such as partial liquid ventilation for patients with severe lung trauma, rather than operational diving [39][41].
