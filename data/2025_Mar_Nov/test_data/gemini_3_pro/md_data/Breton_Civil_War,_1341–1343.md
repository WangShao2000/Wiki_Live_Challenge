# Breton Civil War, 1341–1343

The **Breton Civil War** of 1341–1343 represents the opening phase of the **War of the Breton Succession** (1341–1365), a conflict fought for control of the Duchy of Brittany following the death of Duke John III. The dispute arose between the House of Montfort, led by John of Montfort and his wife Joanna of Flanders, and the House of Blois, led by Charles of Blois and his wife Joan of Penthièvre [1][2]. While ostensibly a dynastic struggle between rival claimants to the ducal coronet, the war rapidly evolved into a proxy theater of the wider Hundred Years' War [2]. King Philip VI of France supported the claim of his nephew Charles of Blois, while King Edward III of England provided military backing to the Montfort faction to further his own claims against the French throne [1][3].

This initial period of the conflict is characterized by the rapid seizure of Breton strongholds by John of Montfort in 1341, his subsequent capture by French forces, and the continuation of the war by his wife, Joanna of Flanders [1][4]. The years 1342 and 1343 saw direct military intervention by English forces, including the first major land battle of the Hundred Years' War at Morlaix and a complex series of sieges at Vannes [5][6]. The phase concluded with the Truce of Malestroit in January 1343, which established a temporary cessation of hostilities and placed the city of Vannes under papal custody [7].

## Background and Succession Dispute

The conflict was precipitated by the death of Duke John III of Brittany on April 30, 1341 [3][8]. The Duke died without direct heirs, leaving a contentious succession crisis involving two primary claimants with distinct legal arguments regarding their right to the duchy.

### The Claimants
The two rival factions were defined by their relationship to the late Duke and the previous Duke, Arthur II:
*   **The House of Blois:** The claim of Joan of Penthièvre was based on her status as the daughter of Guy of Penthièvre, the younger full brother of the late Duke John III. Her husband, Charles of Blois, was a nephew of King Philip VI of France [2][8]. Their faction argued that under Breton customary law, a daughter could represent her deceased father in the line of succession [9].
*   **The House of Montfort:** John of Montfort was the half-brother of the late Duke John III, born of Duke Arthur II's second marriage to Yolande of Dreux [9][10]. Montfort's legal argument relied on the principle of male primogeniture and proximity of blood, asserting that a brother (even a half-brother) had a superior claim to a niece [11].

### International Involvement
The internal dispute was immediately internationalized due to the existing tensions of the Hundred Years' War, which had begun in 1337 [7]. King Philip VI of France naturally supported his nephew, Charles of Blois. Conversely, John of Montfort sought support from Edward III of England. Montfort offered homage to Edward III for Brittany, effectively recognizing the English king's claim to the French throne in exchange for military assistance [3][9]. This alliance transformed the Breton peninsula into a strategic front where English forces could open a new theater of operations against France [12].

## The Campaign of 1341

### Montfort's Seizure of Power
Immediately following the death of John III, John of Montfort acted to secure his position before the French king could intervene. In May 1341, he seized the ducal capital of Nantes and was proclaimed Duke by an assembly of citizens and minor nobles [9][11]. He subsequently moved to Limoges, where he secured the ducal treasury, providing the financial resources necessary to raise an army [2][11]. throughout the summer of 1341, Montfort conducted a rapid campaign, taking control of key fortified towns including Rennes, Vannes, Hennebont, and Brest [2][13].

### The Judgment of Conflans
In September 1341, King Philip VI summoned the peers of France to Conflans to adjudicate the succession dispute. The court ruled in favor of Charles of Blois, accepting the argument for succession through the female line and rejecting Montfort's claim [9][11]. Following this judgment, Philip VI confiscated Montfort's French lands and authorized military action to install Charles of Blois as Duke [11].

### French Counter-Offensive and Montfort's Capture
In October 1341, a French army led by Charles of Blois and John, Duke of Normandy (the future King John II), advanced into Brittany. The campaign began with the Siege of Champtoceaux, a strategic fortress guarding the Loire valley [3][14]. John of Montfort attempted to relieve the siege but was defeated in the Battle of Champtoceaux (also known as the Battle of l'Humeau) and forced to retreat to Nantes [3][8].

By November 1341, French forces had laid siege to Nantes. The city's defenders, demoralized by the execution of Montfortist prisoners within sight of the walls and the catapulting of severed heads into the city, forced John of Montfort to surrender [15][16]. He was taken prisoner and confined in the Louvre in Paris [9][11]. With the leader of the Montfort faction imprisoned, the French crown believed the rebellion had been suppressed [3].

## The War of the Two Joans (1342)

Following the capture of John of Montfort, leadership of his faction passed to his wife, Joanna of Flanders. This phase of the conflict is frequently referred to as the "War of the Two Joans" due to the prominent roles played by Joanna of Flanders and her rival, Joan of Penthièvre [2][17].

### Siege of Hennebont
Joanna of Flanders established her base of operations at the fortified port of Hennebont. In May 1342, Charles of Blois laid siege to the town [18]. Joanna actively participated in the defense, reportedly wearing armor and rallying the inhabitants [19][20]. During the siege, she led a sortie of 300 men to burn the French camp while the besiegers were distracted, an action that earned her the nickname "Jeanne la Flamme" (Fiery Joanna) [17][19][21].

Despite her efforts, the garrison was reduced to starvation. The Bishop of Léon attempted to negotiate a surrender, but the timely arrival of English reinforcements under Sir Walter Mauny in June 1342 prevented the town's capitulation [18][19][22]. The arrival of English troops forced Charles of Blois to lift the siege and withdraw [22][23].

### English Military Intervention
The relief of Hennebont marked the beginning of substantial English military involvement. In August 1342, a larger English force commanded by William de Bohun, Earl of Northampton, landed at Brest, which was also under siege by Charles of Blois [24][25]. The arrival of Northampton's army compelled the French forces to abandon the siege of Brest [5][18].

### Battle of Morlaix
On September 30, 1342, the English and Montfortist forces clashed with the Franco-Breton army of Charles of Blois at the Battle of Morlaix [5]. The English army, besieging the town of Morlaix, was attacked by a relief force led by Blois. The Earl of Northampton deployed his troops in a defensive position protected by concealed pits and ditches [26][27].

The battle is historically significant as the first major land engagement of the Hundred Years' War and an early example of the tactical combination of dismounted men-at-arms and longbowmen that would later prove decisive at Crécy [5][27]. The French cavalry suffered heavy losses due to the defensive pits and archery fire. Although the English were eventually forced to retreat into a nearby wood to avoid being overwhelmed by superior numbers, the battle is generally considered a tactical success for the Anglo-Breton force, as they inflicted disproportionate casualties on the French [5][24][26].

### Sieges of Vannes
In late 1342, the conflict focused on the city of Vannes, which changed hands multiple times in a series of sieges.
1.  **First Siege:** The city was initially held by Montfortists but was taken by Charles of Blois earlier in the war [6].
2.  **Second Siege:** In October 1342, Robert III of Artois, a French exile serving Edward III, landed with a force and captured Vannes for the Montfortists [6][28].
3.  **Third Siege:** A French army under Olivier IV de Clisson retook the city in November 1342. During this engagement, Robert of Artois was mortally wounded; he was transported to London for treatment but died shortly thereafter [6][28][29].
4.  **Fourth Siege:** King Edward III arrived in Brittany in person in late 1342 to lead a siege to retake Vannes [6][30].

## The Truce of Malestroit (1343)

By January 1343, the military situation had reached a stalemate. Edward III's army was besieging Vannes but faced supply shortages and the approach of a large French army commanded by the Duke of Normandy [7][30]. Neither side wished to risk a decisive battle under winter conditions.

### Terms of the Truce
On January 19, 1343, the Truce of Malestroit was signed in the Chapel of the Madeleine in Malestroit [7][31]. The agreement was mediated by two papal legates sent by Pope Clement VI [7][31]. The terms included:
*   A cessation of hostilities between England and France for a period of three years [31].
*   The city of Vannes was placed into the temporary custody of the Papacy [7][30].
*   John of Montfort was to be released from imprisonment in Paris [7][30].
*   Both monarchs agreed to send delegations to Avignon to negotiate a permanent peace treaty [7].

### Aftermath of the Truce
The Truce of Malestroit marked the end of the first intense phase of the civil war, but it failed to secure a lasting peace. King Philip VI did not immediately release John of Montfort, and the peace conference in Avignon collapsed without a treaty [1][7]. Hostilities resumed before the truce's official expiration, with John of Montfort escaping or being released later in 1343/1345, only to die in 1345 [3][9]. The city of Vannes, nominally under papal control, was retaken by English forces in September 1343 after local citizens expelled the papal garrison [1][6]. The War of the Breton Succession continued until the Battle of Auray in 1364 and the subsequent Treaty of Guérande in 1365 [2].
