# Sima de los Huesos hominins

The **Sima de los Huesos hominins** are a population of Middle Pleistocene hominins discovered at the Sima de los Huesos ("Pit of Bones") site within the Atapuerca Mountains of northern Spain. Dated to approximately 430,000 years ago, the assemblage represents the largest single collection of ancient human fossils recovered to date, comprising more than 7,000 bone fragments from at least 28 individuals [1][2][3].

Originally classified as *Homo heidelbergensis*, the taxonomic designation of these hominins has been the subject of significant scientific debate. Morphological analyses of the skulls and teeth indicate a mosaic of traits, possessing Neanderthal-derived features in the face and dentition while retaining more archaic characteristics in the braincase [2][4]. In 2016, the sequencing of nuclear DNA from the remains identified them as early Neanderthals or members of the Neanderthal lineage, diverging from the Denisovans earlier than previously thought [5][6].

The site is notable for the exceptional preservation of the fossils, which has allowed for detailed studies of paleopathology, growth rates, and stature [1][7]. Prominent individual specimens include "Miguelón" (Cranium 5), a complete skull showing signs of severe infection, and "Elvis" (Pelvis 1), the most complete pelvis in the fossil record [8][9]. The discovery of a single red quartzite handaxe, nicknamed "Excalibur," alongside the bodies has led some researchers to propose that the site represents the earliest known evidence of ritual burial practice, although this hypothesis remains a subject of discussion [1][10].

## Site and discovery

### Location and geology
The Sima de los Huesos is a small chamber located at the bottom of a 13-meter (43 ft) vertical shaft within the Cueva Mayor-Cueva del Silo cave system in the Sierra de Atapuerca, Burgos, Spain [1][10]. The chamber is situated approximately 30 meters (98 ft) below the surface and 500 meters (1,600 ft) from the nearest current entrance [2]. The hominin fossils are found within a lithostratigraphic layer known as Unit 6 (LU-6), embedded in clay-rich mud breccias [11][12].

### History of excavation
The first human fossil at the site, a mandible, was discovered in 1976 by Trinidad Torres, a doctoral student who was initially searching for bear fossils [2][13]. Following this discovery, systematic excavations began in 1984 [14]. The research has been conducted by a team of paleontologists, led initially by Emiliano Aguirre and later by Juan Luis Arsuaga, Eudald Carbonell, and José María Bermúdez de Castro [1][2].

Since 1984, the site has been excavated continuously during field seasons. By 2016, the team had recovered nearly 7,000 human skeletal fragments, representing all anatomical regions of the skeleton [15][16]. The excavation is challenging due to the narrowness of the shaft and the difficulty of accessing the chamber, requiring researchers to scramble through narrow corridors and rappel down the chimney [1][17].

### Dating
Determining the precise age of the Sima de los Huesos deposits has been complex. Early estimates varied significantly, but recent applications of radiometric dating techniques have refined the chronology. Uranium-series dating of speleothems (cave formations) formed on the fossils and the surrounding mud suggests the hominin-bearing layer was deposited approximately 430,000 years ago [2][11][18]. This date places the population in the Middle Pleistocene, specifically the Chibanian stage [13].

## Morphology and taxonomy

### Anatomical features
The Sima de los Huesos population displays a high degree of sexual dimorphism and robusticity. The individuals were generally large-bodied, with wide chests and hips. Estimates based on the pelvic and limb bones suggest an average stature of around 170 cm (5 ft 7 in) and a weight of approximately 90 kg (200 lb) for males [2][7].

The crania exhibit a "mosaic" pattern of evolution. The faces and teeth possess derived traits associated with Neanderthals, such as a projected midface, distinct brow ridges, and specific dental cusp patterns [2][19]. However, the braincases retain archaic features more similar to *Homo erectus* or *Homo heidelbergensis*, lacking the "bomb-like" rounded shape typical of classic Late Pleistocene Neanderthals [2]. The average cranial capacity is approximately 1,232 cc, which is lower than that of both classic Neanderthals and modern humans [2][20].

### Taxonomic classification
The classification of the Sima hominins has shifted over time as new evidence has emerged.
*   **Homo heidelbergensis:** For many years, the fossils were classified as *Homo heidelbergensis*, a species considered by many anthropologists to be the common ancestor of Neanderthals and modern humans [17][21].
*   **Early Neanderthals:** The presence of distinct Neanderthal traits in the facial skeleton and dentition led researchers to suggest the "accretion model" of Neanderthal evolution, where distinctive features evolved piecemeal rather than all at once [4][19]. Following genomic analysis in 2016, the Sima hominins are now widely considered to be early Neanderthals or a population closely related to the direct ancestors of Neanderthals [5][15]. Consequently, some researchers argue that the *Homo heidelbergensis* classification should be removed from the Sima population entirely [22].

## Genetics

The Sima de los Huesos fossils have yielded the oldest human DNA ever sequenced, providing critical insights into the divergence of archaic human lineages [20].

### Mitochondrial DNA
In 2013, researchers successfully sequenced the mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) from a femur (Femur XIII). Unexpectedly, the analysis revealed that the Sima hominin's mtDNA was more closely related to that of Denisovans—a sister group to Neanderthals found in Siberia—than to classic Neanderthals [23][24]. This finding was puzzling because the Sima hominins morphologically resemble Neanderthals, not Denisovans (to the extent that Denisovan morphology is known) [25][26].

### Nuclear DNA
In 2016, a team from the Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology sequenced nuclear DNA from two specimens. Unlike mtDNA, which is inherited only from the mother, nuclear DNA provides a more complete picture of ancestry. The nuclear DNA results confirmed that the Sima hominins were indeed members of the Neanderthal lineage, more closely related to Neanderthals than to Denisovans [6][15][27].

This discrepancy between the mitochondrial and nuclear DNA suggests a complex evolutionary history. One hypothesis is that the Sima hominins carried an early lineage of mtDNA common to the ancestors of both Neanderthals and Denisovans, which was later replaced in Late Pleistocene Neanderthals by a different mtDNA lineage, possibly through gene flow from an African population [5][6]. The nuclear DNA results also pushed back the estimated time of divergence between Neanderthals and Denisovans to before 430,000 years ago [1][15].

## Notable specimens

The site has produced several exceptionally complete specimens that have been given popular nicknames.

### Cranium 5 ("Miguelón")
Discovered in 1992, Cranium 5, nicknamed "Miguelón" after the cyclist Miguel Induráin, is one of the most complete skulls in the human fossil record [8][28]. The individual is estimated to have been over 30 years old at the time of death. The skull shows evidence of a severe infection in the left maxilla caused by a broken tooth, which led to sepsis and likely contributed to the individual's death [28][29]. The fact that the bone shows signs of healing suggests the individual survived for some time after the injury, implying they may have received care or food from other group members [8].

### Pelvis 1 ("Elvis")
Recovered in 1994, Pelvis 1 is the most complete hominin pelvis known from the fossil record and was nicknamed "Elvis" in reference to Elvis Presley [2][7]. It belonged to a male individual aged over 45 years. The pelvis is very broad and robust, indicating a stocky build. Pathological analysis revealed that the individual suffered from spondylolisthesis (displacement of a vertebra) and Baastrup's disease, which would have caused severe back pain and limited mobility [7][13]. The survival of this individual to an advanced age despite these debilitating conditions is cited as further evidence of social care within the group [7][30].

### Cranium 4 ("Agamemnon")
Nicknamed "Agamemnon," this skull was found with bony growths in the ear canals known as exostoses, or "surfer's ear." Initial studies suggested the individual might have been deaf, but later analyses concluded that hearing would have been functional [31].

### Cranium 17 (Murder hypothesis)
Cranium 17 represents a young adult individual. Forensic analysis of the skull revealed two penetrating fractures on the frontal bone, above the left eye. The shape and trajectory of the fractures suggest they were caused by two separate impacts from the same object. Researchers concluded that these injuries were likely the result of lethal interpersonal violence, making this one of the earliest known cases of murder in the human fossil record [16][32].

## Behavior and culture

### Accumulation of remains
The mechanism by which 28 individuals ended up at the bottom of a deep shaft is a subject of intense debate. Several hypotheses have been proposed:
*   **Carnivore accumulation:** That bears or other carnivores dragged the bodies into the cave. However, the scarcity of carnivore tooth marks on the hominin bones compared to the bear bones suggests this was not the primary cause [12][33].
*   **Natural trap:** That the individuals accidentally fell into the shaft. While this explains the presence of the bears, the high number of humans (28) makes a mass accidental fall statistically unlikely [2][32].
*   **Intentional disposal:** The excavation team argues that the bodies were intentionally thrown into the pit by other hominins. This is supported by the demographic profile (mostly adolescents and young adults) and the lack of other animal remains typical of a natural trap (besides the bears) [2][11][20].

### "Excalibur" and funerary practices
In 1998, a single stone tool was discovered among the human remains: a handaxe made of red quartzite, a material not found commonly in the immediate vicinity [1][10]. Nicknamed "Excalibur," this Acheulean tool shows no signs of wear, suggesting it was not used for processing food or hides. The excavators and various experts propose that the handaxe was a grave good or ritual offering thrown into the pit alongside the dead [1][10]. If accurate, this would represent the earliest evidence of symbolic funerary behavior in humans, dating back 430,000 years [17][18].

### Language
Studies of the auditory capacities of the Sima hominins, based on the reconstruction of the ear bones (such as in Cranium 4), suggest their hearing was tuned to the frequencies of human speech, similar to modern humans and distinct from chimpanzees. This has been interpreted as evidence that they possessed a vocal communication system or language [3][31].

## Paleoecology

In addition to human remains, the Sima de los Huesos contains a massive accumulation of carnivore fossils. The most abundant species is *Ursus deningeri*, an extinct ancestor of the cave bear (*Ursus spelaeus*). More than 200 individual bears have been identified in the pit [34]. It is believed that these bears fell into the shaft while seeking hibernation spots or were trapped in the cave system [2][34]. Other carnivores found in smaller numbers include lions, wolves, foxes, and mustelids [10].
