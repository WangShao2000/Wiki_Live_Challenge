# National Geographic Society Headquarters

The **National Geographic Society Headquarters** is a complex of buildings located in Washington, D.C., that serves as the administrative center and museum for the National Geographic Society (NGS). Located at 1145 17th Street NW and extending to 1600 M Street NW, the campus occupies a significant portion of a city block in downtown Washington [1], [2]. The site has been the home of the Society since the early 20th century and features a collection of structures representing distinct architectural styles, including Italian Renaissance, Neoclassical, and New Formalism [2].

The complex includes the original Hubbard Memorial Hall (1904), the Administration Building (1913, expanded 1932), and the 17th Street Building (1964) designed by Edward Durell Stone [3], [2]. In 2022, the Society began a major renovation and expansion project known as "Base Camp," which includes the construction of a new entrance pavilion and the **Museum of Exploration**, scheduled to open in 2026 [4], [5]. The campus was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 2023 [2].

## History

### Early years and Hubbard Hall
Founded in 1888, the National Geographic Society originally met at the Cosmos Club in Washington, D.C., and did not possess a dedicated headquarters building [6]. As membership grew, the Society commissioned its first permanent building, **Hubbard Memorial Hall**, which was completed in 1904 [3]. Named for the Society's first president, Gardiner Greene Hubbard, the building was designed by the architectural firm Hornblower & Marshall [6], [7].

### Expansion (1913–1949)
Following a rapid increase in membership—from 2,700 to 150,000 within eight years—the Society required additional space [6]. In 1912, construction began on the Administration Building adjacent to Hubbard Hall along 16th Street. Designed by architect Arthur B. Heaton, the wing was completed in 1913 [2], [7]. Heaton also designed subsequent additions to this structure, including a south wing and a central pavilion which were completed in 1932 [2]. A further expansion, known as the editors' wing, was added in 1948 [3].

### 17th Street Building (1964)
In the early 1960s, the Society expanded its footprint to 17th Street. The NGS hired noted modernist architect Edward Durell Stone to design a new ten-story structure [2]. Stone, who later designed the John F. Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts, utilized the New Formalism style for the building [6], [8].

Construction began in 1961, and the building was dedicated in January 1964 [2]. President Lyndon B. Johnson attended the dedication ceremony, stating that the building stood "as a testament of confidence in—and enthusiasm for—the future" [2]. The building featured a fallout shelter and a morgue, relics of the Cold War era during which it was constructed [6].

### M Street Building and Plaza (1984)
In 1981, the Society commissioned the firm Skidmore, Owings & Merrill (SOM) to design a new multifunctional building to replace the 1948 editors' wing [3]. Completed in 1984, this structure connected the 16th Street and 17th Street buildings and included a 400-seat theater and a terraced design [3], [9]. The project also introduced a landscaped plaza designed by James Urban, which featured the site-specific sculpture *Marabar* by Elyn Zimmerman [3].

## Architecture

The headquarters complex is noted for its assemblage of varying architectural styles that reflect the eras in which they were built.

*   **Hubbard Memorial Hall (1904):** Designed by Hornblower & Marshall, this two-story building features an Italian Renaissance Revival style [2]. It is constructed of buff-colored brick and is distinguished by large arched windows and a hipped roof with terracotta tiles [7].
*   **Administration Building (1913/1932):** Designed by Arthur B. Heaton, this structure employs a Classical Revival or Neoclassical style [7]. It is clad in limestone and features rusticated stonework and double-leaf brass doors [7].
*   **17th Street Building (1964):** Also known as the Stone Building, this ten-story structure is a prominent example of New Formalism [2]. The exterior is composed of white marble and glass, featuring a slender colonnade and a wide overhanging roof cornice [8], [7].
*   **M Street Building (1984):** Designed by SOM, this building was characterized by a ziggurat form with stepped terraces [9], [10]. Unlike the earlier structures, this building is considered non-contributing to the site's historical designation [7].

## Base Camp Renovation (2022–Present)

In 2022, the National Geographic Society broke ground on a comprehensive renovation of the campus, referred to as "Base Camp" [11]. The $250 million project aims to unify the existing buildings, expand public access, and modernize the facilities [12].

### Design and scope
The renovation is designed by the architecture firm Hickok Cole, with landscape architecture by OLIN [13]. A primary component of the project is the construction of a new entry pavilion and the **Museum of Exploration**, which will occupy approximately 100,000 square feet [5]. The design includes a glass façade, a rooftop terrace, and a new public plaza [14]. The project involves the renovation of the four existing buildings to improve connectivity and sustainability [4].

### Sustainability
The renovated campus is designed to meet high sustainability standards. Features include solar panels expected to generate 25% of the campus's power, a gray water HVAC system, and the use of recycled materials from the existing structures [12], [11]. The project targets LEED Platinum certification [14].

### Museum of Exploration
Scheduled to open in mid-2026, the Museum of Exploration will feature immersive exhibitions, a state-of-the-art theater, and an education center [5], [15]. Key attractions include the "Rolex Explorers Landing," which will display artifacts from historic expeditions [16].

## *Marabar* Sculpture Controversy

For nearly four decades, the central plaza of the headquarters featured *Marabar* (1984), a site-specific installation by artist Elyn Zimmerman [3]. The work consisted of a rectangular reflecting pool set among large, polished granite boulders [17].

### Preservation efforts
In 2017, the National Geographic Society announced plans to redesign the plaza as part of the Base Camp renovation, which required the removal of *Marabar* [3]. The Cultural Landscape Foundation (TCLF) designated the site as "threatened" and advocated for its preservation, arguing it was a masterwork of late 20th-century landscape art [18], [17]. The District of Columbia's Historic Preservation Review Board initially approved the renovation plans but later reconsidered following public outcry and letters of protest from the art community [18].

### Relocation
In 2021, an agreement was reached to relocate the sculpture rather than demolish it [19]. The National Geographic Society funded the removal and reinstallation of the work [20]. In 2023, the sculpture was reinstalled at American University in Washington, D.C. [21]. Due to the change in configuration required by the new location, the artist renamed the work *Sudama* [20].
