# 2020 Jonesboro tornado

The **2020 Jonesboro tornado** was a large and destructive tornado that struck the city of Jonesboro in Craighead County, Arkansas, on the afternoon of March 28, 2020. Rated **EF3** on the Enhanced Fujita scale, the tornado carved a path of destruction approximately 12.55 miles (20.2 km) long through the city's commercial and residential areas. The event resulted in 22 injuries but caused no fatalities, a statistic widely attributed by local officials and media to the reduced public activity resulting from the COVID-19 pandemic [1][2][3].

The tornado caused significant structural damage, particularly to the Mall at Turtle Creek, the Jonesboro Municipal Airport, and numerous residential subdivisions. It also derailed a freight train and destroyed industrial facilities. Economic losses were estimated between $300 million and $500 million [2][4]. The tornado was part of a larger severe weather outbreak that affected the Midwest and Southern United States on March 28–29, 2020 [5][6].

## Meteorological synopsis

### Environmental setup
On March 28, 2020, a potent storm system moved across the Central United States, creating conditions favorable for severe weather. A surface low-pressure system was centered over the Central Plains, with a warm front lifting northward into the Midwest and a cold front extending southward through Missouri and Arkansas [7].

By the morning of March 28, a warm and humid airmass had established itself over the Mid-South region. Although cloud cover initially limited surface heating, strong southerly flow transported moisture into the area, with dew points reaching the mid-to-upper 60s°F. As the day progressed, the "cap"—a layer of warm air aloft that inhibits storm development—began to erode across western and eastern Arkansas [7].

The atmospheric environment became increasingly unstable by the afternoon. Mixed-Layer Convective Available Potential Energy (MLCAPE) values ranged from 1,500 to over 2,000 J/kg, indicating significant instability. This was coupled with strong deep-layer wind shear (0–6 km shear of 75–80 knots) and low-level storm-relative helicity (100–150 m²/s²), creating a kinematic environment supportive of supercell thunderstorms capable of producing strong tornadoes [7][8].

### Forecasting and warnings
The Storm Prediction Center (SPC) had issued a "Moderate Risk" (level 4 of 5) for severe weather for parts of Illinois and Iowa, while northeast Arkansas was placed under an "Enhanced Risk" (level 3 of 5) later in the afternoon [4][7]. A Tornado Watch (number 68) was issued for the region earlier in the day, alerting residents to the potential for severe storms [9][10].

At 12:15 p.m. CDT, the SPC issued a mesoscale discussion noting the evolving unstable environment in Arkansas [7]. As the supercell thunderstorm responsible for the Jonesboro tornado developed and showed signs of rotation, the National Weather Service (NWS) office in Memphis issued a Tornado Warning for Craighead County at 4:47 p.m. CDT, approximately ten to eleven minutes before the tornado touched down [2][9].

### Storm evolution
The supercell thunderstorm that produced the Jonesboro tornado originated from a cluster of storms that intensified as they moved into the favorable environment over northeast Arkansas. Prior to striking Jonesboro, this same supercell produced an EF1 tornado that tracked for approximately five miles between the towns of Algoa and Amagon in Jackson County, Arkansas [3][10]. The storm then cycled as it approached Craighead County, reorganizing before producing the Jonesboro tornado [10].

## Tornado event

The tornado touched down at approximately 4:58 p.m. CDT in south-central Jonesboro, just north of Interstate 555 [4][9][10]. It initially caused damage consistent with an EF2 rating as it impacted commercial areas near Red Wolf Boulevard [2][10]. The tornado was captured on video by Arkansas Department of Transportation traffic cameras and local media tower cameras, showing a large, wedge-shaped funnel moving through the city [4][10].

Rapidly intensifying, the tornado moved northeast toward the Mall at Turtle Creek. At this location, the tornado inflicted severe structural damage, destroying major sections of the mall and surrounding retail establishments [2][9]. The path width expanded to a maximum of 600 yards (approximately 0.34 miles) [2][7].

The tornado reached its peak intensity as it crossed the Jonesboro Municipal Airport. Damage surveys conducted by the National Weather Service and local engineers determined that the damage in this area was consistent with an **EF3** rating, with estimated peak wind speeds of 140 mph (225 km/h) [1][2][4]. The tornado destroyed hangars and tossed aircraft before crossing a nearby railway line, where it derailed a Union Pacific freight train [2][11].

Continuing northeast, the tornado tracked through residential subdivisions and industrial areas, causing a mix of EF2 and EF3 damage to homes and businesses. It crossed into the Brookland area before lifting in northeast Brookland at approximately 5:14 p.m. CDT [2][4][7]. The total path length was determined to be 12.55 miles (20.2 km), with a duration of approximately 16 minutes [2][4].

## Impact

The tornado caused widespread destruction across Jonesboro, affecting commercial, industrial, and residential sectors. The Jonesboro Police Department reported that the storm damaged or destroyed nearly 200 homes and dozens of businesses [2][9].

### Commercial and industrial
The **Mall at Turtle Creek**, a major regional shopping center located on Highland Drive, sustained catastrophic damage. The tornado tore through the facility, collapsing roofs and walls, and destroying the interiors of numerous stores. Anchor stores such as Dillard's, JCPenney, and Target were heavily damaged, while the primary enclosed concourse was rendered structurally unsound [2][12][13]. The mall was closed at the time due to the tornado warning and reduced operating hours related to the COVID-19 pandemic, which officials credited with preventing mass casualties at the site [9][14].

At the **Jonesboro Municipal Airport**, the damage was extensive. Several large hangars were flattened or shredded by the high winds. The Aircraft Owners and Pilots Association reported that 50 aircraft were damaged, including a Beechcraft King Air 200 that was blown down the runway [4]. A maintenance hangar owned by Fly Jonesboro was destroyed [4].

Just north of the airport, the tornado struck a moving freight train. The wind force derailed 112 railcars, blowing them off the tracks. The derailment involved hazardous materials, prompting a response from hazardous materials (HAZMAT) teams, though leaks were quickly contained [2][4][13]. Additionally, a large fire broke out at an industrial business in the area following the storm [2].

Other businesses, including a Best Buy, a Cheddar's Scratch Kitchen, and a Gateway Tire and Service Center, were severely damaged or destroyed [14][15][16].

### Residential
Residential areas in the path of the tornado suffered significant losses. The Jonesboro Police Department's final tally indicated that 149 homes were destroyed. An additional 114 homes and 8 apartments were deemed unoccupiable due to major damage, while 446 homes sustained minor damage but remained occupiable [2]. Subdivisions such as Wildwood Point saw homes with roofs torn off and exterior walls collapsed [4].

### Casualties
Despite the severity of the damage and the tornado's path through the city center, there were **zero fatalities**. A total of 22 injuries were reported, with two individuals requiring hospitalization for non-life-threatening injuries [1][2][4].

Local officials, including Jonesboro Mayor Harold Perrin and Police Chief Rick Elliott, stated that the low casualty count was likely due to the COVID-19 pandemic. At the time of the tornado, schools were closed, and many non-essential businesses were shuttered or operating with reduced capacity to encourage social distancing. The Mall at Turtle Creek, which would typically be crowded on a Saturday afternoon, was largely empty [2][9][14][17].

## Aftermath and recovery

### Immediate response
Following the tornado, the Jonesboro Police Department and other emergency agencies established a command post at the damaged Mall at Turtle Creek. Search and rescue operations began immediately, with first responders sifting through debris to locate survivors [2][15]. A citywide curfew was imposed from 7:00 p.m. to 6:00 a.m. to prevent looting and ensure public safety amidst the debris and downed power lines [13][15][17].

The response was complicated by the ongoing COVID-19 pandemic. Emergency shelters had to implement social distancing protocols, and the clean-up effort was hindered by restrictions on large gatherings, which limited the ability of volunteers to congregate as they typically would following a disaster [3][18].

### Damage estimates
Preliminary estimates placed the cost of the damage at approximately $300 million [4]. Later reports and police department summaries suggested the cleanup and recovery costs could exceed $500 million [2]. The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) National Centers for Environmental Information (NCEI) included the event in its database of billion-dollar weather disasters as part of the broader March 2020 severe weather outbreak [5][19].

### Long-term redevelopment
The recovery of the **Mall at Turtle Creek** became a protracted process. While anchor stores like Dillard's, JCPenney, and Target eventually repaired their buildings and reopened, the main enclosed portion of the mall remained shuttered and in a state of disrepair for several years [12][20].

In 2023, Spinoso Real Estate Group, which had taken over management of the property, announced plans to demolish the damaged enclosed section of the mall, citing safety concerns and the need to clear the site for future development [21][22]. Demolition was completed by early 2024 [12].

In September 2025, the 38.89-acre mall site was sold at auction for approximately $4.875 million to a local investor group. Redevelopment plans announced shortly after the sale proposed the construction of a new 96,000-square-foot open-air retail center, marking a shift away from the traditional enclosed mall format [20][23].
