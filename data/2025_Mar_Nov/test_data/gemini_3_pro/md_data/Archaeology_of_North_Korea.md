# Archaeology of North Korea

**Archaeology of North Korea** refers to the archaeological research, excavation, and interpretation of material remains within the Democratic People's Republic of Korea (DPRK). The discipline is characterized by a distinct integration of nationalistic historiography and the state ideology of *Juche* (self-reliance). Since the division of the Korean peninsula, archaeological practice in the North has diverged significantly from that in South Korea and the wider international community, particularly regarding chronology, human origins, and the influence of external cultures [1][2].

Key areas of research include the Paleolithic origins of humans on the peninsula, the Neolithic and Bronze Age cultures associated with the legendary state of Gojoseon, and the tombs and fortresses of the Goguryeo (Koguryo) kingdom [2][3]. North Korea hosts several sites of international significance, including the Complex of Koguryo Tombs, which was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2004 [4][5].

The interpretation of archaeological data in North Korea is often utilized to support state narratives regarding the antiquity, homogeneity, and independence of the Korean people [1][6]. Prominent controversies include the dating of the Tomb of Dangun, the denial of the Han dynasty's Lelang Commandery's location within the peninsula, and the theory of "Taedonggang Culture," which posits the Taedong River basin as one of the world's five independent cradles of civilization [3][7][8]. Despite political isolation, there have been instances of inter-Korean archaeological cooperation, most notably the joint excavations at the Manwoldae palace site in Kaesong [9][10].

## History of Archaeological Research

### Colonial Period and Early Development
Modern archaeology in Korea began during the late 19th and early 20th centuries, largely under the influence of Japanese scholars during the period of Japanese occupation (1910–1945). Japanese archaeologists, such as Sekino Tadashi, conducted surveys to locate ancient sites, often focusing on the Lelang Commandery near Pyongyang to emphasize Chinese influence on early Korean development, or seeking links to the ancestral Yamato people [2][7]. Korean scholars view this period's scholarship as imperialist and intended to justify colonial rule by portraying Korean history as stagnant or dependent on foreign powers [1][2].

### Post-War and the Rise of Juche Archaeology
Following the division of Korea in 1945, the North established its own academic institutions. Early North Korean archaeology was led by scholars such as To Yu-ho and Han Hŭng-su, who had studied in Europe. Initially, the field adopted a framework combining historical materialism with culture-historical approaches [2][11].

By the 1970s, the discipline underwent a major shift to align with *Juche* ideology. This transition moved away from traditional Marxist-Leninist internationalism toward a leader-centered, nationalistic historiography [1][12]. Instructions from leaders Kim Il Sung and Kim Jong Il emphasized the need to demonstrate the independent development of the Korean nation and to reject "flunkeyism" (reliance on great powers) [1][13]. Consequently, archaeological interpretations began to focus on proving that Korean civilization developed indigenously, free from significant Chinese or other external influences [1][6].

## Paleolithic Era and Human Origins

North Korean archaeology places significant emphasis on the Paleolithic period to establish the deep antiquity of human presence on the peninsula.

### Komun Moru
One of the most significant and controversial sites is Komun Moru (Black Anvil) in Sangwon County, near Pyongyang. Excavated in 1966, the site yielded stone tools and fossilized animal bones [14][15]. North Korean archaeologists date the site to the Lower Paleolithic, approximately 1 million years ago (sometimes cited as 600,000 to 400,000 years ago), and argue it represents evidence of *Homo erectus* habitation [2][15][16].

International and South Korean scholars generally estimate the site to be younger, dating it to the Middle Pleistocene (approx. 400,000 years ago) or later, and some have questioned whether the stone objects are definitively human-made tools or geofacts [2].

### Human Evolution Theories
Based on fossils such as "Ryokpho Man," "Tokchon Man," and "Mandal Man," North Korean researchers argue for a continuous evolutionary lineage within the peninsula [17]. This supports the state-endorsed theory that the Taedong River basin was a cradle of humankind where ancient humans evolved into modern Koreans without migration from other regions [3][17]. This view contrasts with the mainstream scientific consensus of human migration and dispersal [2].

## Gojoseon and the Tomb of Dangun

Gojoseon is recorded in Korean tradition as the first state on the peninsula, founded by the legendary figure Dangun Wanggeom. The archaeological investigation of Gojoseon is central to North Korean historiography.

### The 1993 Excavation
In 1993, North Korean archaeologists excavated a tomb at the foot of Mount Taebak in Kangdong County. They announced the discovery of the remains of Dangun and his wife. The Academy of Social Sciences reported that Electron Spin Resonance (ESR) dating of the bones indicated an age of 5011 years (as of 1993), placing the date of the skeleton at approximately 3000 BCE [13][18][19].

### Controversy and Reconstruction
Following the discovery, the site was reconstructed into a monumental white stone pyramid, the Mausoleum of Dangun, completed in 1994 [18][19]. The discovery prompted a revision of North Korean history textbooks, pushing the founding of Gojoseon back from the traditional 2333 BCE to roughly 3000 BCE [20].

International and South Korean archaeologists have expressed skepticism regarding these findings. Critics point to the lack of independent verification, the unusual application of ESR dating on bone (which is often considered unreliable for this period compared to radiocarbon dating), and the political convenience of finding the "founder of the nation" near Pyongyang [8][18][20]. The tomb's structure was originally identified as a Goguryeo-era tomb, which North Korean scholars explain as a later restoration of the original site by the Goguryeo people [8][19].

## The Lelang (Nangnang) Controversy

A major point of divergence between North Korean archaeology and international scholarship concerns the Lelang Commandery.

### Mainstream View
Historical texts and archaeological evidence, including seals, lacquerware, and tombs found in the Pyongyang area, indicate that the Chinese Han dynasty established the Lelang Commandery in northwestern Korea in 108 BCE after defeating Wiman Joseon [7][21]. This commandery served as an administrative center and trade hub until the 4th century CE [21].

### North Korean Interpretation
North Korean scholars deny that the Han commandery was located in Pyongyang. They argue that the artifacts found in the region (such as the "Nangnang tombs") are the remains of an independent Korean state called the "Nangnang Kingdom" or are associated with Gojoseon and Goguryeo [21][22]. They contend that the Chinese Lelang Commandery was actually located to the northwest, on the Liaodong Peninsula in modern-day China [22].

Artifacts bearing Han Chinese characteristics are interpreted as trade goods, forgeries introduced by colonial Japanese researchers, or items produced by locals influenced by international contact, rather than evidence of Chinese political administration [21][22]. This revisionist geography supports the *Juche* narrative by removing a period of foreign colonial rule from the peninsula's history [7].

## Goguryeo (Koguryo) Period

The Goguryeo kingdom (37 BCE – 668 CE) is highly regarded in North Korea as a powerful, militaristic state that defended the peninsula against foreign invaders [1][4].

### Complex of Koguryo Tombs
The most prominent archaeological remains from this period are the elite burial mounds found in Pyongyang and Nampo. In 2004, the **Complex of Koguryo Tombs** became the first site in North Korea to be inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List [4][23]. The site comprises 63 individual tombs, many of which feature elaborate wall paintings [5][24].

*   **Murals:** The murals depict daily life, mythology, religious practices (Buddhism and Taoism), and the "Four Deities" (Dragon, Tiger, Bird, Tortoise/Snake) [5][25].
*   **Architecture:** The tombs demonstrate advanced engineering, with stone chambers and earthen mounds. Notable examples include the Tomb of King Tongmyong (reconstructed), the Anak Tomb No. 3, and the Kangso Three Tombs [4][23].

### Fortresses and Palaces
Excavations have also focused on Goguryeo's military and political infrastructure.
*   **Anhak Palace:** Located in Pyongyang, this site served as a royal residence after the capital was moved to the city in 427 CE. Excavations have revealed the layout of the palace and defensive walls [26][27].
*   **Mt. Taesong Fortress:** A mountain fortress defending the capital, containing gates and pavilions that have been restored [26][28].

## Goryeo (Koryo) Period and Manwoldae

The Goryeo dynasty (918–1392) had its capital in Kaesong, which is located in present-day North Korea.

### Manwoldae Excavations
Manwoldae was the main royal palace of Goryeo. It was destroyed by the Red Turban invasions in 1361 [29][30]. The site is part of the "Historic Monuments and Sites in Kaesong," inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2013 [31][32].

Manwoldae represents a rare example of sustained archaeological cooperation between North and South Korea. Between 2007 and 2015, and briefly in 2018, joint teams from the South's National Research Institute of Cultural Heritage and the North's Korean Central History Museum conducted excavations [9][27][33].

**Key Findings:**
*   **Metal Movable Type:** In 2015, a piece of metal movable type was discovered at the site. It is believed to predate Gutenberg's invention, highlighting Goryeo's technological advancements [29].
*   **Architecture:** The excavations revealed the complex arrangement of buildings, drainage systems, and the use of celadon roof tiles [27][30].

## Taedonggang Culture Theory

In 1998, North Korean academia formally proposed the concept of "Taedonggang Culture" (Taedong River Culture). This theory asserts that the Taedong River basin was one of the world's five great independent cradles of ancient civilization, alongside the Nile, Tigris-Euphrates, Indus, and Yellow Rivers [3][6].

Proponents cite the density of dolmens, the early dates attributed to the Tomb of Dangun, and Paleolithic finds like Komun Moru as evidence that Pyongyang was a center of human evolution and civilization building [3][17]. This theory serves to legitimize the political centrality of Pyongyang and reinforces the ideology of national distinctiveness [1][6].

## Institutions and Museums

Archaeological research in North Korea is state-controlled. The primary institutions include:
*   **Archaeological Institute of the Academy of Social Sciences:** The central body for research and excavation [1][2].
*   **Kim Il Sung University:** The Faculty of History conducts research and trains archaeologists [1][2].
*   **Korean Central History Museum:** Located in Pyongyang, it houses major artifacts including the Komun Moru finds and relics from Manwoldae [14][29].
*   **Koryo Museum:** Located in Kaesong, housing artifacts from the Goryeo dynasty [27][30].
