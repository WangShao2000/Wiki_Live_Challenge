# The Rector of Justin

**The Rector of Justin** is a 1964 novel of manners and psychological fiction by the American author Louis Auchincloss. The story chronicles the life and career of the Reverend Francis "Frank" Prescott, the charismatic and autocratic founder of Justin Martyr, a fictional, exclusive Episcopal boarding school for boys in Massachusetts. Utilizing a multi-narrator structure, the novel examines Prescott's eighty-year life through the journals, letters, and memoirs of his colleagues, friends, and family, framed by the perspective of a young English teacher, Brian Aspinwall.

Widely considered Auchincloss's magnum opus, the novel explores themes of tradition versus progress, the decline of the White Anglo-Saxon Protestant (WASP) establishment, and the complexities of educational leadership. Upon its publication, *The Rector of Justin* was a commercial and critical success. It was a finalist for both the Pulitzer Prize for Fiction and the National Book Award for Fiction in 1965. Literary critics have frequently cited it as one of the finest examples of American boarding school fiction, drawing favorable comparisons to the works of Edith Wharton and Henry James for its incisive portrayal of the American upper class.

## Plot Summary

The narrative is framed by the journals of **Brian Aspinwall**, a shy, devout young man who joins the faculty of Justin Martyr in 1939 [1]. Aspinwall, who is considering a vocation in the Episcopal clergy, becomes fascinated by the school's founder and headmaster (or "Rector"), the Reverend **Francis Prescott**. At the time of Aspinwall's arrival, Prescott is eighty years old but remains a formidable and dominating figure in the institution he established decades earlier [1][2].

Aspinwall resolves to write a biography of the Rector. To do so, he compiles various documents—memoirs, letters, and diaries—from individuals who have known Prescott throughout his life. These accounts, which form the chapters of the novel, present conflicting views of the man, revealing him alternately as a saintly educator and a manipulative tyrant [3][4].

### The Narrative Accounts

The novel covers a timeline roughly spanning from 1860 to 1947, with the primary action of the frame narrative occurring between 1939 and 1947 [1]. The story unfolds through several distinct perspectives:

*   **Horace Havistock:** Prescott's oldest friend and a wealthy bachelor. His memoir details their shared youth and education. He recounts their time at boarding school and Oxford, where Prescott first envisioned a school that would mold American boys into Christian gentlemen [3][5]. Havistock reveals that he persuaded Prescott's first fiancée, **Eliza Dean**, to break off their engagement, arguing that she was unsuited for the life of a headmaster's wife. This intervention allowed Prescott to marry **Harriet Winslow**, a woman more willing to submit to the demands of the school [1][3].
*   **David Griscam:** A wealthy lawyer, alumnus, and chairman of the Justin Martyr board of trustees. Griscam's account describes the early struggles of the school and his own complex relationship with Prescott. While he idolizes the Rector, he also harbors resentment, admitting that the school's culture of bullying and silence taught him to be "underhanded" [3]. Griscam plays a pivotal role in fundraising, often appealing to Prescott's ambition to secure donations from men the Rector personally dislikes [3].
*   **Cordelia Prescott Turnbull:** The Rector's rebellious daughter. Her narrative exposes her father's domestic tyranny and his prioritization of the school over his family. She details her tragic romance with **Charley Strong**, a "golden boy" alumnus and World War I veteran [6]. Cordelia resents her father's interference in her life and his dominating influence over Charley, whom Prescott attempts to bring back to the Christian faith before Charley's premature death [6].
*   **Jules Griscam:** The son of David Griscam. A cynical and rebellious student, Jules detests the Rector and the school's ethos. His account details his acts of defiance, culminating in his expulsion after he throws a rock through the chapel window [6]. Jules eventually dies in a car accident in Europe, an event that serves as a grim counterpoint to the school's ideals of molding character [6].

### Conclusion

As the novel progresses through the war years of the 1940s, the world outside Justin Martyr changes, challenging the insular values of the school. The narrative describes the impact of World War II on the faculty and student body [7]. Eventually, facing the inevitability of time and the shifting cultural landscape, Prescott retires. He dies in 1947, shortly after relinquishing control of the school [1][8].

In the end, Aspinwall is left with a complex, contradictory portrait of a man who was both a visionary and a "petrified old windbag" [3]. The novel concludes with Aspinwall resolving to write the biography, though the text implies the difficulty of capturing the full truth of such a multifaceted figure [3].

## Characters

### Francis Prescott
The titular character, Francis "Frank" Prescott, is the founder and headmaster of Justin Martyr. Born in 1860, he was orphaned young—his father died in the Civil War and his mother from disease—but was educated at prestigious schools due to family trusts [2][6]. Short in stature but immense in presence, Prescott is depicted as a "God-like" figure to his students, embodying a "muscular Christian Humanism" [9]. He is driven by a ferocious ambition to create an institution that fuses Christian morality with rigorous education. However, his character is deeply contradictory; he is a pious Episcopalian who sometimes doubts his faith, and a moralist who supports an unequal, elitist social system [7][10].

### Brian Aspinwall
The frame narrator, Aspinwall is a sensitive, timid English teacher who joins the Justin faculty in 1939 [1]. Rejected from military service due to a heart murmur, he struggles with feelings of inadequacy and indecision regarding his potential clerical vocation [1][6]. He serves as the lens through which the reader views Prescott's final years. While he idolizes the Rector, his compilation of other narratives inadvertently reveals Prescott's flaws [3].

### Horace Havistock
Prescott's lifelong friend and contemporary. A refined, aesthetic bachelor, Havistock represents the "mauve decade" and a lifestyle of leisure that contrasts with Prescott's "strenuous life" philosophy [8]. Although he supports Prescott, he is cynical about the school's environment, which is noted to be institutionally homophobic despite Havistock's own implied homosexuality [3].

### David Griscam
A successful Wall Street lawyer and the first alumnus to serve as a trustee [5]. Griscam is the architect of the school's financial endowment. His relationship with Prescott is defined by a mix of admiration and animosity; he credits the school with his success but resents the psychological toll it took on him [3].

### Cordelia Prescott Turnbull
Prescott's youngest daughter. She is characterized by her rebellion against her father's suffocating authority and the gender roles of her class [6][7]. Her bitterness stems largely from her father's intervention in her relationship with Charley Strong and his general neglect of his daughters in favor of his "boys" [7].

### Charley Strong
A star pupil of Justin Martyr (class of 1911), described as an "American Rupert Brooke" [8]. After being wounded in World War I, he becomes disillusioned and nihilistic. He has an affair with Cordelia in Paris but eventually falls back under Prescott's spiritual influence before dying, an act Cordelia views as a betrayal [5][6].

### Jules Griscam
David Griscam's son. He represents the failure of the Justin system. Unlike his father, who submitted to the school's code, Jules openly rebels against Prescott's "benevolent" tyranny. His expulsion and subsequent death highlight the limits of Prescott's pedagogical methods [6].

### Harriet Winslow Prescott
Prescott's wife. She is depicted as a dutiful, long-suffering spouse who struggles with her role as a headmaster's wife, often feeling overshadowed by the institution her husband built [4].

## Themes

### Tradition versus Progress
A central theme of the novel is the tension between established traditions and the forces of social change. Prescott embodies a specific 19th-century orthodox educational philosophy that becomes increasingly anachronistic as the 20th century progresses [11]. The novel tracks the erosion of the "White Anglo-Saxon Protestant" (WASP) hegemony, showing how the values Prescott championed—religious certainty, social hierarchy, and classical education—are challenged by the secularization and democratization of post-WWII America [3][12].

### The Nature of Education and Leadership
Auchincloss uses the school as a microcosm to explore the purpose of education. Prescott believes in the "transformational potential of education" to shape moral character [11]. However, the novel questions the validity of this "molding" process, suggesting it can be repressive and damaging. The narrative examines the "internal contradictions" of a school that claims to be democratic yet caters almost exclusively to the wealthy elite [3][7].

### Hypocrisy and Honesty
The novel scrutinizes the gap between Prescott's public persona and his private actions. While he preaches Christian virtue, he is shown to be capable of cruelty, manipulation, and prejudice [7]. The text highlights his "secular erosion of religious precepts," noting that he often supports changes that undermine the school's original religious mission to maintain its prestige [7].

### Class and Social Dynamics
*The Rector of Justin* provides a detailed study of the American upper class. It depicts the "dynamics of wealth, power, and exclusivity" within the boarding school system [11]. The characters' lives are heavily determined by their social standing, and the novel illustrates the difficulties faced by those who do not fit the mold, such as the working-class student Carter Goodhue or the aesthetic Brian Aspinwall [11].

## Background and Inspiration

### Real-Life Models
Louis Auchincloss, a graduate of the Groton School (class of 1935), drew heavily from his own experiences in writing the novel [3][13]. The character of Francis Prescott is widely acknowledged to be based on **Endicott Peabody**, the legendary founder and headmaster of Groton [3]. Like Prescott, Peabody was a formidable Episcopal clergyman who educated generations of the American elite, including Franklin D. Roosevelt [14].

Auchincloss also cited Judge **Learned Hand** as a secondary inspiration for Prescott's character, particularly regarding his intellectual complexity and certain biographical details [3][13]. The author stated that he aimed to create a character more complex than Peabody, one who possessed "moments of doubt to balance his faith" and who was "intellectual, cultivated, occasionally cynical" [13].

### Development
Auchincloss had contemplated writing a novel about a headmaster for years but waited until he felt ready to handle the subject matter [3]. He originally drafted the novel with David Griscam as the frame narrator but discarded this approach because Griscam's personality became too dominant. He settled on Brian Aspinwall as the narrator to provide a contrasting, more neutral perspective [3].

## Reception and Legacy

### Critical Response
Upon its release in 1964, *The Rector of Justin* received widespread critical acclaim. *Harper's Magazine* described it as "almost certainly the finest work ever written about an American preparatory school" [3]. Contemporary critics praised Auchincloss's use of multiple narrators to construct a "cubist" portrait of the Rector, allowing for a nuanced exploration of his character [8].

The novel is frequently cited as Auchincloss's best work [3]. Literary scholar Jonathan Yardley called it a "minor masterpiece of 20th-century literature" and noted its ability to explore universal themes through the specific lens of a boarding school [13].

### Awards and Honors
The novel was a commercial success and a bestseller [3]. It was a finalist for two of America's most prestigious literary awards:
*   **Pulitzer Prize for Fiction (1965):** Finalist (lost to Shirley Ann Grau's *The Keeper of the House*) [3].
*   **National Book Award for Fiction (1965):** Finalist (lost to Saul Bellow's *Herzog*) [3].

The loss to *Herzog* is often interpreted by literary historians as a symbolic moment marking the transition in American literature from the realist novels of manners focused on the upper class to a more diverse, modernist, and avant-garde style [3].
