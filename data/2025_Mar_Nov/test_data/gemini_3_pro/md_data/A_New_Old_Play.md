# A New Old Play

**A New Old Play** (Chinese: 椒麻堂会; pinyin: *Jiāomá tánghuì*) is a 2021 drama film written and directed by Chinese filmmaker and artist Qiu Jiongjiong. A co-production between Hong Kong and France, it is Qiu's first fictional feature film, following a career in documentary filmmaking [1][2][3]. The film stars Yi Sicheng, Guan Nan, Qiu Zhimin, and Gu Tao.

The narrative spans over fifty years of Chinese history, from the Warlord Era of the 1920s through the Cultural Revolution to the 1980s. It centers on Qiu Fu, a "clown" (*chou*) role actor in a Sichuan opera troupe, who recalls his life and career while being escorted to the underworld after his death [4][5][6]. The film is notable for its stylized, handcrafted aesthetic, which draws upon traditional Chinese painting and stagecraft, utilizing a shallow depth of field and lateral camera movements to resemble an unrolling scroll [2][7].

*A New Old Play* premiered at the 74th Locarno Film Festival in August 2021, where it was awarded the Special Jury Prize [1][2]. It subsequently won the Firebird Award for Best Film in the Young Cinema Competition (Chinese-language) at the 46th Hong Kong International Film Festival [4][8].

## Plot

The film is framed by a journey into the afterlife. In the 1980s, Qiu Fu, a renowned performer of the clown role in Sichuan opera, dies. He is met by two guardians of the underworld, Ox-Head and Horse-Face, who are sent to escort him to the Ghost City to perform for Yanluo Wang (the King of Hell) [2][5][9]. As they travel toward the netherworld, Qiu Fu attempts to delay his final departure, prompting a series of reminiscences that chronicle his life and the turbulent history of 20th-century China [2][6].

The narrative flashes back to the 1920s during the Warlord Era. A young Qiu Fu, living in poverty, joins an opera troupe. The troupe is known as the "New-New Theatre School" (or New-New Troupe), founded by Pocky, a military commander and opera enthusiast who rises through the ranks of the local warlords [2][5]. Under the strict, military-style discipline of the troupe, Qiu Fu trains in the art of the *chou* (clown) role.

As the decades pass, the troupe navigates a series of political and social upheavals. During the Second Sino-Japanese War and the subsequent Chinese Civil War, the actors struggle to maintain their livelihood amidst violence and instability [2][10]. Following the establishment of the People's Republic of China, the New-New Troupe is nationalized and renamed the People's Sichuan Opera Troupe [2].

The film depicts the impact of political campaigns on the artists. During the Great Leap Forward and the Great Chinese Famine, the characters face starvation and loss; Qiu Fu's daughter dies during this period [2]. Later, during the Cultural Revolution, Qiu Fu and his contemporaries are persecuted. Despite his status as a celebrated artist, he is humiliated and confined to a pigsty while political turmoil rages outside [5][11].

Returning to the frame story in the underworld, Qiu Fu encounters deceased friends and family members. The journey concludes at a waystation where spirits must drink the "Soup of Oblivion" (Forgetfulness Soup) to erase their memories before reincarnation. Qiu Fu drinks the soup, letting go of a lifetime of art, struggle, and suffering [6][12].

## Cast

The cast includes a mix of professional actors, family members of the director, and fellow independent filmmakers [2][7].

*   **Yi Sicheng** as **Qiu Fu**: The protagonist, a famous Sichuan opera clown. The character is based on the director's grandfather, Qiu Fuxin [2][2].
*   **Guan Nan** as **Tong Huafeng**: Qiu Fu's wife [2][13].
*   **Qiu Zhimin** as **Pocky**: A warlord and the founder of the New-New Theatre School. Qiu Zhimin is the director's father [2][7].
*   **Gu Tao** as **Crooky** (also referred to as Tuo Zi): A servant at the theatre school who later appears in the underworld. Gu Tao is a notable independent documentary filmmaker [2][7].
*   **Xue Xuchun** as **Ah Hei** and **Chicken Foot**: Xue plays dual roles as Qiu Fu's son (Ah Hei) and a cook (Chicken Foot) [2].
*   **Chen Haoyu** as **Young Qiu Fu**: The protagonist as a child [2].

## Production

### Development and Writing
Qiu Jiongjiong, born in 1977 in Leshan, Sichuan, grew up in a family of Sichuan opera performers. His grandfather, Qiu Fuxin (1927–1987), was a celebrated clown-role actor [14][15]. Qiu Jiongjiong began his career as a painter before moving into filmmaking with a series of documentaries, including *Mr. Zhang Believes* (2015) and *Ode to Joy* (2008), the latter of which was a tribute to his grandfather [16][17].

*A New Old Play* was conceived in 2017 after Qiu illustrated a biography of his grandfather. He wrote the screenplay between May 2017 and February 2018 [2]. The film is described as a "pre-biography," blending the factual history of his grandfather's life with fictionalized elements and surreal imagery [18][2]. The Chinese title, *Jiao Ma Tang Hui* (椒麻堂会), references "Jiao Ma," a peppery, numbing flavor characteristic of Sichuan cuisine, symbolizing the complex mixture of joy and pain in the characters' lives [7].

### Filming and Design
Principal photography began in August 2019 in Leshan, Sichuan, and lasted for 83 days [2]. The film was shot entirely within a 400-square-meter studio [19][7].

Qiu Jiongjiong, leveraging his background as a visual artist, served as the production designer. The sets were handcrafted and painted to resemble theatrical stages rather than realistic locations [7]. The visual style is characterized by a "scroll painting" aesthetic, achieved through the use of lateral camera tracking and a shallow depth of field that flattens the image, evoking the look of two-dimensional art or a stage play [2][20][21]. The cinematography was handled by Robbin Feng [2].

The film is performed primarily in the Sichuan dialect, which Qiu chose for its humor and vividness, noting that the "clown spirit" is intrinsic to the region's culture [7][7].

## Themes

The film explores the relationship between art and history, portraying the resilience of artists who must adapt to constantly shifting political landscapes [10]. The "clown" (*chou*) role serves as a central metaphor; Qiu Jiongjiong has described the "clown spirit" as the core of his cinematic grammar, representing a perspective that is simultaneously humorous, resilient, and tragic [7][22].

The narrative structure emphasizes the cyclical nature of history and the blurring of lines between the living and the dead. The underworld is depicted as a bureaucratic extension of the living world, populated by spirits who retain their earthly personalities [9][20]. The film also addresses the persistence of memory and the tragedy of destiny, contrasting the vivid lives of individuals with the overwhelming force of historical events [2].

## Release

*A New Old Play* had its world premiere on August 13, 2021, at the 74th Locarno Film Festival in Switzerland [2]. It was subsequently screened at numerous international film festivals, including the Busan International Film Festival, the International Film Festival Rotterdam, the Göteborg Film Festival, and the Singapore International Film Festival [5][6].

In China, due to censorship restrictions, the film was limited to private screenings between 2021 and 2022 [2]. It received a theatrical release in France on April 30, 2025 [2]. In North America, it was distributed by dGenerate Films (Icarus Films) and released on home media by Kani [23][10].

## Reception

### Critical Response
The film received critical acclaim for its visual inventiveness and narrative scope. *Screen Daily* reported that the jury at the Hong Kong International Film Festival praised the film as "an all-rounded gem" and "one of this year's most distinguished films both creatively and artistically" [4][8]. The FIPRESCI jury at the same festival commended its "masterful approach and inventive visual style" [8].

Reviewers have compared the film's aesthetic to the works of Wes Anderson and Roy Andersson due to its meticulous, handcrafted set designs and tableau framing [24][25]. *The New Yorker* described it as "daring and imaginative," while *Cinema Scope* called Qiu Jiongjiong "the brightest light in the Chinese independent cinema world" [23][22]. The film's blend of personal memoir and epic history has drawn comparisons to Chen Kaige's *Farewell My Concubine* and Zhang Yimou's *To Live*, though critics note Qiu's distinctively surreal and theatrical approach [19][9].
