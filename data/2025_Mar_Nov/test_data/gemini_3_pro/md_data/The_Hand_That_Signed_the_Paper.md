# The Hand that Signed the Paper (novel)

***The Hand that Signed the Paper*** is a 1994 novel by the Australian author Helen Dale, who wrote and published the work under the pseudonym **Helen Demidenko**. The novel depicts the experiences of a Ukrainian family before and during World War II, focusing on their collaboration with the SS and their participation in the Holocaust.

The book is the subject of one of the most significant literary controversies in Australian history, known as the "Demidenko affair." Initially acclaimed by critics, the novel won the Australian/Vogel Literary Award for an unpublished manuscript in 1993 and the prestigious Miles Franklin Award in 1995 [1][2]. Following its publication, the book attracted intense criticism for its perceived anti-Semitic content and historical revisionism regarding the Holodomor (the Ukrainian famine) and the Holocaust [1][3].

The controversy escalated in August 1995 when it was revealed that the author, who had presented herself as the daughter of illiterate Ukrainian immigrants and claimed the novel was based on her family's oral history, was actually Helen Darville, the daughter of British immigrants with no Ukrainian ancestry [1][2]. The revelation sparked a widespread national debate concerning literary authenticity, the responsibilities of prize juries, and the representation of history in fiction [3][4].

## Plot summary

The novel is narrated by Fiona Kovalenko, a student at a university in Queensland, Australia. Fiona is of Australian-Ukrainian descent and is the niece of Vitaly Kovalenko [1][3]. The narrative oscillates between Fiona's contemporary life in Australia, where her uncle Vitaly has been charged with war crimes, and the historical events in Ukraine during the 1930s and 1940s [1].

The historical sections describe the Kovalenko family's life in Ukraine under the Soviet Union. The narrative depicts the suffering of the Ukrainian peasantry during the forced collectivization and the subsequent famine (Holodomor) engineered by Joseph Stalin's regime [5][6]. The novel posits that the family perceived the Communist officials enforcing these policies as predominantly Jewish. Consequently, the narrative suggests that the characters' later collaboration with the Nazis was motivated by a desire for revenge against "Jewish Bolsheviks" whom they held responsible for the famine and the destruction of their community [1][3].

Following the German invasion of the Soviet Union in 1941, Vitaly and other family members welcome the Nazis as liberators. Vitaly enlists in the SS auxiliary forces and serves as a guard at the Treblinka extermination camp [1]. The book includes graphic depictions of atrocities, including the massacre at Babi Yar and the operation of gas chambers, often described in a detached, emotionless tone [1][3].

In the contemporary timeline, Fiona investigates her family's past after discovering photographs of her father and uncle in SS uniforms [1]. The novel concludes with the legal proceedings against Vitaly in Australia, which are ultimately discontinued due to his poor health [5].

## Title

The title of the novel is taken from the poem "The Hand That Signed the Paper" by the Welsh poet Dylan Thomas, published in his 1936 collection *Twenty-Five Poems* [7][8]. The poem explores the theme of the detachment of political power, describing how a "hand" signing a treaty or document can cause mass destruction, famine, and death without the ruler physically witnessing the consequences [7][9].

Critics have noted the relevance of the title to the novel's themes of bureaucratic killing and the distance between orders given and actions taken, although the novel focuses on the "hands" that physically committed the violence rather than just the signatories of treaties [8].

## Publication and awards

Helen Darville wrote the novel while she was a student at the University of Queensland. She submitted the manuscript for the Australian/Vogel Literary Award in 1993 under the name Helen Demidenko [1]. The Vogel Award is Australia's richest prize for an unpublished manuscript by an author under the age of 35. The manuscript won the award, securing a publishing contract with Allen & Unwin [1].

Upon its publication in 1994, the book received positive attention from several literary critics. In 1995, it was awarded the Miles Franklin Award, widely considered Australia's most prestigious literary prize [1][2]. The judging panel included significant literary figures such as Dame Leonie Kramer and Jill Kitson [1][10]. The book also won the Australian Literature Society (ALS) Gold Medal in 1995 [1][11].

The judges praised the novel for its "searingly truthful account" and its ability to imagine the perspective of perpetrators [10][12]. The author's purported identity as a young woman of Ukrainian heritage exploring her own family's dark history was cited as a factor in the book's authenticity and power [3][4].

## The Demidenko Affair

The "Demidenko Affair" refers to the intense public and literary controversy that surrounded the book throughout 1995. The debate occurred in two distinct phases: the initial criticism of the book's content, followed by the exposure of the author's false identity.

### Allegations of anti-Semitism
Before the author's identity was revealed, the novel attracted severe criticism from journalists, historians, and members of the Jewish community. Critics argued that the book was anti-Semitic and historically revisionist [1][3].

The primary criticism was that the novel adopted the "Jewish Bolshevism" conspiracy theory as historical fact rather than merely as the characters' delusion. Critics such as Gerard Henderson and Robert Manne argued that the book justified the actions of Ukrainian war criminals by presenting their participation in the Holocaust as a reciprocal act of vengeance for the famine, which the novel blamed on Jewish Communist officials [4].

Commentators noted that the novel contained stereotypes of Jews and lacked a moral center, with the narrative voice often indistinguishable from the anti-Semitic views of its characters [3][4]. The literary critic Andrew Riemer, while defending the book as a work of fiction, acknowledged that the text could be read as "anti-Semitic in a limited and on the whole tolerable sense" within the context of exploring the perpetrators' mindset [1].

### Exposure of the hoax
During the promotion of the book, the author appeared in public wearing traditional Ukrainian embroidered blouses and spoke of her family's history in Ukraine [3][13]. She claimed her father was an illiterate taxi driver named Demidenko and that the novel was based on interviews with her relatives [1][14].

In August 1995, the *Courier-Mail* and other media outlets revealed that "Helen Demidenko" was a pseudonym for Helen Darville [1]. It was discovered that she was the daughter of Harry and Grace Darville, British immigrants from Scunthorpe, England, and that she had no Ukrainian ancestry [1][11].

The revelation caused a scandal in the Australian literary community. The judges of the Miles Franklin Award were criticized for allegedly prioritizing "multicultural authenticity" over literary merit and for failing to detect the fabrication [3][15]. The hoax raised questions about the "fiduciary contract" between author and reader, particularly regarding fiction that claims to be based on historical fact or oral history [3].

### Aftermath and analysis
Following the exposure, the author issued an apology, stating she used the persona to gain the confidence of the Ukrainian community to research the book, though she later claimed the persona allowed her to write about the Holocaust with a detachment she could not otherwise achieve [10][13].

The controversy spawned several books analyzing the affair:
*   ***The Culture of Forgetting*** (1996) by Robert Manne: Manne argued that the literary establishment's embrace of the book demonstrated a lack of historical knowledge about the Holocaust and a failure of moral judgment [1][16].
*   ***The Demidenko Debate*** (1996) by Andrew Riemer: Riemer offered a defense of the novel as a postmodern text and examined the cultural tensions that fueled the controversy [1][17].

The awards were not revoked, though the controversy led to changes in how literary prizes were administered in Australia [18].

## Author's later career

After the controversy, Helen Darville worked as a school teacher before obtaining a law degree. She later adopted the name **Helen Dale** [11][19]. She practiced law and became a political commentator and writer, identifying as a libertarian. She served as a senior adviser to Australian Senator David Leyonhjelm [11].

In 2017 and 2018, she published a two-part speculative fiction novel titled *Kingdom of the Wicked* (comprising *Rules* and *Order*), which reimagines the trial of Jesus in a technologically advanced Roman Empire [11][19].
