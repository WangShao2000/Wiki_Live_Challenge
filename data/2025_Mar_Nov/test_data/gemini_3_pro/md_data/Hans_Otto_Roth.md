# Hans Otto Roth

**Hans Otto Roth** (April 29, 1890 – April 1, 1953) was a Romanian lawyer, journalist, and politician of Transylvanian Saxon origin. A prominent leader of the German minority in Romania during the interwar period, he served as a member of the Chamber of Deputies and the Senate of Romania for over two decades. Roth is noted for his efforts to integrate the Transylvanian Saxons into the Romanian state following the Union of 1918 and for his political opposition to both National Socialism during World War II and the subsequent Communist regime.

As the parliamentary leader of the German Party (*Deutsche Partei*), Roth advocated for the cultural and educational rights of the German minority. In the 1930s, he came into conflict with the radical "renewal movement" within the Saxon community, which sought to align the minority with the ideology of Nazi Germany. Following the Soviet occupation and the establishment of a communist government in Romania, Roth was arrested as a political dissident. He died in detention at the Ghencea labor camp in 1953.

## Early life and education
Hans Otto Roth was born on April 29, 1890, in Sighișoara (German: *Schäßburg*), which was then part of Austria-Hungary [1][2]. He was the son of Karl Roth (1846–1901), a lawyer and city judge, and Louise Roth (née Hausenblaß) [2].

Roth attended elementary school and the Evangelical Gymnasium in Sighișoara, graduating in 1908. During his time at the gymnasium, he held a leadership position in the student organization *Coetus Chlamydatorum* [2][3]. He went on to study law at universities in Budapest, Vienna, Berlin, and Zurich. He completed his education in 1913, receiving a doctorate in law from the University of Budapest [2][3].

Following his studies, Roth worked in law firms in Budapest. During World War I, he served briefly in the Austro-Hungarian Army but was released from service in 1917 due to illness [2][3]. He subsequently turned to journalism, working as an editor for the *Siebenbürgisch-Deutsches Tageblatt* (Transylvanian-German Daily) in Sibiu (Hermannstadt) from 1917 to 1918 [1][2].

## Political career

### Union of Transylvania with Romania
Following the collapse of Austria-Hungary at the end of World War I, Roth played a central role in the political transition of the Transylvanian Saxons. He served as the secretary of the Saxon Central Committee and the German People's Council for Transylvania [2]. Roth was a key proponent of the German minority's support for the union of Transylvania with the Kingdom of Romania. He was a principal speaker at the Saxon assembly in Mediaș in January 1919, where the community officially declared its support for the union [2][4]. He was also a member of the delegation that delivered the "Mediaș Resolution" to the Romanian government and King Ferdinand I in Bucharest [2].

In November 1919, Roth helped organize the "Fourth Saxon Day" in Sighișoara and authored the "Saxon People's Program," which outlined the political goals of the community within the new Romanian state [3][4].

### Parliamentary activity
Roth was elected to the Romanian Chamber of Deputies in 1919, representing the German Party [3][5]. He retained his seat in subsequent elections, serving continuously until the suspension of the parliamentary system in 1938. In 1922, he succeeded Rudolf Brandsch as the chairman of the German Party's parliamentary group [3][6].

While Brandsch was known as a charismatic orator, Roth was often described as a tactician and a "gray eminence" who focused on legal and pragmatic negotiations with the Romanian government [4]. His political work concentrated on securing educational autonomy for denominational schools and protecting the property rights of the German minority [3]. Due to his long tenure in the legislature, Roth was appointed a Senator by right (Senator for life) in 1939, a position granted to MPs who had been elected in ten consecutive legislatures [3][5].

### International representation
Beyond domestic politics, Roth was active in the Congress of European Nationalities. From 1931 to 1934, he served as the President of the Association of German Minorities in Europe (*Verband der deutschen Volksgruppen in Europa*) [3][5]. In this capacity, he addressed issues regarding the status of ethnic minorities across the continent.

## Opposition to National Socialism
During the 1930s, the German minority in Romania became increasingly polarized by the rise of National Socialism in Germany. A radical faction known as the "Renewal Movement" (*Erneuerungsbewegung*), led by figures such as Fritz Fabritius and later Andreas Schmidt, sought to align the Transylvanian Saxons with Nazi ideology [2][3].

### Meeting with Adolf Hitler
In June 1933, Roth secured an audience with Chancellor Adolf Hitler in Berlin. During this meeting, Roth reportedly protested against the anti-Semitic policies being implemented in Germany. He argued that the persecution of Jews in the Reich would have negative repercussions for German minorities living in other European countries [1][4][5]. Hitler reportedly dismissed Roth's concerns, and the meeting contributed to Roth's alienation from the Nazi movement [3].

### Conflict with the "Renewal Movement"
Roth's conservative, Christian-liberal views placed him in opposition to the radical Nazification of the German minority organizations [3][5]. By the early 1940s, the Nazi-aligned leadership of the German Ethnic Group (*Deutsche Volksgruppe*) in Romania, headed by Andreas Schmidt, viewed Roth as an obstacle.

The conflict escalated personally when Schmidt publicly insulted Roth's son, Herbert, calling him a coward for refusing to join the Waffen-SS. In response, Hans Otto Roth challenged Schmidt to a duel, a challenge Schmidt refused. Roth subsequently filed a lawsuit for insult against Schmidt [2][7]. Consequently, Roth was expelled from the German Party and marginalized within the official minority institutions during the war [3][5].

### Refusal of government posts
In 1940, during the political crisis that led to the abdication of King Carol II, Roth was offered positions in the Romanian government. He was appointed Minister for Minorities in the cabinet of Ion Gigurtu in July 1940 but declined to actively exercise the office or resigned shortly thereafter [5][6]. Later in 1940, the new leader, Marshal Ion Antonescu, offered Roth the position of Minister of Justice or Minister of Education. Roth refused these offers, unwilling to collaborate with a government that included the fascist Iron Guard [2][5].

## World War II and postwar period
Following the Royal Coup on August 23, 1944, which saw Romania switch sides to join the Allies, Roth attempted to navigate the precarious situation of the German minority. He issued appeals to the Transylvanian Saxons to remain loyal to the Romanian state and not to flee with the retreating German army [4][5]. This stance led to accusations from Nazi officials that Roth was "communist-friendly" and was hindering the evacuation of the ethnic German population [8].

Despite his call for loyalty, the German minority faced severe repercussions, including deportation to the Soviet Union for forced labor. Roth, along with Rudolf Brandsch, attempted to negotiate with the new authorities to protect the rights of the community and draft a statute for the German minority, but these efforts were largely unsuccessful in the face of the Soviet occupation [1][9].

## Arrest and death
As the Communist Party consolidated power in Romania, Roth was identified as a "class enemy" due to his background as a bourgeois politician and landowner. He was first arrested by the secret police (Siguranța) in 1948 but was released after several months [1][2].

On April 15, 1952, Roth was arrested again by the Securitate. He received an administrative sentence of 24 months imprisonment on the grounds of being a "former deputy and senator" [1]. He was detained in the Ghencea labor camp in Bucharest. Hans Otto Roth died in custody on April 1, 1953 [1][10].

The official cause of death was listed as uremia, though his family was not immediately notified and the exact circumstances remain a subject of historical inquiry [3]. A memorial service was held at the Black Church in Brașov by Pastor Konrad Möckel, despite the political risks involved [3].

## Personal life
Hans Otto Roth was married to Paula Copony (1895–1976). The couple had two children: a daughter, Maria Luise Roth-Höppner (1930–2024), who became a physicist, and a son, Herbert Roth [1][2]. Both children suffered persecution under the communist regime; Maria Luise and Herbert were arrested in 1958 and sentenced to prison for possessing a letter critical of the regime [1][3].

## Legacy
The *Hans Otto Roth Collection*, comprising documents from 1919 to 1951, is preserved at the Black Church Archives in Brașov. The collection documents his political and journalistic activities and his efforts to oppose both right-wing extremism and communism [11].
