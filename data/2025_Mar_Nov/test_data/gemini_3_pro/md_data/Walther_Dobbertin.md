# Walther Dobbertin

**Walther Alexander Dobbertin** (August 28, 1882 – January 12, 1961) was a German photographer and publisher. He was primarily active in German East Africa (modern-day Tanzania, Burundi, and Rwanda) from 1903 to 1916. Dobbertin is regarded as one of the most significant photographers of the German colonial era in East Africa and is noted for being the only professional photographer to document the German side of the East African campaign during World War I [1][2][3].

His photographic work encompasses landscapes, wildlife, colonial infrastructure, and portraits of indigenous peoples and German settlers [1][4]. Following his imprisonment by British forces during the war and subsequent return to Germany, Dobbertin became involved in the German colonial revisionist movement. During the Nazi era, he was a member of the *Sturmabteilung* (SA) and a local leader in the *Reichskolonialbund* (Reich Colonial League) [1][3]. His photographs remain significant historical documents regarding the visual culture of German colonialism and the ecological history of Tanzania [5][6].

## Early life and education
Walther Dobbertin was born on August 28, 1882, in Berlin, Germany, into a family of artisans [1][3]. His ancestors originated from Mecklenburg, near the Dobbertin Abbey [1]. He completed an apprenticeship in photography in Rostock, where he also attended painting courses [1].

## Career in German East Africa
In 1903, at the age of 21, Dobbertin emigrated to the colony of German East Africa [1][6]. Upon his arrival in the colonial capital of Dar es Salaam, he was initially employed at the photographic studio of Carl Vincenti [1][6].

### Studio establishment
Dobbertin's employment with Vincenti ended in a legal dispute in 1907. Vincenti accused Dobbertin of theft and embezzlement of photographic materials; the court sentenced Dobbertin to three days in prison and ordered him to pay court costs [1]. Following this event, Dobbertin established his own independent business. He opened a studio in Dar es Salaam and expanded his operations to include book and art dealerships in Tanga and Moshi [1][3][6].

Dobbertin developed a reputation as a leading photographer in the colony [1]. His commercial output included postcards and "art editions" depicting African life and scenery, which he began publishing around 1910 [3]. His work documented the development of colonial infrastructure, including the construction of railways and the expansion of sisal plantations [6].

### World War I
Following the outbreak of World War I in 1914, Dobbertin was conscripted into the *Schutztruppe*, the German colonial protection force [2][3]. He served as a member of the *Landsturm* (militia) [2]. During the hostilities, he continued to take photographs, becoming the only known photographer to document the conflict from the perspective of the German forces under Paul von Lettow-Vorbeck [1][2][7]. His wartime photography focused on the activities of the *Schutztruppe* and the *Askari* (indigenous soldiers serving in the colonial army) [3][8].

In 1916, Dobbertin was captured by British forces and held as a prisoner of war [1][2]. Following the German defeat, German settlers were expelled from the territory and their property was expropriated. However, Dobbertin's wife, Alwine, successfully smuggled his photographic plates out of East Africa, preserving his body of work [1][2].

## Later life in Germany
After his release from captivity, Dobbertin returned to Germany in 1920 [6]. He settled in Wiedenhof (now part of Jesteburg), south of Hamburg [1][2]. There, he opened a bookshop and a printing house [1][6].

### Political activities and publishing
During the interwar period, Dobbertin engaged in colonial revisionism, a political movement that sought the return of Germany's former overseas colonies. He printed and distributed postcards featuring his earlier photographs from East Africa, often accompanied by slogans such as "Germans, remember your colonies!" (*Deutsche, gedenkt Eurer Kolonien!*) [6].

In 1932, he self-published a photographic book titled *Die Soldaten Lettow-Vorbecks* (Lettow-Vorbeck's Soldiers). The book contained 120 copperplate engravings of his wartime photographs and presented a glorifying depiction of the German military campaign and the loyalty of the Askari soldiers [1][2][3].

During the National Socialist era, Dobbertin aligned himself with the regime's political organizations. He was a member of the SA, the paramilitary wing of the Nazi Party, and served as a district leader (*Kreisverbandsleiter*) for the *Reichskolonialbund* [1][3][9]. Due to these political affiliations, the British occupation authorities revoked his business license in 1945. The license was later restored, and he continued to operate his bookshop until 1960 [1][3].

Dobbertin died on January 12, 1961, in Jesteburg-Wiedenhof, shortly before a planned return trip to Africa [1][3].

## Photographic themes and legacy
Dobbertin's archive consists of hundreds of black-and-white images [1]. His work is held by the German Federal Archives (*Bundesarchiv*) [6][10].

### Colonial visual culture
Scholars of colonial history and visual culture analyze Dobbertin's work as a representation of the "colonial gaze." His photographs were often framed for administrative and commercial audiences, reinforcing colonial hierarchies [3]. While some images documented the harsh realities of forced labor and military training, others were staged to depict a "trustful relationship" between German officers and Askari soldiers [3]. His postcards of sisal plantations, used for decades after the photos were taken, often rendered the African laborers as anonymous figures within a landscape of industrial production [6].

### Historical and ecological documentation
Beyond their ideological usage, Dobbertin's photographs serve as detailed historical records. His images of the Usambara Mountains and the Kilimanjaro region have been used by researchers to study historical land use, agroforestry, and ecological changes over the last century [5][11]. For example, high-resolution scans of his glass plates have allowed historians to analyze the specific environmental consequences of colonial settlement patterns, such as those at the Trappist monastery in Gare and the Mlalo Kaya [5].
