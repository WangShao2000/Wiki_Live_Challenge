# Robert Jacomb-Hood

**Robert Jacomb-Hood** MICE (25 January 1822 – 10 May 1900) was a British civil engineer and railway executive. He served as the Resident Engineer and later a director of the London, Brighton and South Coast Railway (LB&SCR). His career spanned the mid-19th century expansion of the British railway network, during which he oversaw the construction of major infrastructure including the LB&SCR's terminus at London Victoria station and the expansion of London Bridge station.

Jacomb-Hood was a recognized contributor to the professional development of railway engineering. He was awarded the Telford Medal by the Institution of Civil Engineers in 1858 for his work on the design and logistics of railway stations, advocating for functionality and "judicious arrangement" over architectural ornamentation.

## Early life and education

Robert Jacomb-Hood was born on 25 January 1822 at Riseley in Bedfordshire [1]. He was the eldest son of Robert Jacomb, a yeoman farmer who later assumed the surname Hood. In 1833, his father inherited the Bardon Park estate in Leicestershire from a cousin, William Hood, and subsequently adopted the name Jacomb-Hood [1][2].

Jacomb-Hood received his early education at Christ's Hospital. Following his father's wish for him to pursue a legal career, he attended Trinity College, Cambridge, in 1840 and 1841. However, influenced by interactions with peers engaged in railway construction in the Midlands, he developed an interest in engineering. He eventually persuaded his father to allow him to leave the university to pursue this profession [1].

In 1841, he became an articled pupil of George Watson Buck, the Chief Engineer of the Manchester and Birmingham Railway. During his apprenticeship, he worked as an assistant on the Holmes Chapel section of the line under William Baker, and on the Crewe section under William Henry Barlow [1].

## Career

### Early engineering work
Following the opening of the Manchester and Birmingham line to Crewe in 1843, Jacomb-Hood was retained as a Chief Assistant by George Watson Buck, who had moved into private practice in Manchester. During this period, Jacomb-Hood worked on the rebuilding of the New Bailey Bridge in Salford. He later returned to the Manchester and Birmingham Railway as an assistant to William Baker for the construction of the Macclesfield branch. Between 1844 and 1846, he served as the Resident Engineer on the Manchester, South Junction and Altrincham Railway [1].

### London, Brighton and South Coast Railway (1846–1860)
In 1846, following the amalgamation that formed the London, Brighton and South Coast Railway (LB&SCR), Jacomb-Hood was appointed Resident Engineer for the entire system. He held this position for fourteen years, a period characterized by the rapid development of the company's infrastructure [1].

During his tenure, he oversaw the completion of the new London Bridge station and the Bricklayers Arms goods station. He was responsible for laying out and completing numerous branch lines, including those to Newhaven, Eastbourne, Hailsham, Epsom, Croydon, Wandsworth, Littlehampton, and Steyning [1]. Between 1850 and 1860, he also managed the construction of the Crystal Palace branch, the East Grinstead branch, and lines serving Lewes, Uckfield, Shoreham, and Henfield [1].

One of his most significant projects was the design and construction of the LB&SCR's portion of London Victoria station, which opened on 1 October 1860 [3]. The station was a joint venture with the London, Chatham and Dover Railway (LCDR), but the two companies built separate stations side-by-side. Jacomb-Hood designed the roof of the Brighton side, which was noted for its cost-effectiveness; it was constructed for approximately £17 per square (100 sq ft), roughly two-thirds the cost of the adjacent roof designed by John Fowler for the LCDR [4].

### Private practice and directorships
Due to the increasing pressure of parliamentary work associated with railway expansion, Jacomb-Hood resigned as Resident Engineer in September 1860. He retained a role consulting on parliamentary and new works while establishing a private practice in Westminster [1].

In 1865, he entered into a partnership with his cousin and former pupil, William Jacomb. Together, they developed schemes for various railways, including the Surrey and Sussex, West Kent, and lines connecting Norwood to the Crystal Palace High Level station. The partnership was dissolved in 1870 when William Jacomb was appointed Chief Engineer of the London and South Western Railway [1].

Jacomb-Hood held several directorships during his later career. He became a director of the Crystal Palace Company in 1869, serving until 1879, during which time he was involved in the construction of the Crystal Palace Aquarium. In 1870, he joined the board of the Anglo-Maltese Hydraulic Dock Company, occasionally travelling to Malta to inspect the company's affairs [1].

He renewed his close association with the LB&SCR in March 1883, when he was invited to join the company's board of directors following the retirement of Sir Arthur Otway. He remained a director until his death, devoting his final years to the administration of the railway [1].

## Professional contributions and awards

Jacomb-Hood was an active member of the Institution of Civil Engineers (ICE), having been elected a member in March 1847 [1]. He contributed technical papers that reflected his practical experience with railway infrastructure.

### Vertical Lift Bridge (1850)
In 1850, he presented a paper titled *Description of a Vertical Lift Bridge erected over the Grand Surrey Canal*. This work detailed the engineering of a lifting bridge on the Thames Junction Branch of the LB&SCR. The bridge utilized a timber platform balanced by counterweights and operated by hand-gearing, allowing it to be raised to provide clearance for barges on the canal [5][6]. For this paper, the Institution awarded him a Council Premium of Books [1].

### Railway Station Design (1858)
In 1858, Jacomb-Hood presented a paper titled *The Arrangement and Construction of Railway Stations*. In this text, he argued that the design of stations should be the domain of engineers rather than architects. He introduced the concept of "judicious arrangement," positing that the functionality and logistical efficiency of a station were superior to architectural grandeur [4][7]. He was awarded the Telford Medal, the ICE's highest prize, for this contribution [1].

## Personal life and death

Jacomb-Hood married Jane Stothard Littlewood in the early 1850s. She was the daughter of George Littlewood, a printer [7][8]. The couple had several children. Their son, George Percy Jacomb-Hood (1857–1929), became a painter and illustrator [8][9]. Another son, John Wykeham Jacomb-Hood (1859–1914), followed his father into the engineering profession, eventually becoming the Chief Engineer of the London and South Western Railway [2][10].

Robert Jacomb-Hood died suddenly on 10 May 1900 at his residence in Tunbridge Wells, at the age of 78. He had been transacting business for the Brighton Company on the day of his death [1][11].
