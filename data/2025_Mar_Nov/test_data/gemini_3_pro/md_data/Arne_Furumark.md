# Arne Furumark

**Josef Arne Gerdt Furumark** (September 26, 1903 – October 8, 1982) was a Swedish archaeologist and classical scholar specializing in the Aegean Bronze Age. He served as a professor of Classical Archaeology and Ancient History at Uppsala University and as the director of the Swedish Institute at Athens. Furumark is best known for his establishment of the morphological and stylistic classification system for Mycenaean pottery, which became the standard framework for dating and analyzing Late Bronze Age ceramics in the Mediterranean [1][2]. He also directed excavations at Sinda in Cyprus and San Giovenale in Italy [2][3].

## Early life and education
Furumark was born in Kristiania (now Oslo), Norway, on September 26, 1903 [2]. His father was a businessman in the paper industry. Furumark's early life involved work in his father's offices in Oslo and London. During a stay in London at the age of nineteen, he visited the British Museum, an event he later cited as the catalyst for his decision to pursue archaeology rather than a business career [2].

He enrolled at Uppsala University in Sweden in 1925 to study the humanities. There, he became a student of Axel W. Persson, a prominent archaeologist known for his work on Mycenaean sites [2]. Furumark participated in excavations in Greece under Persson's supervision. He completed his doctoral dissertation, *Studies in Aegean Decorative Art: Antecedent and Sources of the Mycenaean Ceramic Decoration*, at Uppsala University in 1939 [2]. That same year, he was appointed as an associate professor at the university [2].

## Academic career
Furumark spent the majority of his academic career at Uppsala University. He was appointed Professor of Classical Archaeology and Ancient History in 1952, a position he held until his retirement in 1970 [2][4]. During his tenure, he supervised several students who later became notable scholars in the field, including Gisela Walberg and Carl Nylander [2].

In addition to his professorship, Furumark served as the director of the Swedish Institute at Athens from 1956 to 1957 [2]. He was also elected a member of the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences in 1958 [2].

## Classification of Mycenaean pottery
Furumark's most significant contribution to the field of classical archaeology was his comprehensive systematization of Mycenaean ceramics. In 1941, he published his two-volume magnum opus: *The Mycenaean Pottery: Analysis and Classification* and *The Chronology of Mycenaean Pottery* [1][5].

Prior to Furumark's work, the study of Mycenaean pottery relied heavily on the earlier classifications by Adolf Furtwängler and Georg Loeschcke (1886) and the systems devised by Arthur Evans for Minoan Crete [1][6]. Furumark's work provided the first exhaustive typology for the mainland Greek material. He analyzed the ceramics based on two primary criteria:
*   **Shape:** He identified and numbered specific vessel forms, creating a catalog of "Furumark Shapes" (FS) [4][6].
*   **Decoration:** He cataloged decorative patterns, assigning them "Furumark Motif" (FM) numbers [6].

This system allowed for a more precise relative chronology of the Late Helladic period. Furumark divided the pottery into stylistic phases, establishing a sequence that demonstrated the continuity of Mycenaean ceramic traditions and their distinction from Minoan styles [2][6]. His analysis challenged the prevailing view held by Arthur Evans that Mycenaean culture was merely a subordinate offshoot of Minoan civilization, demonstrating instead that mainland pottery had its own independent development while exchanging influences with Crete [2].

The "Furumark" numbers (e.g., FS 1–333) remain a standard international reference for describing Mycenaean and Minoan pottery [4][7]. While subsequent scholars, such as Elizabeth French and Penelope Mountjoy, have refined the chronology and incorporated new findings from stratified deposits, Furumark's typology remains the foundational framework for the discipline [1][6].

## Archaeological fieldwork

### Sinda, Cyprus
Between 1947 and 1948, Furumark directed excavations at the site of Sinda in the Famagusta district of Cyprus [3]. This project marked the resumption of Swedish archaeological activity on the island following World War II [2]. Furumark selected the site after illicit digging revealed Mycenaean IIIC1b sherds, a type of pottery previously undocumented on Cyprus [8][9].

The excavations uncovered a fortified town dating to the Late Bronze Age. Furumark identified three distinct occupation phases:
*   **Sinda I:** A town with a defense system, ending in destruction.
*   **Sinda II:** A reconstruction featuring Mycenaean IIIC1a and locally produced IIIC1b pottery, also ending in destruction.
*   **Sinda III:** A poorer reoccupation characterized by "Close Style" pottery, eventually abandoned [9].

Furumark originally attributed the destruction of Sinda I to Greek settlers (c. 1230 BC) and the destruction of Sinda II to the Sea Peoples (c. 1190 BC) [10]. However, these interpretations and the associated chronology were later challenged. Paul Åström, who contributed to the final publication of the Sinda excavations in 2003, proposed a lowered chronology and suggested that the destructions might have been caused by pirates or internal conflicts rather than the specific migration waves Furumark hypothesized [9][10].

### San Giovenale, Italy
From 1962 to 1963, Furumark conducted excavations at San Giovenale, an Etruscan site in Italy [2]. These excavations were part of a larger project initiated by the Swedish Institute in Rome in the 1950s. Furumark's work focused on the "Borgo" area of the site [11].

## Other research
### Linear B
Furumark was involved in the early discussions regarding the decipherment of Linear B, the syllabic script used for writing Mycenaean Greek. Although he was initially skeptical of the theory that Linear B represented the Greek language, he corresponded with Michael Ventris, the architect who deciphered the script [2][12]. Following Ventris's breakthrough in 1952, Furumark was among the first archaeologists to accept the decipherment and apply it to his work [2]. His correspondence from the 1950s includes letters from prominent philologists and archaeologists such as John Chadwick and Massimo Pallottino regarding the script [12].

### Italian Prehistory
In 1947, Furumark published *Det äldsta Italien* (*The Oldest Italy*), a handbook on Italian prehistory [2][13]. While intended for a Swedish audience, the work incorporated his research into the archaeological connections between the Aegean and Italy during the Bronze Age [4].
