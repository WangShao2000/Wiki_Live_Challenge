# Sima de los Huesos hominins

The **Sima de los Huesos hominins** (Spanish for "Pit of Bones") are a group of Middle Pleistocene hominin fossils discovered in the Sierra de Atapuerca in northern Spain. The site contains the remains of at least **28 individuals** of a hominin population that lived approximately **430,000 years ago** [1]. These fossils represent one of the most significant Middle Pleistocene hominin assemblages in the world, providing crucial insights into human evolution in Europe and the origins of the Neanderthal lineage [2].

## 1. Discovery and excavation history

The Sima de los Huesos site was first brought to scientific attention in **1976** with the discovery of a nearly complete ancient human mandible [2]. However, systematic excavations did not begin until **1984** under the direction of Spanish paleontologist Juan Luis Arsuaga [3]. Since then, the site has been continuously excavated in month-long field seasons [3].

The site is located at the bottom of a **13-meter (43-foot) deep vertical shaft** or "chimney," accessible through narrow corridors of the Cueva Mayor (Great Cave) system in the Sierra de Atapuerca mountains . The challenging access to the site, requiring spelunking through tight passages and descending the vertical shaft, has made excavation work particularly difficult and time-consuming .

The discovery of such a large concentration of human fossils in one location was unprecedented in European paleoanthropology. By **2016**, researchers had recovered more than **7,000 human fossils** representing the 28 individuals [3]. The site has yielded **more Pleistocene human fossils than any other site** on Earth [3], making it an invaluable resource for understanding human evolution during the Middle Pleistocene period.

## 2. Geological context and dating

The Sima de los Huesos site is part of the larger Atapuerca archaeological complex, which was designated a **UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2000** . The site is situated in a karst limestone formation in the Sierra de Atapuerca mountains of Burgos province, northern Spain .

### 2.1 Stratigraphy and sedimentology

The hominin remains are embedded in a **mud breccia** (a type of sedimentary rock containing angular fragments in a matrix) that underlies an accumulation of Middle Pleistocene cave bear (Ursus deningeri) remains . The sediments vary greatly over distances of just a few meters, typical of interior cave facies, and were caused by cycles of cut and fill .

The lack of bedding and the chaotic abundance of fragile speleothem (cave formation) clasts in the fossiliferous muds suggest that the deposit was originally a **subterranean pond facies**. After emplacement of the human remains, the deposit underwent vigorous post-depositional rotation, collapse, and brecciation, likely caused by underlying bedrock dissolution and undermining .

### 2.2 Dating methods and results

The age of the Sima de los Huesos hominins has been determined through multiple dating techniques:

**Uranium-series (U-series) dating** of speleothem clasts among the human fossils indicates that all are at or close to isotopic equilibrium, suggesting an age greater than **350,000 years** .

**Electron Spin Resonance (ESR)** analysis of selected bear bones yielded dates of **189 ± 28 thousand years ago**, which were concordant with corresponding U-series dates of **181 ± 41 thousand years ago** .

**Luminescence dating** of sediments overlying the hominin-bearing deposits provided more precise dates. Post-infrared-infrared stimulated luminescence (PIR-IR) dating of K-feldspars gave a weighted mean age of **433 ± 15 thousand years**, while thermally transferred optically stimulated luminescence (TT-OSL) dating of individual quartz grains yielded **416 ± 19 thousand years** [4].

**Paleomagnetic analysis** of the sediments preserved both normal and reversed magnetic components. The underlying sterile unit has exclusively reversed magnetization, dating to the Matuyama chron (older than 780,000 years), while the overlying fossiliferous muds have a dominant normal magnetization overprinting a partially resolved reversed magnetization [5].

Combined, these dating methods indicate that the Sima de los Huesos hominins lived approximately **430,000 years ago** during the Middle Pleistocene period [1].

## 3. The hominin remains

### 3.1 Number of individuals and preservation

The Sima de los Huesos site has yielded remains from at least **28 individuals** representing a single population [2]. By 2016, researchers had reconstructed **17 crania** from these 28 individuals [2]. The remains include individuals of both sexes and various ages at death, providing a comprehensive picture of the population structure .

The preservation of the fossils is exceptional, particularly considering their age. The bones show little deformation, allowing for detailed anatomical analysis . This excellent preservation has enabled researchers to extract ancient DNA from the remains, including **mitochondrial DNA in 2013** and **nuclear DNA in 2016**—the oldest hominin DNA ever recovered [2].

### 3.2 Key individual fossils

Several notable individual fossils have been recovered from the site:



* **Skull 5**, nicknamed "**Miguelón**" (Big Michael), is a nearly complete cranium

* **Skull 4**, nicknamed "**Agamenón**" (Agamemnon), is a fragmented cranial remains

* **Skull 6**, nicknamed "**Rui**" (a reference to the medieval military leader El Cid), is another fragmented cranium

* **Pelvis 1**, humorously nicknamed "**Elvis**", is a complete pelvis

### 3.3 Anatomical characteristics

The Sima de los Huesos hominins display a mix of primitive and derived characteristics that place them on the evolutionary line leading to Neanderthals [6].

**Cranial features**:



* Average skull volume of approximately **1,230 cm³**, about half a cup less than Neanderthals (1,410 cm³) or modern humans (1,350 cm³)

* Thick skull bones with prominent supraorbital ridges (brow ridges)

* Lack of a prominent chin, typical of archaic Homo

* Dental and jaw anatomy generally Neanderthal-like

* Tooth rows are square-shaped, similar to some Neanderthals

**Postcranial features**:



* Stature similar to Neanderthals: males about **5 foot 7 (170 cm)** and **170 pounds (77 kg)**, females around **5 foot 2 (157 cm)** and **125 pounds (57 kg)**

* Robust bone structure suggesting a body heavier than modern humans for their height [2]

* Thick cortical bone and bone walls in limb bones [7]

### 3.4 Pathological conditions

Several individuals show evidence of pathological conditions, providing insights into health and social behavior in this ancient population:

**Craniosynostosis in a child**: A pre-adolescent individual (Cranium 14), nicknamed "**Benjamina**" (meaning "the most beloved" in Hebrew), suffered from congenital craniosynostosis—a condition where the skull bones fuse prematurely . Analysis revealed that the fusion began during the 28th week of gestation. Benjamina survived to at least **10 years of age**, indicating that the child received care beyond what parents could provide alone . CT scans showed signs of elevated intracranial pressure, likely causing psychomotor developmental problems .

**Trauma and violence**: Several skulls show evidence of traumatic injuries. One individual (Cranium 17) shows two penetrating lesions on the frontal bone above the left eye, likely caused by two separate impacts from the same object . Forensic analysis suggests this individual was killed in an act of **lethal interpersonal violence** . Eight additional skulls show evidence of trauma .

**Arthritis and aging**: An aged individual nicknamed "**Elvis**" (the pelvis) showed locomotive impairments, representing the oldest evidence of an elderly individual with evident signs of aging and impairment [8].

## 4. Taxonomic classification and phylogenetic relationships

### 4.1 Initial classification

The Sima de los Huesos hominins were initially classified as **Homo heidelbergensis**, a species thought to have populated Europe before the Neanderthals . This classification was based on morphological similarities with the type specimen from Mauer, Germany, and other Middle Pleistocene European fossils .

### 4.2 Reclassification to the Neanderthal lineage

However, in **2014**, a study led by Juan Luis Arsuaga published in the journal Science raised doubts about this assignment. The study suggested removing the Sima population from Homo heidelbergensis because of its evident similarities to Homo neanderthalensis .

This reclassification was strongly supported by subsequent genetic studies:



* **2014 mitochondrial DNA study**: The first ancient DNA extracted from Sima remains showed surprising results. The mitochondrial DNA was found to be closer to **Denisovan hominins** than to Neanderthals . This was unexpected because Denisovans were thought to be restricted to Asia.

* **2016 nuclear DNA study**: A landmark study published in Nature analyzed nuclear DNA from four Sima specimens. The results conclusively showed that the Sima hominins **belonged to the Neanderthal evolutionary lineage**, not Denisovans [9]. The nuclear DNA placed them on the evolutionary branch leading to Neanderthals after it split from the lineage leading to Homo sapiens .

The nuclear DNA analysis also indicated that the **divergence between Neanderthals and Denisovans predated 430,000 years ago** [9], much earlier than previously thought.

### 4.3 Current understanding

Based on the combined morphological and genetic evidence, the Sima de los Huesos hominins are now considered to be **early Neanderthals** or **pre-Neanderthals**—representing a population on the direct evolutionary line leading to later Neanderthals [2].

The dental histology results support this classification, showing that the Sima hominins exhibited **large coronal and root dentine dimensions, as well as thin enamel**—a histological pattern traditionally considered a distinctive trait of Neanderthals [10].

## 5. Archaeological context and associated finds

### 5.1 The Excalibur handaxe

One of the most intriguing finds from the Sima de los Huesos is a single artifact—a teardrop-shaped **handaxe** made of red and yellow quartzite, nicknamed "**Excalibur**" [11]. This is the **only artifact** discovered among thousands of hominin fossils at the site .

The handaxe has several unusual characteristics:



* It is approximately **500,000 years old** [11]

* It was **not made from local materials** [12]

* It shows no signs of use [12]

* It has been suggested to be a ritual offering [12]

The presence of this single, unused artifact of non-local origin has led some researchers to speculate that it may have been placed at the site as a symbolic or ritual object [12].

### 5.2 Associated fauna

The Sima de los Huesos deposits contain remains of other species, particularly **cave bears (Ursus deningeri)** [13]. The mitochondrial genome of the cave bear from Sima de los Huesos shows that it is a sister lineage to late Pleistocene cave bears (Ursus spelaeus) [13].

Other carnivore remains found at the site include:



* Lions

* Foxes

* More than 175 bears

Interestingly, **no typical prey animals** such as mountain goat or deer were found, which has implications for understanding how the human remains accumulated at the site .

## 6. Taphonomy and formation of the deposit

The question of how the human remains came to be in the Sima de los Huesos has been the subject of much debate among researchers. The site presents a unique taphonomic puzzle because of its unusual location at the bottom of a deep vertical shaft.

### 6.1 Proposed hypotheses

Several hypotheses have been proposed to explain the accumulation of human remains:



1. **Carnivore activity**: Initially, researchers considered whether carnivores might have dragged prey into the cave. However, analysis of tooth marks on bones showed that only about **4%** of human and carnivore bones had bite marks, suggesting scavenging was minimal .

2. **Geological processes**: The possibility of a sinkhole, landslide, or flooding was investigated, but researchers were able to rule out these geological events as the primary cause .

3. **Accidental death**: The hominins might have fallen accidentally into the shaft while exploring the cave system.

4. **Intentional disposal**: The excavation team led by Juan Luis Arsuaga has proposed the most controversial hypothesis—that the bodies were **intentionally deposited** by other humans as part of early mortuary practices .

### 6.2 Evidence for intentional disposal

Supporting the intentional disposal hypothesis:



* The site is **extremely difficult to access**, requiring descent through a 13-meter vertical shaft

* The lack of small bones (which would be more easily washed away) suggests the bodies were placed there before significant decomposition

* The presence of the single, unused handaxe of non-local origin suggests ritual behavior [12]

* The concentration and articulation of some bones indicate they were placed in the pit as complete or nearly complete bodies

### 6.3 Evidence against intentional disposal

Arguments against intentional disposal include:



* The chaotic nature of the deposits, with bones mixed and jumbled

* Evidence of post-depositional disturbance and brecciation

* The possibility that the bodies could have been washed in by underground streams

## 7. Cultural and behavioral implications

### 7.1 Earliest evidence of ritual behavior?

If the intentional disposal hypothesis is correct, the Sima de los Huesos could represent the **earliest documented evidence of ritual behavior** in human evolution—more than 300,000 years before the well-attested burial sites at Es Skhul and Qafzeh in Israel [2].

This interpretation has profound implications for understanding the cognitive and symbolic capabilities of Middle Pleistocene hominins. The presence of the Excalibur handaxe as a possible ritual offering further supports this hypothesis [12].

### 7.2 Evidence of violence and social behavior

The trauma evidence provides insights into the social behavior of these ancient hominins:



* At least one individual was killed in an act of **lethal interpersonal violence**

* Multiple skulls show evidence of blunt force trauma

* The pattern of injuries suggests face-to-face combat

This evidence demonstrates that **lethal violence** was present in human societies at least 430,000 years ago .

### 7.3 Care for the disabled and elderly

The survival of individuals with disabilities provides compelling evidence for **social care** in Middle Pleistocene populations:



* The child with craniosynostosis (Benjamina) survived to at least 10 years despite severe developmental disabilities

* An elderly individual with locomotive impairments (Elvis) also survived to advanced age [8]

These cases suggest that the Sima de los Huesos population had developed a social system that included **care for the disabled and elderly**—behaviors traditionally associated with modern human societies .

### 7.4 Speech and language capabilities

Research on the auditory capacities of the Sima de los Huesos hominins has yielded important insights into their potential for speech and language:



* Analysis of the ear structures shows they had **auditory capacities similar to modern humans** in the frequency range up to 5 kHz [14]

* This includes sensitivity to the critical frequency range of 2-4 kHz, which contains important acoustic information in human speech

* The presence of a hyoid bone (though fragmentary) suggests no anatomical barriers to speech production [15]

These findings suggest that **speech and language capabilities** may have evolved much earlier than previously thought—possibly more than 500,000 years ago .

## 8. Scientific significance

The Sima de los Huesos hominins represent one of the most important discoveries in paleoanthropology, with significance spanning multiple fields:

### 8.1 Understanding human evolution

The site provides an unprecedented glimpse into a **single Middle Pleistocene population**—more than 80% of all human fossils from the Chibanian age (Middle Pleistocene) have come from this site [2]. This large sample size allows researchers to study population variation and evolutionary trends with greater confidence than from single-fossil discoveries.

### 8.2 Neanderthal origins

The Sima de los Huesos fossils are now recognized as the **oldest Neanderthals** or direct ancestors of Neanderthals . They bridge the gap between earlier European hominins like Homo antecessor and later Neanderthals, providing crucial evidence for the evolutionary transition.

### 8.3 Ancient DNA breakthroughs

The site has yielded the **oldest hominin DNA ever recovered**—mitochondrial DNA in 2013 and nuclear DNA in 2016 [2]. This ancient DNA has revolutionized our understanding of:



* The timing of the Neanderthal-Denisovan split

* The phylogenetic relationships among ancient hominins

* The evolutionary history of the Neanderthal lineage

### 8.4 Insights into ancient human behavior

The site provides evidence for several behaviors previously thought to be unique to modern humans or much later in human evolution:



* Possible **ritual/funerary behavior** (if the intentional disposal hypothesis is correct) [2]

* **Care for disabled individuals**

* **Care for elderly individuals** [8]

* **Complex social organization** capable of supporting vulnerable group members

* **Speech and language capabilities**

## 9. Ongoing research and future directions

### 9.1 Continued excavations

Excavations at Sima de los Huesos continue today under the direction of Juan Luis Arsuaga and his team [3]. It is estimated that **more than half of the deposit** remains unexcavated , promising many future discoveries.

### 9.2 Technological advances

Recent technological advances are opening new possibilities for research on the Sima fossils:



* **Improved ancient DNA extraction techniques** may allow recovery of more complete genomes

* **High-resolution CT scanning** enables detailed analysis of internal structures

* **3D printing and digital reconstruction** techniques help researchers study fragile specimens

* **Advanced taphonomic analysis** using modern forensic techniques provides new insights into the circumstances of death

### 9.3 Open questions

Several important questions remain unanswered:



* What was the **exact cause of death** for most individuals?

* Was the accumulation of bodies at the site truly **intentional**?

* What was the **social organization** of this population?

* How did they **acquire and use** the Excalibur handaxe?

* What was the **relationship** between the Sima population and other Middle Pleistocene hominins in Europe?

## 10. Conclusion

The Sima de los Huesos hominins represent a pivotal discovery in our understanding of human evolution. These **430,000-year-old remains** from northern Spain provide an unparalleled window into Middle Pleistocene human populations and the origins of the Neanderthal lineage.

The site's significance extends far beyond the number of fossils recovered. It has yielded the **oldest hominin DNA ever analyzed**, revolutionizing our understanding of human evolutionary relationships. The fossils show evidence of **complex social behaviors** including care for disabled individuals and possible ritual practices. The presence of traumatic injuries reveals that violence was part of ancient human society.

While questions remain about how the bodies came to be in the pit and the exact nature of this population's cultural practices, the Sima de los Huesos continues to yield important insights into what makes us human. As excavations continue and new technologies are applied, we can expect this remarkable site to reveal even more about our ancient ancestors and our shared human heritage.

The Sima de los Huesos hominins remind us that the story of human evolution is far from complete. Each new discovery, each piece of ancient DNA, and each carefully excavated bone brings us closer to understanding our place in the evolutionary history of life on Earth.
