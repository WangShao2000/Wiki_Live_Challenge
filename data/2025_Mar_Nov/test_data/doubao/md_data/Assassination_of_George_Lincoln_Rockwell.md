# Assassination of George Lincoln Rockwell

## 1. Overview

On August 25, 1967, George Lincoln Rockwell, the founder and leader of the American Nazi Party (later renamed the National Socialist White People's Party), was shot and killed in Arlington, Virginia [1]. Rockwell, who was 49 years old at the time of his death, was shot twice by John Patler, a former member of his organization . The assassination occurred shortly after noon as Rockwell was leaving the Econ-O-Wash laundromat at the Dominion Hills Shopping Center [1].

Rockwell was a controversial figure in American politics, known for his neo-Nazi and white supremacist views . He had founded the American Nazi Party in 1959 after retiring from the U.S. Navy . His assassination marked a significant event in the history of American extremist movements and led to internal struggles within his organization [2].

## 2. Background

### 2.1 George Lincoln Rockwell's Life and Career

George Lincoln Rockwell was born on March 9, 1918, in Bloomington, Illinois [3]. He grew up in a relatively affluent family - his father, George Lovejoy "Doc" Rockwell, was a successful businessman whose friends included notable entertainers such as Fanny Brice, Groucho Marx, George Burns, and Jack Benny [3].

Rockwell attended Brown University where he studied philosophy [3]. After leaving university, he joined the U.S. Navy in 1941 and served as a pilot during World War II [3]. He rose to the rank of lieutenant commander before being discharged in 1960 due to his increasingly radical political views [3].

Following his military service, Rockwell moved to Arlington, Virginia, where he founded the World Union of Free Enterprise National Socialists in 1959 . Later that year, he renamed the organization the American Nazi Party . The party espoused white supremacist, anti-Semitic, and neo-Nazi ideologies . By the time of his death, the party had approximately 300 active members nationwide and about 3,000 financial supporters .

### 2.2 John Patler's Background

John Patler, whose birth name was Yanacki Christos Patsalos, was born on January 6, 1938 . He came from a poor immigrant Greek family and grew up in New York City . Patler had a troubled youth - he had been a member of a street gang and reportedly killed a childhood friend at age 16 . He also had a history of mental illness .

Patler changed his name to "Patler" to make it sound more like "Hitler" . He joined the American Nazi Party in 1960 and rose to the rank of captain . He served as the editor and cartoonist for the party's magazine, "Stormtrooper" . However, his relationship with Rockwell deteriorated significantly in 1967.

In March 1967, Rockwell relieved Patler of his duties as editor . On April 4, 1967, Patler was summarily dismissed from the party at Rockwell's direction [4]. According to witness testimony, Patler made statements in June 1967 that "Rockwell is an evil genius and he must be stopped" [4]. At the time of his dismissal, Patler was attempting to organize a group called the "White Power Committee" .

## 3. The Assassination

### 3.1 Events Leading to the Shooting

Prior to the fatal shooting, there had been at least one previous attempt on Rockwell's life. On June 28, 1967, as Rockwell was driving into the party barracks' driveway on Wilson Boulevard, he found it blocked by a felled tree and brush . As a young boy cleared the obstruction, two shots were fired at Rockwell from behind one of the swastika-embossed brick driveway pillars . One shot ricocheted off his car right next to his head .

Following this incident, Rockwell petitioned the Arlington County Circuit Court for a gun permit on June 30, 1967, but no action was ever taken on his request .

### 3.2 The Fatal Shooting

On August 25, 1967, at approximately 11:58 a.m., Rockwell was shot while leaving the Econ-O-Wash laundromat at the Dominion Hills Shopping Center in Arlington, Virginia [1]. According to witnesses, Rockwell had told the proprietor that he needed to return home to fetch some bleach [1].

Patler fired two shots from the rooftop of a beauty salon in the shopping center using a Mauser semi-automatic pistol [5]. The bullets went through the windshield of Rockwell's 1958 Chevrolet . Rockwell's car slowly rolled backwards to a stop .

Rockwell staggered out of the front passenger side door, pointed toward the shopping center roof, and then collapsed face up on the pavement . He died within two minutes from massive internal bleeding caused by a bullet that had ripped through several major arteries just above his heart .

### 3.3 Patler's Arrest

Following the shooting, Patler ran along the shopping center roof and jumped to the ground at the rear . A shop owner and a customer briefly gave chase but were unable to get a clear look at the fleeing figure .

Approximately 30 minutes after the shooting, Patler was arrested at a bus stop several miles away by a passing patrolman familiar with the Arlington Nazis . When arrested, Patler had wet shoes and pants, which he claimed was from walking through wet grass and striking shrubbery . He was also sweating profusely .

## 4. Investigation and Trial

### 4.1 Evidence Collection

The investigation into Rockwell's murder revealed several key pieces of evidence :



1. The murder weapon was identified as a Mauser pistol with serial number 9811, which had been purchased by Robert A. Lloyd III in March 1962 . Lloyd testified that he had loaned the gun to Patler in the fall of 1964 and that Patler had never returned it .

2. Another party member, Robert Lee Pace, testified that he had seen Patler in possession of a Mauser "broomstick" pistol (the same type as the murder weapon) in January or February 1966 .

3. Ballistics experts from the FBI testified that shell casings and spent bullets found on the farm of Patler's father-in-law, Sam Ervin, were fired by the murder weapon .

4. A coat identified as belonging to Patler was found near the park along the escape route .

5. Footprints found on the roof of the shopping center did not match Patler's shoes, though it was established that two workmen had been on the roof earlier that day .

### 4.2 The Trial

Patler's trial began on November 27, 1967, at the Arlington County Courthouse under tight security . The testimony consumed approximately three weeks, and the printed record was more than 1,500 pages long .

The prosecution's case relied largely on circumstantial evidence . Key testimony included:



1. Christopher Vidnjevich testified that Patler had made statements in June 1967 that "Rockwell is an evil genius and he must be stopped" [4].

2. Several witnesses testified about the strained relationship between Rockwell and Patler, including their arguments and Patler's dismissal from the party .

3. Mrs. Alma Kilpatrick testified that she had seen a man matching Patler's description running from the scene .

4. Hall, a witness, testified that he had recognized and identified Patler as he fled past Hall's house less than two blocks from the scene .

Patler testified in his own defense, denying that he had fired the fatal shots . He provided an alibi, claiming to have been approximately three miles from the shopping center at the time of the shooting . He also denied being angry or bitter toward Rockwell, stating that their differences were purely political .

### 4.3 Verdict and Sentence

After thirty hours of deliberation, the jury found Patler guilty of first-degree murder . On December 15, 1967, he was found guilty and released on bond to await sentencing [6]. On February 23, 1968, Patler was sentenced to 20 years in prison - the minimum sentence for a first-degree murder conviction at that time [6].

The Virginia Circuit Court postponed imprisonment pending Patler's appeal . However, on November 30, 1970, the Virginia Supreme Court upheld Patler's conviction and 20-year sentence . On May 16, 1972, the U.S. Supreme Court unanimously rejected Patler's appeal based on claims of witness contamination .

## 5. Aftermath

### 5.1 Funeral Controversy

The aftermath of Rockwell's death was marked by significant controversy, particularly regarding his burial . Initially, there was a conflict between Rockwell's family and Matt Koehl, the second-in-command at the NSWPP, over possession of the body .

Rockwell's parents wanted a private burial in Maine but did not feel up to a public fight with the Nazis for his body . On August 27, 1967, an NSWPP spokesman reported that federal officials had given verbal approval for a planned military burial at Culpeper National Cemetery, which Rockwell was entitled to as an honorably discharged veteran [7].

However, on August 29, when several dozen NSWPP troopers and about 100 party supporters arrived at the cemetery, they were met by General Carl C. Turner and 60 military police who had been rushed in from Vint Hill . The military officials enforced the U.S. Army's burial protocol, which prohibited mourners bearing Nazi insignia from entering the cemetery .

The NSWPP troopers refused to remove their uniforms, which led to a day-long standoff . They unsuccessfully tried to force their way into the cemetery three separate times, and several arrests resulted . With daylight fading, General Turner declared that Rockwell could not be buried until the NSWPP made a new request to the Pentagon and agreed to follow protocol .

The Nazis returned to Arlington with Rockwell's body. Plans were made to bury him in Spotsylvania County, but these fell apart when local Jewish organizations protested . Fearing that Arlington County officials might seize the body, the ANP had Rockwell cremated on August 30, 1967 . A memorial service was held that afternoon at party headquarters .

### 5.2 Legacy and Impact

Rockwell's assassination had a profound impact on the American Nazi Party and the broader white supremacist movement in the United States. Matt Koehl, who had been Rockwell's second-in-command, moved quickly to establish legal control over Rockwell's body and all NSWPP assets .

The party continued under Koehl's leadership but faced significant challenges. By 1983, Koehl renamed the organization "The New Order" and made it more overtly religious, espousing that Hitler had been sent down to earth by a divine entity . This represented a shift toward what is sometimes called "esoteric Nazism" [8].

Rockwell's ashes were reportedly kept by Koehl and are believed to reside with The New Order headquarters in New Berlin, Wisconsin . The organization continues to exist today, though it remains small and marginalized [8].

Patler's sentence was eventually reduced, and he was paroled in August 1975 after serving less than four years of his sentence . Judge Charles S. Russell, who had presided over Patler's murder trial, wrote a lengthy letter to the parole board supporting Patler's release - the only time he ever did this in his career .

However, Patler violated the terms of his parole in 1976 and was returned to prison for an additional six years . After serving the remainder of his sentence, John Patsalos (he had legally changed his name back) returned to the New York City area . He maintained his innocence until his death in 2007 .

The assassination of George Lincoln Rockwell remains a significant event in the history of American extremism. It marked the end of an era for the American Nazi Party under Rockwell's charismatic leadership and led to a period of fragmentation and ideological evolution within the white supremacist movement [8].
