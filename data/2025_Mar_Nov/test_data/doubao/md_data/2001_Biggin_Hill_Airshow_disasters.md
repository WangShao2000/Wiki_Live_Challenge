# 2001 Biggin Hill Airshow disasters

## Lead section

The 2001 Biggin Hill Airshow disasters were two separate fatal aircraft crashes that occurred during the 39th Biggin Hill Airshow in Kent, England, on 2 and 3 June 2001 [1]. These incidents resulted in the deaths of three experienced pilots and marked one of the worst airshow accidents in recent British aviation history . The crashes involved a de Havilland Vampire T11 jet trainer on the first day and a Bell P-63 Kingcobra fighter aircraft on the second day .

The airshow was held over the weekend of 2-3 June 2001 at Biggin Hill Airport in Kent . The first incident occurred on Saturday 2 June when a de Havilland Vampire T11, registered G-DHAV, crashed during a formation display with a Sea Vixen aircraft . Both pilots on board—Air Marshal Sir Kenneth Hayr, a 66-year-old former Deputy Chief of Defence Staff, and electronics engineer Jonathan Kerr—were killed instantly [2].

The second crash happened on Sunday 3 June when a Bell P-63A Kingcobra, registered G-BTWR, crashed during a solo display . The pilot, Guy Bancroft-Wilson, a 43-year-old British Airways captain and former Red Arrows pilot, was killed instantly when the aircraft spiralled out of control and crashed into trees near the airfield [3].

The Air Accidents Investigation Branch (AAIB) conducted investigations into both crashes . The investigation into the Vampire crash identified wake turbulence from the larger Sea Vixen aircraft as the likely cause of the accident [4]. The Kingcobra crash investigation determined that the pilot had entered a spin at the top of a vertical manoeuvre and was unable to recover .

The disasters led to several safety recommendations and changes in airshow regulations [5]. The incidents highlighted the risks associated with formation flying between aircraft of significantly different sizes and weights, as well as the importance of adequate communication systems at airshows .

## Background and context

The 39th Biggin Hill Airshow was scheduled to take place over the weekend of 2-3 June 2001 at Biggin Hill Airport in Kent, England . The airshow was a major event attracting thousands of spectators and featured a variety of historic military aircraft displays .

The airshow had been running since 1978, with the previous fatal incident occurring in 1980 . Biggin Hill spokesman Nick Smith stated that the venue had a relatively safe history prior to these incidents, noting "I have been here since 1978 working on the show and only in 1980 were there any fatalities. Now in two days we have lost two aircraft and we have got two lots of fatalities" .

The airshow featured a diverse range of aircraft, including vintage military aircraft from World War II and later periods . The display programme included both solo performances and formation flying, with aircraft of various sizes and weights participating .

## The Vampire crash (2 June 2001)

### Incident details

The first fatal crash occurred on Saturday 2 June 2001 at approximately 18:02 local time . A de Havilland Vampire T11, registered G-DHAV, was participating in a formation display with a de Havilland Sea Vixen aircraft when it suddenly spiralled out of control and crashed [6].

The Vampire was being flown by Air Marshal Sir Kenneth Hayr, a 66-year-old former Deputy Chief of Defence Staff who had retired in 1992 [7]. In the right seat was Jonathan Kerr, a 32-year-old electronics engineer from Bournemouth who was serving as a safety pilot [2]. Kerr had only left his job at Swansea Airport the previous day to pursue his dream of restoring vintage aircraft full time .

Witnesses reported that the Vampire had flown over the crowd four times before it corkscrewed twice and plummeted into a ridge, bursting into flames . The aircraft crashed approximately 850 metres from the end of Runway 03/21, outside the airfield boundary, in a field of long grass .

The crash occurred during a manoeuvre where the Vampire was following the Sea Vixen in a wingover manoeuvre . The Vampire was flying behind the larger Sea Vixen when it was suddenly upset by jet efflux and wing tip vortices . The aircraft rolled inverted and dived into the ground at a 45-degree angle, banked steeply to the right, and struck the ground before disintegrating in flames .

### Victims

Air Marshal Sir Kenneth Hayr was a highly decorated military officer who had served in the Royal Air Force throughout the Falklands and Gulf Wars . He had planned the RAF's role in the Desert Storm operation during the Gulf War and received the Kuwait Liberation Order (first grade) for his service in 1991, as well as the Knight Commander of the Order of the British Empire (KBE) .

Hayr was described as "one of the best" pilots in the RAF and had a passion for flying fighter aircraft . His hobbies included display aerobatics, paragliding, and parachuting . He had moved to the Midlands from his native New Zealand and had lost his wife Joyce in 1987. They had three sons .

Jonathan Kerr was described by his family as "a very special person, who lived for his passion and died living it" . He had worked hard and achieved his ambition to reassemble and fly his own Vampire aircraft . Kerr's family paid tribute to both victims after the crash, saying: "When we as a family visited the crash site we thought it very apt as a Spitfire, Hurricane and a Lancaster flew overhead. I am sure that it was the most fitting tribute to both Jonathan and Sir Kenneth" .

### Investigation findings

The Air Accidents Investigation Branch (AAIB) conducted a comprehensive investigation into the Vampire crash . The investigation identified wake turbulence from the larger Sea Vixen aircraft as the primary cause of the accident [4].

The AAIB investigation determined that the Vampire had been affected by trailing vortices generated by the Sea Vixen . The study showed that the Sea Vixen, with a mass of 14,784 kg, was generating vortices three to four times stronger than those of the Vampire, which had a mass of 4,400 kg .

The investigation revealed that the Vampire was approximately 550 feet behind the Sea Vixen when it encountered the wake turbulence . The aerodynamic study showed that the vortices could generate roll accelerations exceeding 70°/sec² in the Vampire, potentially greater than the roll authority available to the Vampire at that speed .

The investigation also noted that both aircraft were banked in a turn at the time, which increased the strength of the trailing vortices . The rapid roll to the right of 90° by the Vampire, as seen in the accident, was determined to be a likely result of such a vortex wake interaction .

The AAIB investigation found no evidence of any pre-impact failure within the aircraft structure or the flying controls that could have caused the accident . The investigation also ruled out incapacitation of the pilot or safety pilot, as there was evidence of attempted controlled recovery from the final descent .

## The Kingcobra crash (3 June 2001)


The second fatal crash occurred on Sunday 3 June 2001 at approximately 14:44 local time . A Bell P-63A Kingcobra, registered G-BTWR, was performing a solo display when it suddenly spiralled out of control and crashed into trees near the airfield [3].

The pilot, Guy Bancroft-Wilson, a 43-year-old British Airways captain based at Gatwick Airport, was killed instantly when the 60-year-old American King Cobra fighter spiralled out of control and exploded into trees [3]. Bancroft-Wilson was a former member of the Red Arrows display team who had served from 1987 to 1989 [8].

Witnesses reported that the Kingcobra was performing a vertical manoeuvre when it appeared to stall at the top and entered a spin . The aircraft spiralled out of control and crashed into trees about 50 yards from the control tower, 20 yards from parked general aviation aircraft on the apron, and 20 yards from the main road .

The crash occurred during a display sequence where the Kingcobra was flying with two other American World War II fighter aircraft—a P-47 Thunderbolt and an A-4 Skyraider . The formation had split up and the Kingcobra had peeled off into a tight loop but appeared to stall pulling out of it . The aircraft then pitched nose down violently and, although the pilot attempted to recover, he ran out of altitude and crashed .

### Victim

Guy Bancroft-Wilson was a highly experienced pilot with 7,730 hours of flying experience, including 13 hours on the Kingcobra type . He had served as a member of the Red Arrows display team from 1987 to 1989 [8]. After leaving the RAF, he continued to fly professionally as an airline pilot with British Airways while also performing occasional displays in historic aircraft .

Bancroft-Wilson was married with three sons aged between six and 11 [9]. He was described as a passionate pilot who had enjoyed flying the Kingcobra aircraft . His former Red Arrows colleagues expressed their condolences following the crash, noting his skill and dedication as a pilot [8].


The AAIB conducted a thorough investigation into the Kingcobra crash, which was assigned report number EW/C2001/6/4 . The investigation determined that the pilot had entered an incipient spin at the top of a vertical manoeuvre and was unable to recover .

The investigation revealed that the Kingcobra had performed a successful display on the first day of the airshow . However, on the second day, during a vertical manoeuvre, the pilot appeared to lose control and the aircraft departed controlled flight before impacting the ground in a steep nose-down attitude .

The investigation examined the pilot's flight history and found that he had flown the Kingcobra in displays on at least 10 occasions since first flying the type in April 1998 . He held a current Display Authorisation (DA) for a Category C aircraft, which was valid until September 2001 .

The AAIB investigation determined that the pilot had entered a spin at the top of a loop manoeuvre . The investigation revealed that at the top of the manoeuvre, in the inverted position, full nose-up elevator was maintained and a substantial amount of right rudder was applied . The aircraft yawed to the right and then departed into an upright incipient spin .

The investigation found that the pilot had attempted to recover from the spin but was unable to do so in the available altitude . The aircraft impacted the ground in a nose-down attitude at approximately 160 knots . There was an immediate fire that was quickly extinguished by the attending fire crew, but the pilot had suffered fatal injuries in the impact .

The investigation also examined the aircraft's performance and found that the pilot had entered the final manoeuvre with insufficient speed for it to be completed successfully . The lack of speed was most likely evidence of a continuing loss of energy during the display, partially as a consequence of an earlier failed manoeuvre .

## Safety recommendations and changes

The AAIB investigations into both crashes resulted in several important safety recommendations . These recommendations aimed to prevent similar accidents from occurring in the future and led to changes in airshow regulations and procedures.

### Communication system improvements

One of the key recommendations from the Kingcobra crash investigation was the improvement of communication systems at airshows . The investigation revealed that during the Kingcobra's display, a Flying Display Committee member had become concerned about the pilot's loss of control during an earlier manoeuvre and had attempted to contact Air Traffic Control five times by radio and the Display Director twice by telephone, but was unable to get an answer .

This communication failure highlighted a critical safety issue . As a result of this finding, the event organisers installed a direct telephone line between the Flying Display Committee on the crowd side of the runway and the Display Director in the ATC tower .

The AAIB also recommended that airshow organisers should ensure that adequate communication systems are in place to allow for immediate 中止 of displays when safety concerns arise . This recommendation was incorporated into updated airshow regulations and guidance documents [5].

### Formation flying safety

The Vampire crash investigation highlighted the risks associated with formation flying between aircraft of significantly different sizes and weights [4]. The investigation determined that the large disparity in operating weights between the Sea Vixen (14,784 kg) and the Vampire (4,400 kg) had contributed to the accident .

The AAIB recommended that formation displays should carefully consider the compatibility of aircraft participating in close formation flying . The investigation noted that trailing vortices from larger aircraft can have significant effects on smaller aircraft, particularly when both aircraft are manoeuvring .
