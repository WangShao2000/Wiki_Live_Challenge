# Modes of underwater diving

Underwater diving modes are the various methods and techniques used for descending below the water's surface, each requiring specific equipment, procedures, and skills . These modes are distinguished primarily by their breathing gas supply systems, diving equipment, and whether the diver is exposed to ambient pressure . Understanding the different modes is essential for both recreational and professional divers, as each offers unique advantages and limitations for specific applications.

## 1. Introduction to underwater diving modes

### 1.1 Definition and scope

A mode of underwater diving, also referred to as an underwater diving mode, is a specific type or method of underwater diving that requires particular equipment, procedures, and techniques . These modes are categorized based on several factors including the breathing gas supply system, the type of equipment used, and whether the diver experiences the full pressure of the surrounding water .

The scope of underwater diving modes encompasses all methods by which humans can explore beneath the water's surface, from simple breath-hold diving to complex saturation systems that allow divers to work at extreme depths for extended periods . Each mode has evolved to serve specific purposes, whether for recreational exploration, scientific research, military operations, or commercial applications .

### 1.2 Classification criteria

Underwater diving modes can be classified according to several criteria. The primary classification is based on whether the diver is exposed to ambient pressure or operates in a pressure-isolated environment . Ambient pressure diving, also known as compressed-gas diving, includes all modes where the diver experiences the full hydrostatic pressure of the water at their depth . This category can be further divided by the breathing gas used (air, oxygen, or mixed gases) and by the circuit type (open, semi-closed, or closed) .

Another important classification criterion is the source of breathing gas. Some modes rely on gas carried by the diver, while others receive gas from the surface through hoses [1]. Additionally, modes can be classified by their purpose, such as recreational, commercial, military, or technical diving .

## 2. Ambient pressure diving modes

### 2.1 Freediving

Freediving, also known as breath-hold diving or skin diving, is the practice of diving underwater without breathing apparatus, relying solely on the diver's ability to hold their breath until resurfacing . This mode includes underwater swimming, snorkeling, and competitive apnea dives . Freediving equipment typically consists of fins, a diving mask for improved vision, and sometimes a snorkel for surface breathing .

Historically, freediving was the only method available for underwater work and exploration until the development of mechanical breathing devices . Ancient civilizations used this technique for gathering food, harvesting resources such as sponges and pearls, reclaiming sunken valuables, and supporting military campaigns . Modern freediving encompasses recreational activities, competitive sports, and professional applications including scientific research and underwater photography .

#### 2.1.1 Snorkeling

Snorkeling is a surface-based activity where the diver floats face-down on the water, using a snorkel (a short breathing tube) to breathe while keeping their face submerged to observe underwater environments [2]. This mode requires minimal equipment - a mask, snorkel tube, and fins - and allows for extended periods of surface swimming with continuous underwater visibility [2]. Snorkeling is popular among tourists and beginners due to its simplicity and low cost .

#### 2.1.2 Competitive freediving

Competitive freediving has evolved into a structured sport with multiple disciplines governed by organizations such as AIDA International and CMAS (Confédération Mondiale des Activités Subaquatiques) . These disciplines include:



* **Constant Weight (CWT)**: Diving to depth and returning using fins without assistance

* **Free Immersion (FIM)**: Using a line to pull oneself down and up without fins or weights

* **No Limits (NLT)**: Using any means of descent and ascent, typically involving weighted sleds

* **Variable Weight (VWT)**: Using a weighted sled for descent but swimming or pulling up

* **Static Apnea**: Holding breath underwater while remaining stationary at the surface

The world record for no limits freediving stands at 253.2 meters, achieved by Herbert Nitsch in 2012 .

### 2.2 Scuba diving

Scuba diving (Self-Contained Underwater Breathing Apparatus) is a mode where divers carry their own breathing gas supply, allowing them greater mobility and independence than surface-supplied systems . The equipment includes breathing apparatus, a diving suit, buoyancy control and weighting systems, fins, a mask, and various safety equipment .

Scuba diving can be divided into two main categories: open-circuit and closed-circuit (rebreather) systems . Open-circuit systems discharge exhaled gas directly into the water, while closed-circuit systems recycle the breathing gas, removing carbon dioxide and adding oxygen .

#### 2.2.1 Open-circuit scuba

Open-circuit scuba systems consist of one or more diving cylinders containing breathing gas at high pressure, connected to a regulator that delivers gas to the diver at ambient pressure . The most common configuration uses a "single-hose" two-stage demand regulator, with the first stage reducing cylinder pressure (up to 300 bars) to an intermediate pressure of about 8-10 bars above ambient pressure, and the second stage delivering gas at ambient pressure to the diver's mouth .

Open-circuit systems are straightforward and reliable but use gas relatively quickly, limiting dive duration. They are suitable for most recreational diving applications and are the most common type of scuba equipment .

#### 2.2.2 Rebreather systems

Closed-circuit and semi-closed circuit rebreather systems process exhaled breath for reuse by removing carbon dioxide and replacing consumed oxygen . These systems use much less gas volume than open-circuit systems and produce minimal bubbles, making them attractive for military operations, scientific research, and underwater photography .

Closed-circuit rebreathers (CCR) maintain a constant oxygen partial pressure in the breathing loop, while semi-closed circuit rebreathers (SCR) inject a continuous flow of fresh gas mixture . Rebreathers allow for longer dives and quieter operation but are more complex and require specialized training .

### 2.3 Surface-supplied diving

Surface-supplied diving provides breathing gas to the diver through a hose (umbilical) from the surface, either from shore or a diving support vessel [1]. This mode is particularly useful for long-duration underwater work where carrying sufficient gas supply would be impractical .

The diver's umbilical typically includes breathing gas supply lines, communication cables, pneumofathometer hoses for depth measurement, safety lines, and may include hot water hoses for heating, video cables, and breathing gas reclaim lines . Surface-supplied divers wear full-face masks or helmets and receive gas either on demand or as continuous free flow .

#### 2.3.1 Surface-oriented diving

Surface-oriented diving, also called "bounce diving," refers to operations where the diver starts and finishes at atmospheric pressure . This mode is commonly used for commercial applications such as hull cleaning, underwater construction, and salvage operations . Divers may be deployed from shore or a diving support vessel and may use diving stages or diving bells for transport .

A basic form of surface-supplied diving is "airline" or "hookah" diving, which uses only an air hose from a compressor on the surface. This system is used for tasks such as hull cleaning, archaeological surveys, and shellfish harvesting .

#### 2.3.2 Saturation diving

Saturation diving allows professional divers to live and work under pressure for days or weeks at a time . After working in the water, divers rest and live in a dry pressurized underwater habitat on the seafloor or in pressure chambers on a diving support vessel at the same pressure as their work depth .

This technique takes advantage of the physiological principle that after approximately 24-48 hours at depth, the body tissues become saturated with inert gases. By maintaining pressure between dives, divers avoid repeated decompression and recompression, significantly reducing decompression time and risk . Divers are transferred between work site and living quarters in a pressurized closed diving bell .

Saturation diving enables deep and long-duration work that would be impossible with other modes. Commercial saturation divers using heliox breathing gases routinely work at depths exceeding 100 meters, with some experimental dives reaching 534 meters .

## 3. Atmospheric pressure diving

Atmospheric pressure diving uses rigid suits or submersibles that maintain internal pressure at one atmosphere while protecting the diver from external water pressure . This mode eliminates the physiological risks associated with ambient pressure diving, including decompression sickness, nitrogen narcosis, and oxygen toxicity [3].

### 3.1 Atmospheric diving suits (ADS)

An atmospheric diving suit (ADS), also known as an atmospheric pressure diving suit or single atmosphere diving suit, is a small one-person articulated submersible that resembles a suit of armor . The suit has pressure-tight joints that allow articulation while maintaining a constant internal volume and pressure of one atmosphere .

ADS units enable diving to depths of approximately 700 meters for many hours . They are used primarily for deep-sea salvage, underwater construction, and scientific research. The suits are equipped with manipulator arms that allow the wearer to perform tasks underwater while remaining in a safe, comfortable environment .

The development of ADS technology began in the early 20th century, with the first practical suit, the Tritonia, developed by Joseph Salim Peress in 1932 . Modern ADS units such as the ADS 2000, developed jointly by OceanWorks International and the US Navy in 1997, provide increased depth capability for submarine rescue operations .

### 3.2 Submersibles

Submersibles are underwater vehicles that can carry one or more people to extreme depths while maintaining atmospheric pressure inside . These vehicles are used for deep-sea exploration, scientific research, and underwater filming.

Human-occupied vehicles (HOVs) are manned submersibles designed to carry researchers to great depths. The most famous example is Alvin, operated by Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution, which has been in operation since 1964 and can reach depths of 4,500 meters, carrying two scientists and one pilot .

## 4. Unmanned underwater systems

Unmanned underwater vehicles (UUVs), also known as underwater drones or unmanned submarines, are submersible vehicles that can operate underwater without a human occupant . These systems have become increasingly important in underwater exploration, military operations, and scientific research.

### 4.1 Remotely operated vehicles (ROVs)

Remotely operated vehicles (ROVs) are tethered to a surface vessel, allowing control signals to be sent and received directly between topside operators and the underwater vehicle [4]. ROVs are controlled by an operator who dictates the vehicle's movements and functions remotely [5].

ROVs are equipped with various sensors, cameras, and manipulator arms that allow them to perform complex tasks underwater. They are often used for deep-sea exploration, pipeline inspection, underwater construction, and scientific sampling . The tether provides power and communication but limits the vehicle's range to the length of the cable.

### 4.2 Autonomous underwater vehicles (AUVs)

Autonomous underwater vehicles (AUVs) are independent underwater robots that operate without a physical connection to their operator [6]. Unlike ROVs, which are tethered to a service vessel, AUVs have no umbilical cable and operate entirely on their own [6].

AUVs are pre-programmed with a mission plan before deployment and follow a predetermined route using onboard navigation systems . They carry batteries for power and various sensors for data collection, including sonar systems for mapping the seafloor, cameras for visual inspection, and environmental sensors for measuring water quality parameters .

Since their development in the 1960s, AUVs have become key tools in ocean exploration . Modern AUVs can operate for days or weeks at a time, covering vast areas of the ocean floor. They are used for military surveillance, scientific research, and environmental monitoring .

## 5. Historical development of diving modes

The history of underwater diving modes spans thousands of years, beginning with simple breath-hold techniques and evolving through technological innovations to today's sophisticated systems . Understanding this development helps explain why different modes exist and how they continue to evolve.

### 5.1 Early developments

The earliest form of underwater diving was simple breath-hold diving, used by ancient civilizations for gathering food and resources . Ancient Greeks and Romans practiced sponge diving and marine salvage using weights (skandalopetra) of up to 15 kilograms to speed descent to depths of up to 30 meters .

The first mechanical diving aid was the diving bell, described by Aristotle in the 4th century BC [7]. The diving bell was essentially an inverted open-ended barrel that trapped air when submerged. In 1691, Edmond Halley developed an improved diving bell capable of remaining submerged for extended periods, with air replenished by weighted barrels sent from the surface [8].

### 5.2 Technological advances

The 18th and 19th centuries saw significant advances in diving technology. In 1771, Sieur Fréminet of Paris designed an autonomous breathing machine with a compressed air reservoir . In 1827, German-born British engineer Augustus Siebe built the first "smoke helmets," which were later converted into diving helmets .

The development of surface-supplied diving systems continued with the invention of the demand regulator in 1864 by French engineers Auguste Denayrouze and Benoît Rouquayrol . This allowed for more efficient use of breathing gas compared to earlier free-flow systems.

### 5.3 Modern scuba development

The 20th century brought revolutionary changes with the development of self-contained underwater breathing apparatus. In 1925, Yves Le Prieur in France devised the first open-circuit scuba system using compressed air cylinders . However, the most significant development came in 1943 when Jacques-Yves Cousteau and Émile Gagnan designed the first successful and safe open-circuit scuba, known as the Aqua-Lung [9].

The Aqua-Lung combined an improved demand regulator with high-pressure air tanks, giving divers unprecedented freedom underwater . This invention revolutionized underwater exploration and made recreational scuba diving possible.

### 5.4 Recent innovations

Recent decades have seen continued innovation in diving technology. The development of dive computers in the 1980s provided real-time decompression monitoring, making diving safer [10]. Advances in materials science have led to lighter, more efficient equipment.

The development of mixed-gas diving techniques, including trimix (oxygen, nitrogen, and helium), has extended the depth limits of recreational and technical diving . Rebreather technology has evolved from simple oxygen systems to sophisticated electronic systems that automatically adjust gas mixtures based on depth.

## 6. Safety considerations and decompression

Safety is paramount in all modes of underwater diving, with each mode presenting unique risks and requiring specific safety protocols [11]. Understanding these risks and implementing proper safety procedures is essential for all divers.

### 6.1 Decompression sickness

Decompression sickness (DCS), also known as "the bends," is a condition that can occur when dissolved gases (primarily nitrogen) come out of solution in the body tissues due to a reduction in ambient pressure [12]. This condition can cause joint pain, skin irritation, neurological symptoms, and in severe cases, paralysis or death [11].

Decompression sickness should be suspected if any associated symptoms occur following a drop in pressure, particularly within 24 hours of diving . Symptoms may include joint pain, numbness, dizziness, shortness of breath, and fatigue [11].

### 6.2 Decompression procedures

Proper decompression procedures are essential for preventing decompression sickness. These procedures involve ascending slowly from depth and making staged stops to allow dissolved gases to escape safely .

The decompression schedule depends on several factors including maximum depth, bottom time, and the breathing gas used. For example, a diver who ascends too quickly risks decompression sickness, which can cause extreme pain, paralysis, or worse . Decompression stops, which depend on time spent at depth, help avert the bends by allowing excessive nitrogen in tissues to escape .

Technical divers using mixed gases have developed sophisticated decompression procedures. During ascent, open-circuit technical divers switch from their main cylinders to stage bottles containing enriched air (nitrox) to aid in off-gassing nitrogen . Closed-circuit rebreather divers have their systems automatically adjust gas mixtures to optimize decompression .

### 6.3 Equipment safety

Equipment maintenance and safety checks are critical for all diving modes. Divers must regularly inspect and service their equipment to ensure proper functioning . This includes checking hoses for leaks, inspecting valves and regulators, and verifying cylinder pressure and integrity.

Technical divers carry redundant equipment as backup in case of malfunction. This may include extra masks, computers, lights, and regulators attached to alternate air sources . The principle of "no decompression without a backup" is fundamental to technical diving safety.

### 6.4 Environmental hazards

Underwater environments present various hazards including strong currents, poor visibility, low temperatures, and marine life . Divers must be trained to recognize and respond to these hazards appropriately.

Specific hazards vary by location and diving mode. For example, cave diving presents unique risks including entrapment and loss of orientation. Deep diving faces challenges such as nitrogen narcosis and oxygen toxicity. Cold water diving requires specialized thermal protection and increased awareness of hypothermia risk.

## 7. Applications and purposes of different diving modes

Different diving modes serve various purposes, from recreational exploration to professional applications in industry, military, and science . Understanding these applications helps divers choose the appropriate mode for their needs.

### 7.1 Recreational diving

Recreational diving encompasses various activities including underwater sightseeing, photography, and sport diving . Popular recreational modes include snorkeling for shallow water exploration and open-circuit scuba for deeper dives.

Recreational divers typically operate within depth limits specified by their certification level. The recreational diving depth limit set by EN 14153-2/ISO 24801-2 level 2 "Autonomous Diver" standard is 20 meters . More extensively trained recreational divers may dive to 30-40 meters, with some organizations allowing up to 60 meters for experienced teams .

### 7.2 Commercial and professional applications

Commercial diving is used for underwater construction, maintenance, and inspection of offshore structures, ships, and underwater pipelines . Surface-supplied diving is commonly used for these applications due to the long duration requirements and the need for heavy tools and equipment.

Commercial divers work in various industries including oil and gas, shipbuilding, underwater construction, and salvage operations. They may use surface-oriented diving for shorter tasks or saturation diving for extended deep-water projects.

### 7.3 Military and special operations

Military diving encompasses various applications including underwater demolition, mine clearance, search and rescue, and intelligence gathering . Military divers, often called frogmen or combat divers, require specialized training and equipment.

Closed-circuit rebreathers are particularly valuable for military operations due to their silent operation and minimal bubble production, which reduces detection risk . Military divers may operate in hostile environments and must be prepared for various threats including underwater mines and enemy divers.

### 7.4 Scientific research and exploration

Scientific diving is used for marine biology research, underwater archaeology, geological surveys, and environmental monitoring . Scientists use various diving modes depending on their research objectives and depth requirements.

Technical diving has expanded the range of scientific research by allowing access to deeper environments previously unreachable by recreational divers . Rebreathers are particularly useful for scientific work as they produce minimal disturbance to marine life and allow for longer observation periods .

Underwater vehicles, including ROVs and AUVs, have become increasingly important for scientific research, particularly in deep-sea environments where manned diving is impractical or impossible .

## 8. Equipment requirements for different modes

Each diving mode requires specific equipment designed to meet its unique demands . Understanding equipment requirements is essential for safe and effective diving.

### 8.1 Basic diving equipment

All diving modes require certain basic equipment including a mask for underwater vision, fins for propulsion, and some form of exposure protection . Beyond these basics, equipment requirements vary significantly by mode.

Snorkeling requires only a mask, snorkel, and fins [2]. Freediving typically adds a weight belt for neutral buoyancy control and may include a wetsuit for thermal protection .

### 8.2 Scuba equipment

Scuba diving requires more extensive equipment including:



* **Breathing apparatus**: Cylinders, regulator, and buoyancy compensator device (BCD)

* **Diving suit**: Wetsuit or dry suit for thermal protection

* **Weighting system**: Weights to achieve neutral buoyancy

* **Safety equipment**: Dive computer, depth gauge, compass, and emergency signaling devices

The buoyancy compensator device (BCD) helps divers control their buoyancy underwater, allowing them to maintain a constant depth and ascend or descend easily [13]. Modern BCDs incorporate integrated weight systems and multiple air chambers for redundancy.

### 8.3 Surface-supplied equipment

Surface-supplied diving requires specialized umbilical systems that deliver breathing gas, communications, and often hot water for thermal protection . Divers wear full-face masks or helmets with integrated communications systems.

Commercial surface-supplied divers typically use demand helmets that provide gas only when the diver inhales, conserving gas compared to free-flow systems. For deep diving, helium-based gas mixtures may be used to reduce nitrogen narcosis and oxygen toxicity risks.

### 8.4 Technical diving equipment

Technical diving requires specialized equipment for extended range and increased safety . This may include:



* **Multiple cylinders**: For different gas mixtures (travel, bottom, and decompression gases)

* **Dual regulators**: For redundancy in case of failure

* **Dive computers**: Capable of modeling multiple gas mixtures and complex decompression profiles

* **Stage cylinders**: Additional cylinders carried at strategic points for decompression

Technical divers using rebreathers require even more complex equipment including oxygen sensors, carbon dioxide scrubbers, and electronic control systems. These systems require extensive training and regular maintenance to operate safely.

## 9. Training and certification requirements

Proper training is essential for safe diving regardless of the mode . Different modes and applications require varying levels of training and certification.

### 9.1 Recreational diving certification

Recreational diving certification typically follows a standardized progression from beginner to advanced levels. Entry-level certification, such as the PADI Open Water Diver course, teaches basic diving skills including equipment use, underwater navigation, and safety procedures .

Advanced certification may include specialties such as deep diving, wreck diving, and night diving. Technical diving requires additional training in decompression theory, gas management, and emergency procedures .

### 9.2 Professional diving certification

Professional diving requires more extensive training and certification. Commercial divers must meet specific standards and may require certification from organizations such as the Association of Diving Contractors International (ADCI) or national regulatory bodies.

Saturation diving requires specialized training in life support systems, decompression procedures, and working in confined spaces. Military divers undergo rigorous training in combat diving techniques, underwater navigation, and special operations.

### 9.3 Freediving certification

Freediving certification is not required by law, but it is strongly recommended due to the inherent risks . Freediving courses typically include three levels: theory studies, confined water sessions (usually in a swimming pool), and open water sessions in the ocean or reservoir .

Competitive freediving requires additional training in specific disciplines and safety procedures. Safety divers are an essential part of competitive freediving, providing rescue support in case of blackout or other emergencies .

### 9.4 Underwater vehicle operation

Operating underwater vehicles requires specialized training in vehicle control, navigation, and mission planning. ROV pilots must develop skills in remote manipulation and system troubleshooting. AUV operators need expertise in mission programming and data analysis.

Training programs for underwater vehicle operation often include both classroom instruction and hands-on practical training. Operators must understand the limitations and capabilities of their equipment and be prepared to handle system failures.

## 10. Environmental impact considerations

Underwater diving can have various impacts on marine environments, and responsible diving practices are essential for conservation . Different diving modes have different environmental footprints and impact potential.

### 10.1 Direct environmental impacts

Divers can cause direct impacts through physical contact with marine life and habitats. Coral reefs are particularly vulnerable to damage from careless diving practices including touching, kicking, or anchoring on reef structures. These impacts can take years to recover and may result in permanent damage.

Surface-supplied diving operations, particularly in commercial applications, may cause sediment disturbance and habitat modification. The use of heavy equipment and the deployment of umbilical systems can create physical changes to the seafloor environment.

### 10.2 Indirect environmental impacts

The production and transportation of diving equipment contribute to environmental impacts through resource extraction, manufacturing processes, and energy consumption. Different diving modes have varying equipment requirements, with technical and commercial diving typically requiring more complex and resource-intensive equipment.

The use of compressed gases, particularly helium for deep diving, has environmental implications due to helium's scarcity and the energy required for extraction and purification. Responsible diving organizations encourage efficient gas use and recycling where possible.

### 10.3 Mitigation strategies

Responsible diving organizations promote best practices to minimize environmental impact. These include:



* Maintaining proper buoyancy control to avoid contact with the seafloor

* Using mooring buoys instead of anchors in sensitive areas

* Following designated dive routes to minimize disturbance

* Avoiding touching or chasing marine life

* Properly disposing of waste and maintaining equipment to prevent pollution

The development of quieter diving systems, such as rebreathers, reduces disturbance to marine life compared to open-circuit scuba . This is particularly important for scientific research and underwater photography where minimal disturbance is essential.

### 10.4 Conservation diving

Many divers participate in conservation activities including marine debris removal, coral reef monitoring, and marine life surveys. These activities help raise awareness of environmental issues and contribute to scientific research.

Underwater vehicles are increasingly used for environmental monitoring and research, allowing for large-scale surveys with minimal disturbance. AUVs equipped with sensors can collect data on water quality, temperature, and salinity over vast areas, providing valuable information for environmental management.

## 11. Future developments in diving modes

The field of underwater diving continues to evolve through technological innovation, scientific research, and changing applications . Several trends are shaping the future of diving modes.

### 11.1 Technological innovations

Advances in materials science are leading to lighter, stronger, and more efficient diving equipment. Carbon fiber and other composite materials are being used to create lighter cylinders and more streamlined equipment. Smart materials that can change properties in response to temperature or pressure offer new possibilities for buoyancy control and thermal protection.

Electronic systems continue to advance with improved sensors, processors, and displays. Future dive computers may incorporate artificial intelligence for real-time risk assessment and adaptive decompression modeling. Wireless communication systems will enable better diver-to-diver and diver-to-surface communication.

### 11.2 Mixed reality and augmented reality

The integration of mixed reality (MR) and augmented reality (AR) technologies with diving equipment opens new possibilities for training, navigation, and data visualization. Divers may soon have heads-up displays showing depth, decompression status, and navigation information directly in their field of view.

These technologies could enhance safety by providing real-time alerts for decompression issues, gas supply, and environmental hazards. They may also improve the diving experience by overlaying information about marine life and historical sites.

### 11.3 Sustainable diving practices

Environmental awareness is driving the development of more sustainable diving practices and equipment. Bio-based materials, recycled components, and energy-efficient systems are becoming more common. The diving industry is working to reduce its carbon footprint through more efficient operations and sustainable sourcing.

Alternative gas systems, including the use of hydrogen for extreme depth diving, are being researched. Closed-loop life support systems for saturation diving may reduce waste and environmental impact.

### 11.4 Underwater vehicle integration

The integration of manned and unmanned systems offers new possibilities for underwater operations. Divers may work in coordination with underwater vehicles for complex tasks requiring both human judgment and robotic precision.

Autonomous systems are becoming more capable, with AUVs incorporating artificial intelligence for adaptive mission planning and obstacle avoidance. These systems will continue to expand the range of underwater exploration and research.

### 11.5 Extreme environment diving

As our understanding of deep-sea environments grows, new diving technologies are being developed to explore previously inaccessible areas. These include ultra-deep ADS units for exploring the deepest ocean trenches and specialized systems for diving in extreme temperatures or high-pressure environments.

Research into human physiological adaptations to diving, including studies of indigenous peoples like the Bajau who have evolved larger spleens for improved diving performance , may lead to new training methods and equipment designs.

## 12. Conclusion

Underwater diving modes represent a diverse range of technologies and techniques that enable humans to explore and work beneath the ocean's surface. From simple breath-hold diving practiced by ancient civilizations to sophisticated saturation systems and autonomous underwater vehicles, each mode offers unique capabilities for specific applications.

The evolution of diving modes reflects humanity's ongoing fascination with the underwater world and our technological ingenuity in overcoming the challenges of operating in this hostile environment. Each mode has its place, whether for recreational exploration, scientific research, commercial operations, or military applications.

Understanding the characteristics, capabilities, and limitations of different diving modes is essential for anyone involved in underwater activities. Proper training, appropriate equipment, and adherence to safety procedures are fundamental to safe and successful diving regardless of the mode chosen.

As technology continues to advance and environmental awareness grows, we can expect further innovations in diving modes that will expand our capabilities while minimizing environmental impact. The future of underwater diving promises exciting developments that will continue to push the boundaries of human exploration and knowledge of our planet's underwater realms.
