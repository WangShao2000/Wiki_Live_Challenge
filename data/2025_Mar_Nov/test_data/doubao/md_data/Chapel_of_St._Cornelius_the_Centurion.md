# Chapel of St. Cornelius the Centurion

## Lead Section

The Chapel of St. Cornelius the Centurion is a historic Carpenter Gothic-style Episcopal chapel located at Fort Monroe National Monument in Hampton, Virginia. Constructed between 1856 and 1858, it is the oldest continually used wooden military structure for religious services in the United States . The chapel is named after Cornelius the Centurion, a Roman military officer who is documented in the Book of Acts as the first Gentile to convert to Christianity .

Designed by prominent Gothic Revival architect Richard Upjohn based on his standardized plans from the 1852 publication "Rural Architecture," the chapel features significant historical and architectural importance [1]. Its notable stained-glass windows include works from Tiffany Glass Company, J. & R. Lamb Studios, R. Geissler, and the John Bolton School, representing almost a century of American glass-making tradition [2].

The chapel was commissioned by First Lieutenant Julian McAllister, who survived an explosion at the fort's arsenal in 1855 and attributed his survival to divine intervention . It was consecrated on May 3, 1858, by Bishop John Johns of St. John's Church in Hampton [3]. After more than 150 years of continuous military use, the chapel held its final official U.S. Army religious service on August 21, 2011, and reopened as a civilian inter-denominational church on September 4, 2011 .

## History and Construction

### Military Origins and Commissioning

The Chapel of the Centurion has its origins in a dramatic event at Fort Monroe in 1855. First Lieutenant Julian McAllister was the sole survivor of an explosion at the fort's arsenal that killed two men . Convinced that divine intervention had saved his life, McAllister became the main donor of funds for constructing the chapel as an expression of gratitude . His personal experience with what he perceived as divine protection directly led to the chapel's creation, establishing a spiritual foundation for the building that would serve military personnel and their families for over a century and a half.

Construction of the chapel began in 1856 under McAllister's commission . The project represented a significant investment in the spiritual welfare of the military community at Fort Monroe, which was one of the most strategically important military installations on the East Coast. The decision to build a dedicated chapel within the fort's walls reflected the U.S. Army's recognition of religious services as an essential component of military life and morale.

### Architectural Design and Construction

The chapel's design came from standardized plans published in "Rural Architecture," a 1852 book by renowned Gothic Revival architect Richard Upjohn [1]. Upjohn, who was known for his work on Trinity Church in New York City and numerous other ecclesiastical buildings, provided a modified version of his chapel design specifically for Fort Monroe [4]. The building reflects the Carpenter Gothic style, characterized by its steep gabled roof, decorative bargeboard trim, and pointed-arch windows [2].

The chapel is a single-story, rectangular structure constructed of wood with board-and-batten siding [2]. When completed in 1857, the exterior was painted dark green with red trim, a color scheme that remained unchanged until 1948 when it was repainted white . The building stands on the edge of the parade ground facing northeast, positioned as the only religious structure within the stone walls of Fort Monroe [5].

Construction was completed in 1857, and the chapel was consecrated on May 3, 1858, by Bishop John Johns of St. John's Church in Hampton, Virginia [3]. The consecration ceremony marked the beginning of the chapel's long service to military personnel and their families, establishing a tradition of religious observance that would continue uninterrupted for more than a century.

### Early Use and Military Significance

From its consecration, the Chapel of the Centurion served as a vital center for religious life at Fort Monroe. The chapel was used for regular Episcopal services, but it also accommodated other denominations, reflecting the diverse religious needs of the military community [6]. This inter-denominational use became a hallmark of the chapel's ministry, serving as a place of worship for soldiers and their families regardless of their specific religious affiliations.

During the American Civil War (1861-1865), Fort Monroe served as a Union stronghold and became known as "Freedom's Fortress" after General Benjamin Butler declared escaped enslaved persons who reached the fort to be contraband of war [7]. The chapel continued to function throughout this period, providing spiritual comfort and community to soldiers on both sides of the conflict who were stationed or held at the fort.

## Architectural Features

### Carpenter Gothic Design Elements

The Chapel of the Centurion exemplifies the Carpenter Gothic style, a variant of the Gothic Revival movement that was particularly popular for religious buildings in rural and military settings during the mid-19th century . The style is characterized by its use of wood construction techniques to achieve Gothic architectural effects, including steeply pitched gabled roofs, pointed-arched windows, and decorative wooden trim.

The chapel's most distinctive architectural feature is its steeply pitched roof, which rises dramatically above the single-story structure. The roofline is accentuated by decorative bargeboard trim along the gables, featuring intricate scrollwork patterns that are typical of Carpenter Gothic design [2]. The pointed-arched windows, another hallmark of Gothic architecture, are arranged symmetrically along the side walls and in the front and rear elevations.

The building's rectangular floor plan is typical of Upjohn's standardized chapel designs, providing an efficient use of space for worship services [4]. The interior features exposed wooden ceiling beams that create a sense of verticality and height, enhancing the spiritual atmosphere of the worship space. The simplicity of the design, combined with its Gothic-inspired decorative elements, creates a building that is both modest and elegant, suitable for its military setting.

### Interior Design and Layout

The chapel's interior is designed to accommodate religious services with seating arranged in traditional pews facing the chancel area. The chancel features a raised platform with an altar at the eastern end of the building, following traditional Christian liturgical orientation . Above the altar is a window depicting Cornelius the Centurion, serving as a visual reminder of the chapel's namesake and his significance in Christian history .

The interior walls are finished with plain wooden paneling, painted in light colors that help to brighten the space. The exposed wooden roof trusses create a sense of architectural drama, with their triangular forms echoing the shape of the exterior roof. The simplicity of the interior design reflects the functional needs of a military chapel while maintaining an appropriate level of dignity and reverence.

The chapel's dimensions and layout were designed to accommodate the military community at Fort Monroe. The building provides seating for approximately 200 people, making it suitable for company-sized gatherings while maintaining an intimate atmosphere for smaller services [6]. The design includes a small vestry area for clergy and a separate entrance for the officiating minister, allowing for the proper conduct of religious ceremonies.

### Stained-Glass Windows

The chapel's stained-glass windows represent one of its most significant artistic and historical features. The collection includes works from several premier American glass-making studios, representing almost a century of glass-making practice and design [2]. These windows not only enhance the aesthetic beauty of the chapel but also serve as visual narratives of military history and Christian faith.

Three of the chapel's windows are attributed to Louis Comfort Tiffany and his Tiffany Glass and Decorating Company in New York City [1]. Tiffany, who later became world-renowned for his Art Nouveau glass designs, was just beginning his career in the 1870s when these windows were created. The Tiffany windows in the Chapel of the Centurion represent early examples of his work and feature his characteristic use of opalescent glass and intricate design patterns.

The other notable stained-glass windows in the chapel come from J. & R. Lamb Studios, R. Geissler, and the John Bolton School [2]. Each studio contributed windows with distinct stylistic characteristics, creating a diverse collection that spans from the 1870s to the early 20th century. The windows depict various religious and military themes, including scenes from the life of Christ, military heroes, and symbols of sacrifice and service [2].

One particularly significant window illustrates the story of Cornelius the Centurion from the Book of Acts. This window, located above the altar, depicts Cornelius receiving his vision from an angel and summoning Peter to come and baptize him and his household . This window serves both as a theological statement about the universality of the Christian message and a historical connection to the chapel's namesake.

## Notable Features and Artifacts

### Memorial Elements

The Chapel of the Centurion contains numerous memorial elements that honor the military service and sacrifice of those connected to Fort Monroe. Many of these memorials are incorporated into the stained-glass windows, which depict military events, achievements, and sacrifices of military personnel and their families [2]. These windows serve as both works of art and historical records, commemorating the service of specific individuals and units.

The chapel also contains memorial plaques and inscriptions dedicated to various military figures and events. These include memorials to soldiers who died in service, units that were stationed at Fort Monroe, and significant military occasions . The memorial elements create a sense of continuity between the chapel's present use and its long history of serving military communities.

One notable memorial feature is the Carillon chimes dedication plaque, which commemorates a gift to the chapel . The carillon, with its melodious bells, adds another dimension to the chapel's spiritual atmosphere and serves as a reminder of the many ways in which the chapel has been supported and enhanced over the years.

### Tiffany Glass Windows

The three Tiffany glass windows represent some of the most valuable and historically significant artifacts in the chapel [1]. These windows were created during Tiffany's early career, before he became famous for his Art Nouveau designs. They feature his distinctive use of opalescent glass, which creates a luminous effect when illuminated by sunlight [3].

The Tiffany windows in the Chapel of the Centurion are particularly significant because they represent an early phase of American stained-glass production. Unlike the more traditional European approaches to stained glass, Tiffany's technique involved the use of layers of colored glass and innovative methods of glass-making that produced unique effects [8]. These windows demonstrate his experimentation with new techniques and materials that would later become his trademark.

The subjects of the Tiffany windows include religious scenes and symbols that are appropriate to the chapel's function. While the specific designs of these windows are not detailed in the available sources, they are described as memorial windows that honor military service and sacrifice [3]. The combination of their artistic merit and historical significance makes them important cultural artifacts.

### Architectural Artifacts

Beyond the stained-glass windows, the chapel contains other architectural elements that are historically significant. The building's original wooden pews, altar, and pulpit have been preserved and continue to be used in services . These elements provide a tangible connection to the chapel's early history and maintain the authentic character of the interior space.

The chapel's original doors and hardware are also preserved, featuring decorative elements that are consistent with the Carpenter Gothic style [4]. The door hinges and handles, though functional, are crafted with attention to detail that reflects the overall quality of the building's construction. The building's original lighting fixtures, though updated with modern electrical components, maintain their historic design . These fixtures, along with other architectural details, create a cohesive visual environment that transports visitors back to the mid-19th century while remaining functional for contemporary use.

## Religious and Historical Significance

### Connection to St. Cornelius the Centurion

The Chapel of St. Cornelius the Centurion is named after a significant figure in early Christian history. Cornelius was a Roman centurion stationed in Caesarea, Palestine, who is documented in the New Testament Book of Acts as the first Gentile to convert to Christianity . His conversion is described in Acts 10 and 11 as a pivotal moment in the early Church's history, marking the expansion of Christianity beyond Jewish communities .

According to biblical accounts, Cornelius was a "devout and God-fearing" man who gave generously to the poor and prayed regularly . He received a vision from an angel who instructed him to send for Simon Peter, who was staying in Joppa. Meanwhile, Peter received a vision that prepared him to accept the invitation, breaking down traditional Jewish barriers regarding Gentile interaction . When Peter arrived, he preached the gospel to Cornelius and his household, and they were all baptized, receiving the Holy Spirit .

The significance of Cornelius's conversion lies in its theological implications for the early Christian Church. His baptism demonstrated that the Christian message was not limited to Jewish people but was intended for all nations . This event is described as "the beginning and the first fruits of the calling of the nations" , establishing a precedent for the universal mission of the Church.

The choice of Cornelius as the chapel's namesake reflects the military context of Fort Monroe and the desire to honor both military service and religious faith . As a centurion, Cornelius represents the military profession in a positive light, combining faithful service to the state with 虔诚 religious devotion. His story provides a model for military personnel seeking to integrate their professional duties with their spiritual lives.

### Military Chaplaincy History

The Chapel of the Centurion has served as a center for military chaplaincy for over 160 years, providing religious services and spiritual support to generations of military personnel [6]. The chapel's history reflects the evolution of military chaplaincy in the United States, from its early days serving a predominantly Protestant military to its current role in supporting religious diversity.

Throughout its history, the chapel has hosted services for various denominations, including Episcopal, Protestant, Catholic, and inter-denominational services . This multi-denominational use has been essential to its function as a military chapel, where the religious needs of all service members must be accommodated. The chapel has served as a place of worship, reflection, and community for soldiers, sailors, airmen, and Marines from diverse religious backgrounds.

The chapel's role in military life extends beyond formal religious services. It has been used for weddings, funerals, baptisms, and other life events that are significant to military families . These occasions often bring together the military community in times of celebration and mourning, reinforcing the chapel's importance as a social and spiritual center.

During times of war and conflict, the chapel has provided comfort and support to military personnel facing the stresses and uncertainties of military service . The chapel's consistent presence throughout various military campaigns and global conflicts has made it a symbol of continuity and stability for those serving at Fort Monroe.

### Civilian Use Transition

In 2011, the Chapel of the Centurion underwent a significant transition when Fort Monroe ceased to be an active military installation. The U.S. Army held a Chapel Decommissioning Service on May 22, 2011, followed by the final official U.S. Army religious service on August 21, 2011 . This marked the end of over 150 years of continuous military use.

The chapel's transition to civilian use began on September 4, 2011, when it reopened as a civilian inter-denominational church . This transition was facilitated by the Fort Monroe Authority, which manages the former military installation as a national monument and historic site. The decision to maintain the chapel as a place of worship preserved its historic function while making it accessible to the broader community.

Today, the Chapel of the Centurion continues to serve as a place of worship and community gathering for the citizens of Hampton and visitors to Fort Monroe National Monument [2]. The chapel hosts regular Sunday worship services at 10:30 AM and is available for special events and ceremonies . This continuity of use demonstrates the chapel's enduring significance as a spiritual and cultural landmark.

The transition to civilian use has also opened new opportunities for the chapel to serve educational and cultural purposes. The building is now part of the Fort Monroe National Monument and is included in guided tours of the historic site [9]. Visitors can learn about the chapel's history, architecture, and cultural significance while experiencing its peaceful interior environment.

## Preservation and Current Status

### Historic Preservation Efforts

The Chapel of the Centurion has been recognized for its historic significance through multiple preservation designations. The building was individually listed on the National Register of Historic Places on March 28, 2011, with the reference number 10000582 . This designation recognizes the chapel's architectural and historical importance and provides legal protections for its preservation.

The chapel is also included as a contributing structure within the Fort Monroe National Historic Landmark District, which was designated on October 15, 1966 [7]. The National Historic Landmark designation applies to the entire Fort Monroe complex, including the chapel, and recognizes its national significance in American history [7].

Preservation efforts for the chapel have focused on maintaining its historic integrity while ensuring its continued functionality. The Fort Monroe Authority, which manages the property, has implemented a comprehensive preservation plan that addresses both the building's structural needs and its historical features . This includes regular maintenance of the wooden structure, preservation of the stained-glass windows, and protection of the historic interior finishes.

The chapel's stained-glass windows have received particular attention from preservation specialists. The Tiffany windows and other historic glassworks require specialized care to prevent deterioration from environmental factors and normal wear [3]. Conservation efforts have included the installation of protective glazing, environmental monitoring systems, and regular inspections by stained-glass experts.

### Current Usage and Accessibility

The Chapel of the Centurion continues to function as an active place of worship under civilian management [2]. The chapel is now operated by the Centurion Inter-Church organization, which provides inter-denominational services that welcome people from all faith backgrounds . This inclusive approach continues the chapel's tradition of serving diverse religious communities.

The chapel is open to the public for worship services and special events. Sunday worship services are held at 10:30 AM, and the chapel is available for weddings, funerals, and other ceremonies . The building's accessibility has been improved to meet modern standards, including the installation of ramps and other features to accommodate visitors with disabilities.

As part of Fort Monroe National Monument, the chapel is also accessible for educational and cultural purposes. The building is included in guided tours of the historic fort and is available for group visits [9]. These educational opportunities allow visitors to learn about the chapel's role in military history, its architectural significance, and its cultural importance.

The chapel's location within Fort Monroe National Monument provides additional opportunities for interpretation and education. Visitors can explore the fort's extensive history, including its role in the Civil War as "Freedom's Fortress," its importance as a military installation, and its transformation into a national monument [7]. The chapel serves as a tangible connection between the fort's military past and its present role as a historic site and community resource.

### Future Plans and Maintenance

The Fort Monroe Authority has developed long-term plans for the preservation and maintenance of the Chapel of the Centurion. These plans address the ongoing needs of the historic building while ensuring its sustainability for future generations . The preservation strategy includes regular structural inspections, preventive maintenance, and the development of emergency response protocols.

Funding for preservation efforts comes from multiple sources, including federal historic preservation grants, private donations, and revenue from tourism . The Fort Monroe Authority works closely with preservation organizations, historic preservation consultants, and government agencies to ensure that preservation efforts meet professional standards and follow best practices.

Future plans for the chapel include continued improvements to accessibility, the development of educational programs, and the enhancement of interpretive materials [9]. The goal is to make the chapel more accessible and engaging for visitors while maintaining its historic character and spiritual atmosphere. These efforts will help to ensure that the Chapel of the Centurion continues to serve as a valuable cultural and spiritual resource for the community and for visitors to Fort Monroe National Monument.
