# Grupo Frontera Political Controversy

## 1. Background and Formation

### 1.1 Grupo Frontera Overview

Grupo Frontera is an American regional Mexican music band formed in 2022 in Edinburg, Texas [1]. The band consists of six members: Adelaido "Payo" Solís III (lead vocals and bajo quinto), Juan Javier Cantú (second vocals and accordion), Alberto "Beto" Acosta (congas), Carlos Guerrero (drums), Brian Ortega (bass), and Carlos Zamora (accordion) [2]. Although the majority of the members were born in the United States, they maintain strong cultural ties to Mexico, with their ancestors originating from Tamaulipas and Nuevo León .

The band's musical style is characterized as norteño, a traditional Mexican regional genre that has experienced a significant resurgence in popularity in recent years [2]. What distinguishes Grupo Frontera from other norteño bands is their ability to blend traditional musical elements with contemporary influences, creating a sound that appeals to both older and younger audiences [2]. Their fashion style also reflects this fusion, as they typically wear modern streetwear brands like Prada or Balenciaga, contrasting with traditional norteño attire .

### 1.2 Musical Career and Rise to Fame

Grupo Frontera's breakthrough came in 2022 with their cumbia version of the Colombian band Morat's song "No Se Va" [3]. This cover went viral on TikTok, eventually charting on the Billboard Hot 100 at number 57 and becoming the fifth regional Mexican song to appear on this prestigious chart [4]. The band's success continued with subsequent releases, including collaborations with major artists such as Bad Bunny on "Un x100to," Fuerza Regida on "Bebé, dame," and Peso Pluma on "Tulum" [5].

In 2023, the band released their debut album "El Comienzo" (The Beginning), which featured several hit songs and collaborations . The album's success was recognized at the 25th Annual Latin Grammy Awards, where "El Comienzo" won the award for Best Norteño Album, while the single "El Amor de Su Vida" won Best Regional Mexican Song [6]. This marked a significant achievement for the young band, which had only been together for about a year at the time of these prestigious awards.

Their rapid rise to fame was further evidenced by their inclusion in major music festivals and events. In 2023, they performed at the celebration of Mexico's Independence Day in the Zócalo of Mexico City, an event headed by then-President Andrés Manuel López Obrador . They were also scheduled to perform at the 2025 Sueños Music Festival in Chicago, alongside artists such as Shakira and Don Omar .

### 1.3 Political Neutrality Prior to 2025

Before the controversy erupted in early 2025, Grupo Frontera had maintained a notably apolitical stance throughout their career. In an interview with EL PAÍS published on January 5, 2025, band member Juan Javier Cantú explicitly stated: "We rarely get involved in politics. It's very controversial for us and we just want to make music" [7]. This sentiment was echoed by other members of the band, who consistently emphasized their focus on their musical career rather than political matters.

The band's apolitical stance was also reflected in their charitable activities. In the same EL PAÍS interview, Cantú and Alberto "Beto" Acosta highlighted their donation of toys to children at a community center for deported immigrants affected by border policies . When asked about the U.S.-Mexico border wall, Acosta shared the band's disagreement with the concept, while Julián Peña Jr. commented on its incomplete status . These statements indicated a general concern for immigrant communities without explicitly endorsing any particular political party or candidate.

Their previous interactions with political figures had been largely ceremonial and non-partisan. For instance, they had performed at events organized by both Democratic and Republican officials in Texas, including the declaration of February 16, 2023, as "Grupo Frontera Day" in Edinburg, Texas . Such appearances were viewed as cultural celebrations rather than political endorsements, reinforcing their image as musicians who sought to unite rather than divide through their music.

## 2. The Political Controversy

### 2.1 The Viral Video and Initial Accusations

The political controversy surrounding Grupo Frontera began in late January 2025 following the viral spread of a TikTok video [8]. The video showed the grandmother of band member Adelaido "Payo" Solís III, known as "La Abuela Frontera," dancing to the song "YMCA" by Village People after voting [9]. This video immediately sparked controversy because "YMCA" had become closely associated with Donald Trump's 2024 presidential campaign [9].

The controversy was exacerbated when social media users claimed that the band had deleted several videos from their social media accounts that allegedly showed band members expressing support for Trump [10]. A TikTok user identified as @sarahjasmine\_\_ alleged that she had seen videos of Grupo Frontera members stating they would vote for Trump, but that these videos had been removed [8]. She claimed: "There's a video where you can see Grupo Frontera saying they vote for Trump. Now that they're canceling different Latino influencers who support Donald, I have to talk about this because I saw them" [8].

The situation escalated when users discovered what they believed to be additional evidence of political support. They claimed to have found screenshots of the band members' personal Instagram accounts following Trump's profile and liking his posts . Furthermore, some users alleged that the band had deleted a TikTok video in which they themselves were dancing to the same "YMCA" song as Solís's grandmother .

### 2.2 Social Media Backlash and Boycott Calls

The accusations against Grupo Frontera triggered an immediate and severe backlash on social media platforms, particularly on X (formerly Twitter) and Instagram [11]. The band's social media accounts, which had accumulated over 2.3 million followers, were flooded with negative comments and criticism [11]. Many fans expressed feelings of betrayal, arguing that the band was turning its back on its own community by supporting policies that negatively affected Mexican immigrants [11].

The backlash was particularly intense among Mexican fans, who viewed any association with Trump as a betrayal given his well-known anti-immigration stance and policies . Social media users began calling for a boycott of the band, using hashtags such as #CancelGrupoFrontera and accusing the band of being "traitors" to the Latino community [9]. Messages on social media included statements like: "Make music, not politics" and "We don't want racists here" .

The controversy also led to a significant drop in the band's social media following. Reports indicated that they lost thousands of followers in a short period of time [12]. The negative sentiment extended beyond mere unfollowing, with many fans expressing their intention to stop listening to the band's music entirely and urging others to do the same [8].

### 2.3 The [Change.org](https://Change.org) Petition

The social media backlash culminated in the creation of a [Change.org](https://Change.org) petition on February 11, 2025, calling for Grupo Frontera's removal from the 2025 Sueños Music Festival lineup . The petition, created by an individual using the alias "Espíritu," argued that the band's alleged support for Trump was incompatible with the festival's celebration of Latin music and culture .

The petition stated: "We want this because Grupo Frontera has been caught and found out to be Trump Supporters. This does not sit well with Latinos who may or may not be fans of the group but will not support racism. We Latinos stand united in boycotting Grupo Frontera and if they are not removed from the line-up, we will go as far as Boycotting Sueños Music Festival as well. La Raza Unida... Jamas sera Vencida!" [9]

The petition gained significant traction, accumulating over 9,700 signatures by February 12, 2025 . The festival's co-founder, Christopher Den Uijl, responded to the controversy by posting on Instagram that the festival supports the Latino community and that none of the co-founders support the president, though he did not specifically mention Grupo Frontera . Despite the controversy, Grupo Frontera remained on the festival lineup, and the event took place as scheduled on May 24-25, 2025 [13].

## 3. Grupo Frontera's Response

### 3.1 First Official Statement (February 7, 2025)

After several weeks of silence amid the growing controversy, Grupo Frontera released their first official statement on February 7, 2025, through their Instagram Stories . The statement was brief but direct, in which the band categorically denied any political affiliation or alliance with any party that opposes immigrants and the Latino community .

In this initial statement, the band emphasized their own immigrant backgrounds, stating that they and their families are immigrants from the border region . They also reaffirmed their support for the Mexican people and clarified that "the opinions of our friends and family do not represent the band" . This statement was seen by many as an attempt to distance themselves from the political controversy while acknowledging their community ties.

The band's statement came after days of mounting pressure from fans and social media users demanding clarification on their political stance. Prior to this official response, the band had maintained radio silence on the matter, with their social media accounts continuing to post content unrelated to the controversy, including a video collaboration with Spanish artist Alejandro Sanz .

### 3.2 Second Statement and Video Response (February 22, 2025)

On February 22, 2025, Grupo Frontera released a more detailed video response on Instagram, seeking to address the controversy more comprehensively [14]. In this video, lead vocalist Adelaido "Payo" Solís III made several key statements aimed at 澄清 the band's position and refuting the accusations.

Solís began by stating: "We are migrants, our parents are migrants, and we tell you straight up, we do not support any political party that is against migrants" [14]. He went on to address the misinformation that had spread on social media, saying: "On TikTok there have been so many false news stories that we are constantly having to deny each one... we can't keep up... The only opinions that matter in Grupo Frontera are those of the members, no one else can speak for us" [14].

The video also included explanations for some of the controversial content that had surfaced. Regarding the deleted TikTok video of the band dancing to "YMCA," the band claimed it was simply a backstage routine from their previous tour, the "Jugando a Que No Pasa Nada Tour," where they had danced to songs from their elementary school years . They denied that this was in any way related to political support for Trump.

Alberto "Beto" Acosta also addressed the band's initial silence, explaining that they had been overwhelmed by the amount of "fake news" circulating on the internet and had not known how to respond . He emphasized that the band's primary focus remained on making music and supporting their community, stating: "We are migrants, we are for migrants... and to the fans, you know what we have done, you know what we have supported for our race, whether in Mexico, the United States, or wherever we are, and that is what Grupo Frontera represents" [14].

### 3.3 Media Interviews and Additional Clarifications

In addition to their social media statements, Grupo Frontera gave several interviews to media outlets in an attempt to further clarify their position. One of the most significant was their interview with EL PAÍS, conducted in Madrid on January 5, 2025, and published shortly before the controversy erupted . In this interview, which took place before the viral video emerged, the band members had already expressed their general stance on politics and immigration.

During the interview, when asked about Trump's re-election and his proposals regarding the border with Mexico, Juan Javier Cantú responded: "We rarely get involved in politics. It's very controversial for us and we just want to make music. But we've always supported immigrants. Last year, we went to visit a \[community] center: we donated toys for the children. It's our neighborhood, after all" [7].

Alberto "Beto" Acosta added: "We respect the laws that the country has, but we support immigrants as best we can. We do things for the community. We donate toys for the children of the migrants who are there and who are being detained \[by the authorities]" [7]. When specifically asked about their stance on the border wall, Acosta stated that the band disagreed with the concept, while Julián Peña Jr. noted that it was only half-built .

The band also gave an interview to Jesse Cervantes for EXA FM, where they affirmed their commitment to the immigrant community and their pride in Mexican culture "without distinction of ideologies or borders" . In the red carpet interview at the 37th Lo Nuestro Awards, when asked about the controversy by Cuban-American television host Raúl de Molina, the band appeared visibly uncomfortable. Peña's response—"We dedicate ourselves to music. We love everyone and want peace and love. Take care of yourselves"—was criticized by fans as inadequate and evasive .

## 4. Reactions and Consequences

### 4.1 Public and Media Reception

The public reception of Grupo Frontera's statements was overwhelmingly negative, with many fans and social media users viewing them as insincere attempts to protect the band's reputation rather than genuine expressions of their beliefs [11]. Critics pointed out that the band had not explicitly denounced Trump by name, which they saw as a significant omission .

The media coverage of the controversy was extensive, with major outlets on both sides of the border covering the story. Mexican media outlets such as Infobae, TV Azteca, and Milenio provided detailed coverage of the controversy, often taking critical stances toward the band [12]. American media outlets including the Chicago Sun-Times, Latin Times, and The Direct also covered the story, with some focusing on the broader implications for Latino artists in the United States .

Some media outlets attempted to provide more balanced coverage. The Chicago Sun-Times noted that the connection between the band and Trump might simply be a matter of shared musical taste, suggesting that both might enjoy "YMCA" by Village People without any political significance . However, this perspective was largely overshadowed by more critical coverage that emphasized the potential betrayal of the Latino community.

The controversy also sparked broader discussions about the role of Latino artists in American society and the expectations placed upon them to represent community values. Some commentators argued that artists should be free to hold their own political opinions, while others maintained that given their platform and influence, Latino artists have a responsibility to align their public positions with the best interests of their community [11].

### 4.2 Impact on the Band's Career

The political controversy had a significant impact on Grupo Frontera's career, both in terms of their public image and their professional opportunities. The band experienced a substantial drop in social media followers, with reports indicating they lost thousands of followers in the weeks following the controversy [12]. Their social media platforms continued to receive 大量 negative comments, with many users expressing their intention to stop supporting the band .

Despite the controversy, the band's scheduled performances continued as planned. They performed at the 2025 Sueños Music Festival in Chicago on May 25, 2025, alongside artists such as Shakira and Don Omar [15]. However, there were reports of protests at some of their concerts, and videos emerged showing alleged booing during their performances, though some of these were later revealed to be manipulated or taken out of context .

The controversy also affected the band's relationships with other artists. Several prominent Latino artists spoke out against the band, including Ángela Aguilar, who had previously collaborated with them on the song "Frágil" . Other artists who had been vocal about their opposition to Trump's policies, such as Residente, Selena Gomez, and Lupillo Rivera, were praised by fans for their stance, while those who remained silent, including Grupo Frontera initially, faced criticism .

The band's commercial success also appeared to be affected by the controversy. While specific sales figures were not widely reported, industry analysts noted a decline in the band's chart performance and streaming numbers following the controversy [11]. The band had been scheduled to perform at several high-profile events in Mexico, but there were reports that some of these bookings were canceled or postponed due to the controversy [11].

### 4.3 The Aguilar Family Conflict

The Grupo Frontera controversy also sparked a public feud within the Aguilar family, one of Mexico's most prominent musical families. The conflict began when Emiliano Aguilar, son of legendary Mexican singer Pepe Aguilar, released a song titled "Ilegal" in February 2025, which was a direct criticism of Trump's deportation policies [16].

In the song, Emiliano Aguilar raps: "Todos somos habitantes del mundo y en nuestra tierra nadie debería ser ilegal" (We are all inhabitants of the world and on our earth no one should be illegal) [16]. The song was accompanied by the hashtag #TheWallIsNotEnough and was described by the artist as "a hymn for migrant workers who face the siege of American authorities" [16].

Following the release of "Ilegal," Emiliano Aguilar took to Instagram to express his anger toward Grupo Frontera, posting a series of stories with strong language, including "Fuck Grupo Frontera. Mexico deserves respect" . His brother Leonardo Aguilar, however, expressed support for the band in the comments section of one of their posts, writing: "Don't worry. The fans will continue supporting you. The others are people with nothing to do. We know who you are" .

This led to a public argument between the brothers, with Emiliano responding with more inflammatory posts, including one where he referred to Leonardo as "gallito fino" (fancy rooster) and accused him of indifference toward the immigrant community . Emiliano wrote: "Here's a Mexican who dies for his race with his balls in the right place... You've always had everything, you've never lacked the attention and love of the public" .

The conflict between the Aguilar brothers added another layer of controversy to the already tense situation. According to Jessica Luna of TVNotas, Emiliano later deleted his posts after reportedly receiving a reprimand from their father, Pepe Aguilar . However, the public feud had already drawn significant attention and further highlighted the deep divisions within the Latino community regarding political allegiances and cultural identity.

The coverage often included emotional language and emphasized the negative impact on the band's fanbase, particularly among Mexican fans who felt betrayed.

American media coverage was more varied in its approach. Other outlets, such as Latin Times, framed the controversy within the broader context of Latino celebrities and their political responsibilities, noting that while many artists had spoken out against Trump's policies, Grupo Frontera had remained silent for weeks .

The Chicago Sun-Times took a more nuanced approach, suggesting that the connection between the band and Trump might be overblown. The paper noted that both the band and Trump might simply share a taste in music, rather than political beliefs . However, this perspective was in the minority, with most media coverage emphasizing the political implications of the controversy.

Social media played a crucial role in amplifying the controversy, with platforms like X and TikTok serving as primary sources of information for many people. The Grupo Frontera political controversy has had lasting implications for the band, the Latino music industry, and discussions about political responsibility in entertainment. For the band specifically, the controversy marked a turning point in their career trajectory. Prior to the controversy, they had been experiencing rapid success, winning Latin Grammy awards and collaborating with major artists [6]. The controversy, however, damaged their reputation among a significant portion of their fanbase, particularly in Mexico and among politically active Latino communities in the United States.

The controversy also highlighted broader issues within the Latino music industry regarding political neutrality and public responsibility. Artists like Ángela Aguilar, who had collaborated with Grupo Frontera on the song "Frágil," faced questions about whether they should sever ties with the band . The situation created a precedent where artists' political beliefs, or even perceived beliefs, could significantly impact their professional relationships and opportunities.

It highlighted the tensions between cultural identity and political ideology, particularly for Mexican-American artists who straddle both cultures . The band's statement that they are "migrants supporting migrants" while maintaining American citizenship reflected these complex identities [14].

From a social perspective, the controversy revealed deep divisions within the Latino community regarding political allegiance and the role of public figures. The intense backlash against the band demonstrated the high expectations placed on Latino artists to represent community values, particularly regarding immigration issues [9]. The fact that some fans defended the band's right to hold their own political opinions, while others called for their cancellation, showed the complexity of these discussions [11].

The controversy also had implications for the music industry's approach to artist management and crisis communication. The band's initial silence and delayed response were widely criticized, suggesting that artists need more sophisticated strategies for handling political controversies . The speed at which misinformation spread on social media platforms also highlighted the challenges of managing public perception in the digital age.

Looking forward, the Grupo Frontera controversy will likely serve as a case study for other Latino artists navigating political controversies. It demonstrates the potential consequences of political neutrality in polarized times and the importance of clear, timely communication when addressing sensitive issues. The controversy also underscores the need for artists to consider the broader implications of their actions and statements, particularly when they have significant influence within their communities.

## 6. Conclusion

The Grupo Frontera political controversy of 2025 represents a significant moment in the intersection of music, politics, and cultural identity. What began as a viral video of a band member's grandmother dancing to a popular song evolved into a full-blown controversy that challenged the band's relationship with their fanbase, their industry peers, and the broader Latino community.

The controversy emerged against a backdrop of heightened political tensions in the United States, particularly regarding immigration policies under the Trump administration. The band's alleged association with Trump, based primarily on circumstantial evidence and social media speculation, triggered intense backlash from fans who viewed such support as a betrayal of their Mexican heritage and immigrant community values.

Grupo Frontera's response to the controversy was characterized by initial silence followed by carefully worded statements that attempted to clarify their position while avoiding explicit political endorsements. Their statements emphasized their own immigrant backgrounds and support for migrant communities, but these efforts were largely unsuccessful in appeasing their critics, who viewed the statements as insufficient and potentially disingenuous.

The controversy's impact extended beyond the band itself, sparking debates about the political responsibilities of Latino artists and the tensions between personal beliefs and cultural representation. The public feud within the Aguilar family further highlighted these tensions, demonstrating how political controversies can divide even those within the same cultural and professional circles.

From a journalistic perspective, the controversy revealed both the power and the limitations of social media in shaping public opinion and the challenges of reporting on complex issues involving cultural identity and political affiliation. The speed at which misinformation spread and the difficulty in verifying certain claims highlighted the challenges of maintaining accuracy in the digital age.

The Grupo Frontera controversy ultimately serves as a reminder of the complex relationship between artists and their communities, particularly in politically polarized times. It demonstrates how seemingly small events can snowball into major controversies when they touch on sensitive issues of identity, loyalty, and political belief. The controversy also underscores the importance of clear communication and thoughtful consideration of public responsibility for artists who wield significant influence within their communities.

As the dust settles on this controversy, its long-term impact on Grupo Frontera's career and the broader Latino music industry remains to be seen. However, it has already established itself as a significant event that will likely be referenced in discussions about political controversy in Latin music for years to come. The controversy's lessons about the intersection of music, politics, and culture will continue to resonate as Latino artists navigate an increasingly complex and politically charged landscape.

The Grupo Frontera political controversy thus stands as a notable example of how modern media, social platforms, and political polarization can transform a minor incident into a major cultural moment, with lasting implications for all parties involved.
