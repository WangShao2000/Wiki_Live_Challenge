# Scottish invasion of England (1648)

The **Scottish invasion of England (1648)** was a military campaign during the **Second English Civil War** in which Scottish forces known as the **Engagers** invaded northern England in support of King Charles I [1]. Led by James Hamilton, 1st Duke of Hamilton, the invasion began on 8 July 1648 and culminated in a decisive defeat at the **Battle of Preston** on 17–19 August [2]. The invasion was part of a broader series of Royalist uprisings across England and Wales in 1648 [3].

## Background

### The Engagement Treaty

The invasion originated from a secret agreement known as **"the Engagement"**, which King Charles I signed with Scottish Covenanter representatives on 26 December 1647 while imprisoned at Carisbrooke Castle on the Isle of Wight . Under the terms of this treaty, Charles promised to impose Presbyterianism on England for a period of three years in exchange for Scottish military support to restore him to his throne .

The agreement was controversial among the Scots. Those who supported the deal became known as the **"Engagers"**, while the opposing faction, led by Archibald Campbell, 1st Marquess of Argyll, became known as the **"Kirk Party"** . The Kirk Party distrusted Charles and objected to forming an alliance with English and Scottish Royalists, denouncing the Engagement as "sinful" [4].

### Political Developments in Scotland

It was not until April 1648 that the Engagers achieved a majority in the Scottish Parliament [4]. Despite the Church of Scotland declaring the Engagement a breach of God's law on 5 May 1648 [2], the Engagers proceeded with their plans. James Hamilton, 1st Duke of Hamilton, emerged as the leader of the Engager faction and was appointed to command the invasion force [5].

The Scottish army that assembled for the invasion faced significant challenges. Many experienced Covenanter veterans refused to fight for the King, leaving the army largely composed of raw levies . Additionally, there were quarrels among the Scottish leaders, and the army lacked proper training and equipment .

## The Invasion

### Crossing the Border

On 8 July 1648, the Scottish Engager army, under the command of the Duke of Hamilton, crossed the border into northern England [6]. The size of the invading force is disputed among historians. Some sources suggest it may have been as large as 20,000 men, while others estimate it at around 9,000-18,000 men [1].

The Scottish army consisted primarily of Presbyterian soldiers who had sworn to support the Engagement [1]. They marched south through Lancashire, with the intention of joining forces with English Royalists and marching on London to restore Charles I to the throne .

### March Through Lancashire

As the Scottish army moved through Lancashire, it was harassed by a small Parliamentary cavalry force under the command of General John Lambert [1]. Lambert's force successfully slowed the Scottish advance and blocked their path to the Royalist stronghold at Pontefract .

Facing these obstacles, the Scottish army changed its strategy and decided to head west toward Wales, where they hoped to link up with Royalist forces there . This change of direction proved to be a critical mistake, as it allowed Parliamentary forces to better position themselves for confrontation.

### Cromwell's Response

Oliver Cromwell, who was fighting in Wales at the time, immediately left his campaign upon hearing news of the Scottish invasion . His army was tired and poorly equipped, with torn clothes and worn-out shoes . However, Cromwell recognized the urgency of the situation and began a rapid march northward.

Along the way, Cromwell's army passed through the Midlands, where they were able to obtain new boots and stockings . By the time Cromwell reached the north, he had joined forces with Lambert's cavalry, giving him a combined force of approximately 8,600 men . Although smaller than the Scottish army, Cromwell's forces were disciplined, well-led, and moved quickly .

## The Battle of Preston

### The Engagement Begins

The decisive confrontation between the two armies occurred at Preston in Lancashire on 17 August 1648 [7]. The Battle of Preston was fought over three days in pouring rain . Cromwell's Parliamentarian army of 9,000 troops, including New Model Army soldiers and local militias from Yorkshire, County Durham, Northumberland, and Lancashire, faced the Scottish and Royalist army .

The Scottish army was severely disadvantaged by its poor leadership and organization. By the time Cromwell and Lambert caught up with them near Preston, the Engager army was dangerously strung out over twenty miles of countryside . This disorganization would prove fatal to their chances of victory.

### The Three-Day Battle

On the first day of battle (17 August), Cromwell's well-disciplined army attacked the Scottish advance guard on the road into Preston and quickly seized the town . The next day (18 August), Cromwell launched a full-scale assault on the main Scottish force, engaging them in hand-to-hand combat where both sides relied heavily on pikemen .

Cromwell first engaged 3,500 Engagers commanded by Sir Marmaduke Langdale on Preston Moor. Despite Langdale's brave defense, his forces were outnumbered and driven back to Preston . Cromwell then turned his attention to the bulk of the Engager army, who were defending Ribble Bridge and the ground around the River Darwen .

The fighting was described as "savage," but Cromwell's superior tactics and discipline ultimately prevailed . The Scottish forces were completely outmaneuvered and began a disorganized retreat .

### Pursuit and Surrender

On the third day (19 August), Cromwell relentlessly pursued the retreating Scottish army . Hamilton and the remaining Engagers fled toward Warrington, with Cromwell's forces in hot pursuit . The Scottish infantry made a final stand three miles outside Warrington, defending a pass that took Cromwell three attempts to clear .

Once the pass was secured, Cromwell marched on Warrington, where the remaining Scottish forces surrendered on 19 August 1648 [8]. The scale of the defeat was devastating for the Scots: approximately 2,000 were killed, and between 5,000 and 9,000 were captured . In contrast, Cromwell's forces suffered fewer than 100 casualties .

## Aftermath and Consequences

### Treatment of Prisoners

The captured Scottish soldiers faced harsh treatment. Many of those who had voluntarily taken up arms were transported to the New World as indentured servants or slaves, with large numbers sent to work in the plantations of Barbados and Virginia . Others were sent to the Republic of Venice . This practice of transporting prisoners to overseas colonies became a common punishment for rebels during this period.

### Execution of Leaders

The political consequences of the defeat were severe. James Hamilton, Duke of Hamilton, was captured and subsequently executed for his role in the invasion . Sir Marmaduke Langdale managed to escape and fled the country, reportedly disguised as a milkmaid .

### Impact on the Second Civil War

The Battle of Preston effectively ended the Royalist hopes of winning the Second English Civil War . Although there were a few more attempts to raise the Royalist standard in Wales and elsewhere, these were quickly suppressed by Parliamentary forces .

The crushing defeat at Preston had a profound impact on the course of the English Civil War. It demonstrated the superiority of Cromwell's New Model Army and convinced Parliament that Charles I could never be trusted [3].

### Execution of Charles I

The events of 1648, particularly the Scottish invasion and its aftermath, played a crucial role in the decision to try and execute King Charles I . The invasion convinced Parliament that the King was actively seeking foreign military support against them, and that he could not be trusted to keep any agreements [3].

On 6 December 1648, Parliament was purged of its more moderate members in an event known as **"Pride's Purge"** . This left a rump Parliament that was more favorable to trying the King. Charles I was put on trial for treason, found guilty, and executed on 30 January 1649 [9].

### Long-term Significance

The Scottish invasion of 1648 and its defeat at Preston marked a turning point in British history. It led directly to the execution of Charles I and the establishment of the Commonwealth of England . The invasion also demonstrated the military superiority of Cromwell's New Model Army and the effectiveness of professional, disciplined troops over poorly organized levies.

The harsh treatment of the Scottish prisoners, including their transportation to the colonies, had lasting consequences for Anglo-Scottish relations. The invasion and its aftermath also contributed to the development of anti-royalist sentiment and the rise of republican ideas in England.

## Legacy

The Scottish invasion of England in 1648 is remembered as a significant event in the Wars of the Three Kingdoms. It represents the final attempt by the Scots to restore Charles I to the throne through military force. The decisive defeat at Preston not only ended the Second English Civil War but also sealed the fate of the monarchy in England.

The invasion highlighted the political divisions within Scotland between those who supported the Engagement and those who opposed it. These divisions would continue to influence Scottish politics for many years to come. The memory of the invasion and the harsh treatment of the prisoners also contributed to anti-English sentiment in Scotland.

Today, the Battle of Preston is commemorated as one of Cromwell's greatest military victories. The site of the battle has historical significance, and several monuments and plaques mark the events of August 1648. The invasion and its consequences remain an important topic in the study of British civil war history and the development of constitutional monarchy in Britain.
