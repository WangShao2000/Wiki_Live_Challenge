# Central Africa Regiment

The Central Africa Regiment was a British colonial military unit that served in what is now Malawi during the late 19th and early 20th centuries. It was formed in 1896 as the British Central African Rifles and underwent two name changes before being amalgamated into the King's African Rifles in 1902 . The regiment was notable for its service in several campaigns across Africa, including the suppression of the Ngoni Rebellion, the War of the Golden Stool, and operations against the "Mad Mullah" in Somaliland [1].

## 1. History

### 1.1 Formation and Early Years

The Central Africa Regiment traces its origins to 1896 when it was established as the British Central African Rifles . The force was initially composed of 738 African troops and 175 Sikh soldiers . This multi-ethnic composition reflected the British colonial strategy of maintaining a mixed force with both local African recruits and Indian troops serving as a stabilizing element.

In 1898, the unit underwent its first name change, dropping the word "British" from its title to become simply the Central African Rifles . This renaming was significant as it marked a shift in the British colonial administration's approach to local military forces, signaling a move toward greater Africanization of the unit's identity while maintaining British command structure.

The following year, in 1899, the force was renamed once more to become the Central Africa Regiment . This final name change occurred shortly before the regiment was to participate in several major military campaigns that would define its legacy.

### 1.2 Campaigns and Operations

The Central Africa Regiment saw extensive action in several military campaigns across Africa. In 1898, the regiment, along with its Indian contingent, played a crucial role in suppressing the Ngoni Rebellion in northeastern Rhodesia [1]. During this campaign, the regiment's seven-pounder guns were used "with great effect" against the rebels [1].

One of the regiment's most significant victories came on September 30, 1899, when it fought in the Battle of Obassa, achieving a decisive victory that led to the end of a war . The exact nature of this conflict and its broader context remain somewhat unclear from available sources, but the battle is noted as a key moment in the regiment's history.

In 1900, elements of the Central Africa Regiment were deployed to the Gold Coast (modern-day Ghana) to participate in the War of the Golden Stool against the Asante Empire . This conflict, also known as the Yaa Asantewaa War, was a major uprising against British colonial rule . Half of the regiment's battalion was sent to the Gold Coast, while the other half was engaged in suppressing an uprising in the Gambia Colony and Protectorate .

The regiment also participated in the Somaliland campaign against Muhammad ibn 'Abdallah Hassan, known to the British as the "Mad Mullah" . This campaign, which lasted from 1900 to 1920, was a series of conflicts between the British and the Dervishes led by Hassan [2]. The Central Africa Regiment's involvement in these operations helped earn it a reputation "rare amongst colonial troops for their skills and professionalism" .

### 1.3 Amalgamation into the King's African Rifles

On January 1, 1902, the Central Africa Regiment was amalgamated with the East Africa Rifles and Uganda Rifles to form the King's African Rifles (KAR) [3]. This reorganization was part of a broader effort to centralize British colonial military forces in East Africa and create a more unified command structure .

The formation of the KAR created six battalions, with the Central Africa Regiment contributing the 1st (Nyasaland) Battalion and the 2nd (Nyasaland) Battalion [3]. These battalions were also known as the 1st and 2nd Central African Battalions [3]. At the time of formation, the King's African Rifles included 4,683 men, including 104 British officers .

## 2. Organization and Structure

### 2.1 Initial Composition

The Central Africa Regiment initially consisted of six companies with distinct ethnic compositions . Company A, D, and E were nominally Tonga, Companies B and C were Yao, and Company F was composed of Chewa and Ngoni . This ethnic organization reflected the British colonial strategy of maintaining separate units based on tribal affiliations, which was believed to reduce the risk of unified resistance while leveraging traditional leadership structures.

### 2.2 Command Structure

The Central Africa Regiment was commanded by several notable British officers. Alfred Sharpe and Willoughby Verner are specifically mentioned as notable commanders . These officers were responsible for leading the regiment through its various campaigns and maintaining discipline among the diverse ethnic groups within the unit.

The command structure followed the typical British colonial model, with British officers in command positions and non-commissioned officers (NCOs) drawn from both British and Indian ranks. This structure provided a chain of command that was familiar to British military doctrine while allowing for some cultural adaptation in dealing with African troops.

### 2.3 Size and Strength

The Central Africa Regiment varied in size during its existence, typically maintaining between one and two battalions . The exact strength of the regiment at any given time is not precisely documented, but historical records indicate that it was comparable in size to other colonial units of the period.

When the regiment was amalgamated into the King's African Rifles in 1902, its contribution included two full battalions. The 1st (Nyasaland) Battalion served from 1902 to 1964, while the 2nd (Nyasaland) Battalion served from 1902 to 1963 . These battalions were later incorporated into the independent nations' armies, with the 1st Battalion becoming the 1st Battalion, Malawi Rifles, and the 2nd Battalion becoming the 2nd Battalion, Northern Rhodesia Regiment (later Zambia Regiment) .

## 3. Equipment and Uniforms

### 3.1 Weaponry

The Central Africa Regiment was equipped with standard British colonial military weapons of the period. Historical records indicate that the regiment had access to seven-pounder guns, which were used effectively during the suppression of the Ngoni Rebellion in 1898 [1]. These weapons were particularly valuable in African campaigns where they could breach mud walls and wooden stockades and break up concentrations of warriors .

The infantrymen were likely equipped with the standard British service rifles of the time, which would have included the Martini-Henry rifle and later the Lee-Metford or Lee-Enfield rifles. The regiment's Indian contingent would have been armed similarly to British Indian Army units of the period.

### 3.2 Uniforms and Insignia

While specific details about the Central Africa Regiment's uniforms are limited, it can be inferred from historical context that they followed the general pattern of British colonial forces in Africa. The regiment's successor units in the King's African Rifles wore distinctive uniforms that included blue jerseys with khaki shorts and blue puttees, along with red fezzes (tarboosh) .

The 1st and 2nd Battalions of the Central Africa Regiment (later part of KAR) were noted for wearing black fezzes rather than the standard red ones . This distinctive headgear may have been unique to the Central African units and served as a way to distinguish them from other battalions in the King's African Rifles.

## 4. Legacy and Impact

### 4.1 Transformation into National Armies

The Central Africa Regiment's legacy extends beyond its six-year existence as an independent unit. When the various territories from which it was recruited gained independence, the regiment's successor units were incorporated into the new national armies . The 1st Battalion became the 1st Battalion, Malawi Rifles, while the 2nd Battalion became the 2nd Battalion, Northern Rhodesia Regiment, which later became part of the Zambia Regiment .

This transformation represented the culmination of a process that began with the regiment's formation in 1896. The British colonial military structure, with its emphasis on discipline, organization, and modern weaponry, provided a foundation for the armed forces of the newly independent nations of Malawi and Zambia.

### 4.2 Military Traditions and Customs

The Central Africa Regiment established several military traditions that were carried forward by its successor units. The use of English as the common language of command, for example, was maintained even when former members of the regiment served in German forces . When German forces in East Africa recruited former 2nd Central Africa Battalion soldiers in 1911, they retained English as the language of command and even kept British bugle calls .

The regiment's reputation for professionalism and skill in combat also influenced later military forces. The Central Africa Regiment earned a reputation "rare amongst colonial troops for their skills and professionalism" through its service in various campaigns . This reputation helped establish a standard of military excellence that was maintained by the Malawi Rifles and other successor units.

### 4.3 Historical Significance

The Central Africa Regiment holds an important place in the military history of British colonial Africa. It was one of the three original regiments (along with the East Africa Rifles and Uganda Rifles) that formed the foundation of the King's African Rifles in 1902 . The KAR went on to become one of the most famous colonial military units, serving with distinction in both World Wars and various colonial campaigns.

The regiment's formation in 1896 marked a significant development in British colonial military policy in Africa. It represented a shift from ad hoc local levies to more professional, organized military units that could be deployed across different territories as needed. The regiment's participation in campaigns across Africa, from the Gold Coast to Somaliland, demonstrated the flexibility and effectiveness of this approach.

The Central Africa Regiment also played a role in the broader context of British imperial expansion and consolidation in Africa. Its campaigns helped establish and maintain British control over territories that would later become part of modern Malawi, Zambia, and other African nations. The regiment's legacy thus extends beyond military history into the broader story of African colonialism and independence.

## 5. Commanders and Notable Personnel

### 5.1 Commanding Officers

The Central Africa Regiment was led by several notable British officers during its six-year existence. Alfred Sharpe and Willoughby Verner are specifically identified as notable commanders of the regiment . These officers were selected for their experience in African colonial warfare and their ability to command multi-ethnic forces effectively.

Colonel William Manning, while not specifically identified as a commander of the Central Africa Regiment, had significant connections to the unit. Manning commanded operations against Chief Mpezeni in northeast Rhodesia in 1898, for which he was promoted to brevet major in 1898 and brevet lieutenant-colonel in 1899 [4]). During the Anglo-Boer War, Manning was noted as a spectator at a military event where a "dusky and handsome fellow of the 1st battalion central africa regiment" was in attendance [5].

### 5.2 Other Notable Personnel

Alexander Cobbe served as second-in-command of the 1st Battalion, Central African Rifles, in October 1899, with the local rank of captain [6]. Cobbe's appointment highlights the British military's practice of appointing experienced officers to command colonial units, even at the battalion level.

The regiment also included Indian soldiers from the 23rd and 32nd Pioneers and Hyderabad Lancers, who served as a nucleus for the police force in Central Africa [7]. These Indian troops provided valuable experience and stability to the predominantly African regiment, serving as both trainers and reliable fighting men until the loyalty and effectiveness of the local troops could be ensured.

## 6. Operations and Campaigns

### 6.1 The Ngoni Rebellion (1898)

One of the Central Africa Regiment's earliest major campaigns was the suppression of the Ngoni Rebellion in 1898 in northeastern Rhodesia (modern-day Zambia) [1]. The regiment's Indian contingent, along with their seven-pounder guns, played a crucial role in this campaign. The guns were particularly effective against the Ngoni warriors, who fought from mud-walled villages and stockades .

The Ngoni were a powerful warrior people who had migrated from southern Africa and established themselves in what is now Malawi and Zambia. Their rebellion threatened British colonial authority in the region, and the Central Africa Regiment's successful suppression of this uprising helped establish British control over the area.

### 6.2 The War of the Golden Stool (1900)

In 1900, the Central Africa Regiment was called upon to participate in the War of the Golden Stool, also known as the Yaa Asantewaa War or the Third Ashanti Expedition . This conflict arose when the British attempted to find the sacred Golden Stool of the Ashanti people, which was considered the throne of the Ashanti Empire and the symbol of their nationhood.

Half of the Central Africa Regiment's battalion was deployed to the Gold Coast (modern-day Ghana) to participate in this campaign, while the other half was engaged in suppressing an uprising in the Gambia Colony and Protectorate . This simultaneous deployment across different territories demonstrated the regiment's versatility and the British colonial authorities' confidence in its capabilities.

Historical records mention a Captain who served as a special service officer attached to the 2nd Battalion Central Africa Regiment during the Ashanti 1900 campaign. This officer was one of two British officers who participated in the capture of the powerful Ashanti chief Kobina Cherri at Su in Jam [8].

### 6.3 The Somaliland Campaign

The Central Africa Regiment also participated in the Somaliland campaign against Muhammad ibn 'Abdallah Hassan, known to the British as the "Mad Mullah" . This campaign was part of a series of conflicts between the British and the Dervishes that lasted from 1900 to 1920 [2].

Hassan led a holy war against British, Italian, and Ethiopian colonial intrusions in the Somali peninsula . The Central Africa Regiment's involvement in these operations was part of a broader British effort to maintain control over their Somaliland protectorate. The regiment's service in these difficult campaigns, fighting in harsh desert conditions against determined opponents, helped establish its reputation for professionalism and skill.

### 6.4 The Battle of Obassa

The Battle of Obassa, which took place on September 30, 1899, was a decisive victory for the Central Africa Regiment . The battle resulted in the end of a war, though the specific conflict and its broader context are not clearly documented in the available sources.

This victory was significant enough to be specifically mentioned in historical records as a key moment in the regiment's history. The decisive nature of the victory suggests that it was a major engagement that had far-reaching consequences for British colonial control in the region where it occurred.

## 7. Conclusion

The Central Africa Regiment, though it existed for only six years (1896-1902), played a significant role in the military history of British colonial Africa. From its formation as the British Central African Rifles to its amalgamation into the King's African Rifles, the regiment served as a crucial instrument of British imperial policy in Central and East Africa.

The regiment's military campaigns demonstrated its versatility and effectiveness across different African territories and against various opponents. Whether fighting the Ngoni in Rhodesia, participating in the War of the Golden Stool in the Gold Coast, or campaigning against the Mad Mullah in Somaliland, the Central Africa Regiment consistently performed with the professionalism that earned it a reputation "rare amongst colonial troops" .

The legacy of the Central Africa Regiment extends beyond its immediate military contributions. Through its successor units in the King's African Rifles and eventually in the national armies of Malawi and Zambia, the regiment helped establish military traditions and organizational structures that continue to influence African military forces today. The regiment's use of English as the common language of command, its emphasis on discipline and training, and its integration of different ethnic groups within a unified military structure all became lasting features of African military organizations.

The Central Africa Regiment thus stands as an important example of how colonial military forces shaped the development of modern African nations. While its primary purpose was to serve British imperial interests, the regiment also contributed to the modernization of military forces in the territories where it served, providing a foundation for the professional armies of independent Africa.

The regiment's history also highlights broader themes in the study of colonial military forces, including the challenges of commanding multi-ethnic troops, the adaptation of European military methods to African conditions, and the complex relationships between colonial powers and indigenous populations. Through its six years of service, the Central Africa Regiment left an indelible mark on the military and political history of Central Africa, a legacy that continues to resonate in the nations that emerged from the British colonial empire.
