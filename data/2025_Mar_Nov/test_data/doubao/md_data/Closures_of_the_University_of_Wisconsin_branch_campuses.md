# Closures of the University of Wisconsin branch campuses

The **closures of the University of Wisconsin branch campuses** refer to a series of campus closures and shutdowns that have occurred within the University of Wisconsin System since 2023, following a major restructuring of the system's two-year campuses in 2018-2019.  These closures represent the most significant reduction in higher education access in Wisconsin since 1980, when the University of Wisconsin–Medford campus closed.

The closures have been driven by declining enrollment, demographic changes, and financial pressures facing both the individual branch campuses and the broader UW System. [1] As of 2025, six branch campuses have closed or announced plans to close, leaving only seven of the original thirteen two-year campuses fully operational. [2] The closures have had profound impacts on students, faculty, and the rural communities that host these institutions, sparking debates about the future of higher education access in Wisconsin. [3]

## Background and restructuring of the UW Colleges system

The University of Wisconsin Colleges system was established as a network of thirteen two-year campuses across Wisconsin, providing accessible higher education opportunities primarily to rural and non-traditional students.  In October 2017, University of Wisconsin System President Ray Cross publicly proposed restructuring the UW Colleges system to bring these campuses under the control of their nearest comprehensive university, creating regional two-year campuses within the system.

The restructuring was approved by the UW Board of Regents and officially took effect on July 1, 2018. [4] Under this new structure, the UW Colleges system was dissolved as an individual entity, and each campus became a branch campus of a nearby four-year university. [5] The restructuring aimed to address enrollment declines and financial pressures facing the two-year campuses, which had seen enrollment drop by 32 percent in some locations. [6]

However, despite the restructuring, the branch campuses continued to face significant challenges. Total branch campus enrollment more than halved from 2018 to 2023, falling from 9,741 students in fall 2018 to an estimated 4,803 in fall 2023.  This dramatic decline, combined with ongoing financial pressures, ultimately led to the series of closures beginning in 2023.

## List of closed or closing campuses

### 1. UW-Platteville Richland

The **University of Wisconsin–Platteville Richland** campus was the first to close since the restructuring, and the first UW campus to close since UW-Medford in 1980.  It was announced on November 22, 2022, that the campus would close on July 1, 2023, due to declining enrollment.  At the time of closure, the campus had only 64 students enrolled, a dramatic decline from previous years.

Local leaders near the Richland Center campus tried for months to negotiate a new partnership with the university system, but UW Vice President for University Relations Jeff Buhrandt announced that the campus would be completely vacated by July, with \$2 million in grants being the state's final offer. [3] The closure marked the end of 55 years of higher education in Richland County.

### 2. UW-Milwaukee at Washington County

The **University of Wisconsin–Milwaukee at Washington County** campus closed in 2024. [7] Enrollment at this campus had declined precipitously over the decade, falling from 1,054 students in 2010 to just 276 students by closure—a nearly 75 percent decline.

The campus served as an important educational resource for students in Washington County, many of whom were non-traditional students or those seeking a more affordable alternative to four-year universities.  Students expressed frustration about the closure, noting that many of their peers might stop pursuing higher education altogether due to the lack of nearby alternatives.

### 3. UW-Milwaukee at Waukesha

The **University of Wisconsin–Milwaukee at Waukesha** campus announced its closure on March 11, 2024, with classes ending at the conclusion of the spring 2025 semester.  At the time of the announcement, the campus had 672 students enrolled.  This closure was the most recent announced as of 2025. [3]

The Waukesha campus closure followed similar patterns of declining enrollment and financial pressures facing other branch campuses. Students and faculty expressed concern about the loss of educational opportunities in the Waukesha area, particularly for students who relied on the campus's smaller class sizes and individualized attention.

### 4. UW-Oshkosh Fond du Lac

The **University of Wisconsin–Oshkosh Fond du Lac** campus closed in 2024. [8] Enrollment at this campus had fallen from 733 students in 2010 to an estimated 243 students at closure.  The campus had deep roots in the Fond du Lac community, with many families having multiple generations of alumni.

Student Matthew Caine, whose father and brother are Fond du Lac alumni and whose five uncles attended the school, expressed the emotional impact of the closure: "If it wasn't for this college, I wouldn't be here."  The closure left students like Caine without options to attend a UW school close to home.

### 5. UW-Oshkosh Fox Cities

The **University of Wisconsin–Oshkosh Fox Cities** campus in Menasha announced its closure in June 2024, with in-person learning ending after the spring 2025 semester.  This campus had experienced one of the most dramatic enrollment declines, falling from 1,115 students in 2018 to 497 in 2022, and further to just 563 students in fall 2023.

Projections showed that fewer than 100 students could attend the school by 2032, well below the 491 students needed to remain financially viable.  The campus's 16 faculty members were transferred to UW-Oshkosh, while the future of its 64 staff members depended on the four-year school's 2026 budget.

### 6. UW-Platteville Baraboo Sauk County

The **University of Wisconsin–Platteville Baraboo Sauk County** campus will cease operations on May 22, 2026. [9] This announcement came in October 2025, making it the most recent closure to be announced. [9] The campus will downsize its operations to a single building by fall 2025, giving the other buildings back to Sauk County for other uses.

The Baraboo campus closure represents the eighth UW branch campus to close or downsize since 2023 due to low enrollment. [10] The campus had been offering primarily associate degrees, alongside one type of bachelor's degree, but faced increasing financial pressures.

## Reasons for closures

### Declining enrollment

**Enrollment decline** has been the primary driver of the branch campus closures. [1] The UW System has pointed to "market realities" such as drops in enrollment as the justification for the closures. [1] Between 2018 and 2023, total branch campus enrollment more than halved, from 9,741 students to an estimated 4,803 students.

Several factors contributed to this decline:



1. **Demographic changes**: The UW System has faced a "demographic cliff," with fewer traditional-age (18-22 year old) students available for higher education. [11] This pattern is consistent with national trends affecting higher education institutions across the United States.

2. **Shift in student preferences**: UW System President Jay Rothman stated that "the market is telling us that the mission of the two-year branch campuses is not as attractive to prospective students as it once was."  Students are increasingly choosing technical colleges or other educational pathways.

3. **Decline in dual-enrollment programs**: Some of the enrollment drop is attributed to fewer high school students taking dual-enrollment classes while still in high school. [11]

4. **Competition from technical colleges**: The Wisconsin Technical College System has expanded its offerings, providing alternative pathways for students seeking two-year degrees.

### Financial pressures

The branch campuses faced **significant financial challenges** that made their continued operation unsustainable. [12] These financial pressures included:



1. **High operational costs**: The UW System's internal briefing noted that "branch campus student costs are higher than main campuses" and that gains in two-year degree interest are "improbable."

2. **State funding cuts**: The UW Colleges experienced significant state funding cuts in the 2015-2017 biennium under the previous administration, which contributed to the need for regionalization and later restructuring. [13]

3. **Structural deficits at host universities**: Several of the four-year universities hosting branch campuses faced their own financial crises. UW-Oshkosh faced an $18 million structural deficit, while UW-Milwaukee had the largest projected deficit at $18.8 million.

4. **Budget cuts and layoffs**: In response to financial pressures, UW Oshkosh cut over 200 non-faculty employees and administrators in fall 2023, representing about 14% of its workforce, and furloughed all others.

### UW System restructuring and policy changes

The UW System's internal briefing from September 19, 2023, recommended that the system **downsize its remaining branch campuses** and shift its focus away from two-year degree programs. [14] The briefing suggested:



1. **Reduced physical footprints**: Negotiating to reduce space allocations and footprints of remaining campuses, repurposing facilities, and negotiating exits where appropriate.

2. **Strategic shift in mission**: The briefing recommended that the UW System emphasize its mission "as a four-year/graduate degree provider" and eliminate "two-year" nomenclature surrounding the branch campuses.

3. **New partnerships**: The briefing suggested tailoring new dual-enrollment programs with high schools or working with local businesses on employment training opportunities.

## Impact on students and communities

### Educational access and student displacement

The closures have had a **profound impact on educational access** in rural and suburban communities across Wisconsin. [3] Students who attended branch campuses often came from smaller towns and rural areas, were non-traditional students seeking degrees later in life, or needed more academic support or were low-income.

The loss of these campuses has created significant challenges for affected students:



1. **Limited alternatives**: Many students are forced to travel long distances to attend other institutions. For example, students from Washington County who previously attended the UW-Milwaukee Washington County campus now face a 40-mile commute to UW-Milwaukee.

2. **Economic barriers**: The closure of the Washington County campus was particularly impactful as it ended a 24-year-old transit service, making it even more difficult for students without private transportation to access higher education.

3. **Academic disruption**: Students in degree programs that were discontinued had to find alternative pathways to complete their education, often requiring them to transfer to different institutions and potentially lose credits.

### Faculty and staff impacts

The closures have also significantly affected **faculty and staff** who worked at the branch campuses.  Many faculty members had spent their entire careers at these institutions:



1. **Job losses and uncertainty**: Faculty and staff faced layoffs, forced retirements, or uncertain futures. Some faculty members, like Christine Klingbiel, who moved to Washington County from Marshfield specifically to avoid a looming closure, found themselves facing unemployment again.

2. **Emotional impact**: Long-serving faculty members expressed deep sadness about the loss of their institutions and the impact on their communities. History professor Paisley Harris, who worked at Fond du Lac for 21 years, noted: "I think it's just going to be a terrible loss to the community."

3. **Career disruptions**: Many faculty members had to seek positions at other institutions or consider early retirement. While some faculty at Fond du Lac were promised positions at UW-Oshkosh, those at Washington County were left waiting for news about their employment futures.

### Community and economic impacts

The closures have created **significant economic and social impacts** on the communities that hosted these campuses. [3]



1. **Economic losses**: The campuses served as economic anchors in their communities, bringing students and employees who spent money locally. At a student apartment complex next to the Fond du Lac campus, the number of UW students renting units dropped from a majority in 2018 to just 10-15% by closure.

2. **Building repurposing challenges**: Since the branch campus facilities are owned by the counties and leased to the UW System for \$1 per year until 2042, county leaders face the challenge of finding new uses for these buildings.

3. **Social and cultural impacts**: The campuses provided not just education but also cultural events, community programs, and served as gathering places for their communities. The loss is felt beyond just educational opportunities.

4. **Legislative response**: In response to community concerns, Governor Tony Evers signed a bill in April 2023 releasing up to $20 million—up to $2 million per county—for counties affected by branch campus closures.

## Current status and future outlook

As of 2025, the UW System's branch campus landscape has been significantly altered. Of the original thirteen two-year campuses that comprised the UW Colleges system:



* **Six campuses have closed or announced closure**: UW-Platteville Richland, UW-Milwaukee at Washington County, UW-Milwaukee at Waukesha, UW-Oshkosh Fond du Lac, UW-Oshkosh Fox Cities, and UW-Platteville Baraboo Sauk County

* **One campus remains open without in-person classes**: UW-Green Bay Marinette

* **Seven campuses remain fully operational**: UW-Eau Claire Barron County, UW-Green Bay Manitowoc, UW-Green Bay Sheboygan, UW-Stevens Point Marshfield, UW-Stevens Point Wausau, UW-Whitewater Rock County, and one other campus [2]

The UW System's internal briefing from September 2023 recommended that the system **downsize its remaining branch campuses** and shift its focus away from two-year degree programs.  The briefing suggested that the UW System emphasize its mission "as a four-year/graduate degree provider" and eliminate "two-year" nomenclature surrounding the branch campuses.

However, the UW System has stated that these recommendations are not final decisions. Mark Pitsch, media relations director for UW System, emphasized that "these are point-in-time briefing documents during an ongoing evaluation of our branch campuses and don't reflect final decisions."

## Criticism and controversy

The branch campus closures have generated **significant criticism and controversy** from various stakeholders. [3]

### Criticism of closure process



1. **Lack of community input**: Critics argue that the closure decisions were made without sufficient input from local communities, faculty, and students.

2. **Short timelines**: The closure announcements often gave students and faculty little time to prepare or find alternatives.

3. **Unclear criteria**: The criteria used to determine which campuses would close were not always transparent, leading to perceptions of arbitrary decision-making.

### Alternative perspectives on the closures



1. **Financial necessity**: Supporters of the closures argue that they were financially necessary given the severe enrollment declines and the high costs of operating the campuses.

2. **Mission misalignment**: Some argue that the branch campuses' mission of providing two-year liberal arts education is no longer aligned with student needs and market demands.

3. **Alternative models**: Critics suggest that the UW System should have explored alternative models for the branch campuses, such as partnerships with technical colleges or community-based organizations, before resorting to closure.

### Political and legislative response

The closures have also generated political controversy:



1. **Partisan disagreements**: Governor Tony Evers has blamed Republican lawmakers for inaction and funding cuts over the past decade, arguing that these policies contributed to the need for closures. [15]

2. **Legislative support**: Republican legislators have supported bills to provide financial assistance to counties affected by closures, including up to \$2 million per county in redevelopment funds.

3. **Budget proposals**: Governor Evers has proposed an \$800 million increase for the UW System in the next state budget, though the odds of this passing are considered low if Republicans maintain control of the Legislature.

## Conclusion

The closures of the University of Wisconsin branch campuses represent a **watershed moment** in Wisconsin's higher education history.  These closures, occurring primarily between 2023 and 2026, have dramatically reduced access to higher education in rural and suburban communities across the state, affecting thousands of students, faculty, and community members.

The closures were driven by complex factors including **demographic changes, declining enrollment, and financial pressures** facing both the branch campuses and the broader UW System. [1] While the closures may have been financially necessary from the perspective of the UW System, they have resulted in significant educational, social, and economic losses for the affected communities.

The **long-term implications** of these closures remain uncertain. The UW System's shift away from two-year degree programs and toward its mission as a four-year/graduate degree provider represents a fundamental change in the state's higher education landscape.  Whether this shift will adequately serve the educational needs of Wisconsin's diverse population, particularly non-traditional students and those in rural areas, remains to be seen.

The controversy surrounding these closures has also highlighted broader questions about the **future of public higher education** in an era of demographic change, fiscal constraints, and evolving student needs.  As the remaining branch campuses face continued scrutiny and potential downsizing, the debate over access to higher education in Wisconsin is likely to continue.

The closures serve as a cautionary tale about the challenges facing public higher education institutions nationwide, particularly those serving rural and underserved populations. [16] As other states grapple with similar demographic and financial pressures, the Wisconsin experience provides valuable lessons about the difficult decisions that may be necessary to maintain the viability of public higher education systems.

The legacy of these closures will be felt for decades to come in the communities that lost their campuses, in the students who were displaced, and in the broader conversation about the role of public higher education in serving all members of society.
