# Morris Park Aerodrome

**Morris Park Aerodrome** was a historic airfield that operated from 1908 to 1910 in what is now the Morris Park section of the Bronx, New York.  It was the world's first officially established airport, built on the site of a defunct horse and automobile racetrack. [1] The aerodrome served as a crucial center for early aviation development in the United States, hosting pioneering aircraft exhibitions and serving as a workshop for experimental aircraft construction.

## History

### Establishment and Early Operations

The Morris Park Aerodrome was established when the newly formed Aeronautic Society of New York leased the 372-acre property of the defunct Morris Park Racetrack from the City of New York in July 1908.  The racetrack, originally built in 1888 by John A. Morris, had been one of the most fashionable attractions in the Bronx until its closure in 1904. [2]

The Aeronautic Society of New York was the first aeronautical society in the world to possess a machine that would fly and the first to give a public demonstration of successful human flight. [3] The society's lease of the Morris Park site marked a significant milestone in aviation history, as it provided a dedicated location for aircraft development and public exhibitions.

### First Public Air Meet

The inaugural event at Morris Park Aerodrome was the world's first public air meet, held on November 3, 1908. [4] The event attracted approximately 20,000 spectators who paid a 50-cent entrance fee plus a five-cent subway fare from Manhattan to witness the dawn of the aerial age.

The air meet featured various aerial demonstrations including "distance races for gliders," "wind wagon pursuit races," men shot from catapults, and competitions between balloon and parachute descents.  However, none of the powered machines on display were able to leave the ground that day. The only successful flight was by 16-year-old Laurence Lesh, a glider pilot who managed to take to the sky but ended up with a broken ankle after his flight.

Despite the limited success of the powered aircraft, the event was significant as it demonstrated public interest in aviation and provided a platform for early aviators to showcase their machines. The event was immortalized in a painting titled "The Fledglings" by Rudolph Dirks, the creator of the comic strip "The Katzenjammer Kids." [5]

### Glenn Curtiss and the First Flights Over New York City

In June 1909, the aerodrome hosted a series of historic flights by Glenn H. Curtiss, one of the pioneering figures in American aviation.  Curtiss performed in a biplane named the "Golden Flyer," which made the first airplane flights ever taken over New York City.  The weather conditions were reported as ideal for these historic flights.

Curtiss's appearance at Morris Park was part of a broader effort to promote aviation and develop commercial aircraft. His flights at the aerodrome demonstrated the practical potential of powered flight and helped establish New York as a center for aviation development.

### Aircraft Development and Innovation

The workshops at Morris Park Aerodrome became a hub of aviation innovation during its two-year operation.  In the twelve months following the opening of the workshops, the Aeronautic Society of New York built 24 full-sized, heavier-than-air machines, several gliders, and numerous models.

The society's workshops produced remarkable innovations in aircraft design. One notable achievement was a machine that successfully carried not only the operator but also two passengers, powered by an ordinary stock automobile motor rather than a special aeronautical motor.  This demonstrated the practical potential of adapting existing technology for aviation use.

Another significant development was an aircraft that, although it did not fly successfully, won a \$500 prize for excellence of construction. This was the first money prize ever won in America by a flying machine.

### The First Commercial Airplane

In 1909, the aerodrome witnessed another historic milestone with the delivery of the first commercial airplane ever sold. [6] Glenn Curtiss delivered this aircraft, the "Golden Flyer," to the New York Aeronautic Society at their flying field on the old Morris Park race track. [6]

The recipient of this historic aircraft was Charles F. Willard, who became the first person taught to fly by Glenn Curtiss.  Willard later became known as the first barnstormer and made his first solo flight on July 30, 1909, at Mineola, Long Island, using the Golden Flyer.  This marked the beginning of the commercial aviation industry in the United States.

### Closure and Legacy

The Morris Park Aerodrome remained in operation through December 1910.  Following its closure, the land was divided into 3,019 lots and auctioned off for residential development.  The aerodrome's closure was likely influenced by the expiration of the Aeronautic Society's lease and the growing demand for residential development in the Bronx.

The site's legacy as the world's first officially established airport has been recognized by aviation historians. [1] A government siting committee later chose the Bronx site as one of six potential airport locations for the New York region, with the other five eventually becoming LaGuardia, Newark Liberty, JFK, and Teterboro airports.

## Notable People Associated with Morris Park Aerodrome

### Aeronautic Society of New York

The Aeronautic Society of New York was founded in 1908 as the first aeronautical society in the world to possess a machine that would fly. [3] The society's membership was remarkable for its high concentration of inventors and builders, with nearly one-fourth of its entire membership being patentees or actual builders of machines.

The society's influence extended beyond its immediate membership. Through its example, it led to the formation of practical classes of instruction in New York City and several societies and clubs in different parts of the country.  Wherever the society's machine traveled, it sparked the formation of new clubs and societies in response to the interest it generated.

### Laurence Lesh

Laurence Lesh was a 16-year-old glider pilot who made the only successful flight during the first public air meet at Morris Park on November 3, 1908.  He was said to be the first known aviator in Canada, having built and flown horse-drawn gliders as early as 1907.  Lesh completed a record-setting 24-minute, 0.62-mile flight, though he suffered a broken ankle in the process.

### Charles F. Willard

Charles F. Willard (1883-1977) was a Harvard graduate and race car driver who became the first person taught to fly by Glenn Curtiss.  He received the first commercial airplane ever sold, the "Golden Flyer," from Curtiss at Morris Park in 1909. [6] Willard later became known as the first barnstormer and remained involved in aviation throughout his life, dying at age 94 in 1977.

### William Greene

William Greene was another aviator associated with Morris Park Aerodrome. He set up shop at the aerodrome and began work on his first truly successful airplane, which flew for the first time in 1909.  Greene's work at Morris Park contributed to the innovative atmosphere of aircraft development at the aerodrome.

## Physical Characteristics and Facilities

The Morris Park Aerodrome occupied the site of the former Morris Park Racetrack, which had been a 372-acre property in the Bronx.  The complex included the former racetrack facilities, stables, and a five-story clubhouse in the style of a Pompeiian villa that had been part of the original racetrack.

The aerodrome's workshops were particularly notable for their productive capacity. In addition to the 24 full-sized aircraft built there, the workshops also produced several gliders and many models.  The society's workshops became a center of innovation, with members working on various aspects of aircraft design including propellers, engines, and construction techniques.

The aerodrome also housed the largest successful airship yet made in the United States at that time.  This airship represented another aspect of the society's commitment to developing various forms of aerial transportation.

## Cultural Impact and Legacy

The Morris Park Aerodrome had a significant cultural impact beyond its immediate aviation achievements. The painting "The Fledglings" by Rudolph Dirks, created in 1908, is considered the first serious artistic attempt to depict aircraft flight.  This painting, now in the collection of the National Air and Space Museum, captures the energy and excitement of the crowd gathered for their first glimpse of winged craft.

The aerodrome's role in aviation history has been recognized by various institutions. The National Aeronautic Association (NAA), which began as the Aero Club of America in 1905, has documented the significance of early aviation sites like Morris Park in the development of American aviation. [7]

## Current Status

The site of the Morris Park Aerodrome is now part of the Morris Park neighborhood in the Bronx. [8] Following the aerodrome's closure in December 1910, the land was divided into 3,019 lots and auctioned off for residential development.

Today, the area is a stable and long-standing neighborhood, though it is actually only about a century old. [8] Some sections of the former racetrack site are now part of Loreto Playground, operated by the New York City Department of Parks & Recreation. [9]

The playground honors slain New York City Police Officer Alfred Loreto (1902-1950), who was killed while pursuing armed men who had kidnapped a neighbor.  The park includes facilities for baseball, soccer, and other recreational activities.

## Conclusion

The Morris Park Aerodrome stands as a crucial milestone in the history of aviation. As the world's first officially established airport, it provided a foundation for the development of commercial aviation in the United States. [1] The aerodrome's brief two-year operation witnessed numerous innovations in aircraft design and construction, including the development of the first commercial airplane and the first flights over New York City. [6]

The site's significance extends beyond its physical facilities to encompass the broader cultural impact of early aviation. The aerodrome brought together inventors, pilots, and the public in a shared exploration of human flight.  Through its workshops and public demonstrations, it helped establish aviation as a viable technology with practical applications.

Today, while the physical traces of the aerodrome have been replaced by residential development and parks, its legacy endures in the recognition of its role in the birth of the aviation industry. [8] The Morris Park Aerodrome remains an important chapter in the history of human achievement and technological progress.
