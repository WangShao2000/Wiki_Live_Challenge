# Breton Civil War (1341–1343)

The **Breton Civil War (1341–1343)**, also known as the **War of the Breton Succession** or the **War of the Two Jeannes**, was a succession conflict in the Duchy of Brittany that arose following the death of Duke John III on April 30, 1341 [1]. The war pitted two rival claimants—John of Montfort, the duke's half-brother, and Joan of Penthièvre, the duke's niece—against each other for control of the duchy . The conflict quickly became entangled in the broader Hundred Years' War between England and France, with King Philip VI supporting Joan and Charles of Blois while King Edward III backed John of Montfort .

The war's initial phase was marked by rapid developments, including John of Montfort's capture and imprisonment in November 1341, followed by his wife Joan of Montfort's remarkable resistance against Charles of Blois's forces [2]. The conflict reached its peak in 1342 with several major military engagements, including the siege of Hennebont and the battle of Morlaix . The war ended inconclusively with the signing of the Truce of Malestroit on January 19, 1343, which temporarily halted hostilities while the succession question remained unresolved [3].

## Background and Succession Crisis

The Duchy of Brittany was a semi-autonomous province within the Kingdom of France, with its own distinct customs and traditions . Duke John III, who ruled from 1312 until his death on April 30, 1341, left no legitimate children despite three marriages . This succession crisis created a power vacuum that would plunge Brittany into civil war.

The two primary claimants to the duchy had equally valid claims under different legal interpretations. Joan of Penthièvre was the daughter of Guy, Duke John's younger brother, who had died in 1331 . In 1337, Duke John had married Joan to Charles of Blois, a nephew of King Philip VI of France . John of Montfort, meanwhile, was Duke John's half-brother, the son of Duke Arthur II by a second marriage .

The succession dispute was complicated by conflicting legal traditions. Brittany had historically allowed female succession, as evidenced by practices in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries before Brittany attained peerage status . However, John of Montfort argued that as a peerage of the French realm, Brittany should follow the same succession practices that governed the French kingship, which explicitly excluded women .

Duke John himself appeared to favor each claimant at different times. While he was generally explicit about his distaste for John of Montfort during most of his rule, Montfort claimed that the duke had reconciled with him three years after Joan's marriage to Charles of Blois . The chronicler Guillaume de Nangis reported that Duke John had even proposed to sell the duchy to Philip VI "in order to avoid the dangers which would befall the kingdom should the duchy fall into the hands of a woman," but this plan was opposed by certain Bretons and ultimately came to nothing .

## The Outbreak of War (1341)

Following Duke John's death, the rival claimants initially agreed to submit their dispute to King Philip VI for resolution. The French king convened a council at Conflans that met in September 1341. On September 7, the council issued a judgment in favor of Joan of Penthièvre and Charles of Blois [2].

However, John of Montfort refused to accept the decision. According to chroniclers, he made a dramatic escape from the Parisian court and returned to Brittany in September 1341 . Upon his return, he seized the ducal treasury at Limoges and began assembling forces to press his claim through military means .

Charles of Blois and King Philip VI responded swiftly. In November 1341, they launched a campaign that culminated in the capture of John of Montfort at Nantes [2]. The chronicler Jean Froissart later described how Montfort was taken prisoner and sent to Paris, where he would remain imprisoned for the next four years .

The imprisonment of John of Montfort might have ended the conflict immediately, but for the remarkable actions of his wife, Joan of Montfort. Upon learning of her husband's capture, she immediately took steps to secure the treasury, moving it to the castle of Brest under the guard of Tanguy of Chastel . She then emerged as the visible head of the Montfortist party in Brittany, rallying supporters and preparing for the inevitable French assault.

## Joan of Montfort's Resistance (1341–1342)

Joan of Montfort's leadership during this critical period has been noted by chroniclers for its military and political sophistication. Contemporary accounts describe her as having "the heart of a man and a lion" . She proved herself to be a capable military commander and diplomat, performing tasks that were traditionally reserved for the male half of the noble couple.

In March 1342, Joan sent a letter to the people of Saint-Malo, promising to safeguard their town and goods by both sea and land . This document represents one of the few surviving pieces of direct evidence of her activities during this period. The letter demonstrates her commitment to military action and her recognition of the need to protect key coastal towns that remained loyal to the Montfortist cause.

By early 1342, Charles of Blois had assembled a substantial army. According to chroniclers, his forces included 5,000 French soldiers, 2,000 Genoese mercenaries, and a large but unknown number of Breton soldiers [1]. With this army, he began a systematic campaign to eliminate the remaining Montfortist strongholds.

## The Siege of Hennebont (1342)

One of the most dramatic episodes of the war occurred in May-June 1342 with the siege of Hennebont, a fortified port town in southern Brittany. Joan of Montfort had taken refuge there with her supporters, including members of the Clisson family, notably Amaury of Clisson .

The siege began when Charles of Blois's forces surrounded the town, cutting off all supply routes and launching assaults against its defenses. Joan of Montfort's leadership during this siege has been particularly celebrated in historical accounts. According to Froissart, she rallied the women and children inside the town, urging them to tear stones from the streets to use as ammunition against the attacking forces .

The most famous incident occurred when Joan devised a daring plan to break the siege. She armed herself and mounted a warhorse, leading a small group of men behind the enemy lines. Under cover of darkness, they set fire to the French camp, which was poorly guarded. The resulting confusion allowed her to lead her foes on a fruitless chase away from the town . She returned the next day to rally her troops and townspeople, who continued to hold out until help arrived.

The arrival of English reinforcements in June 1342 finally lifted the siege. A force of 234 men under Sir Walter Mauny, a member of Edward III's household, managed to penetrate the Blavet River (present-day Port-Louis) and relieve the besieged town . This marked the beginning of direct English military intervention in the Breton Civil War.

## English Intervention and the Campaign of 1342

The English had been watching developments in Brittany with increasing interest. King Edward III saw an opportunity to establish a friendly ruler in Brittany who would provide access to Breton ports, greatly aiding England's naval war against France and offering a ready entry point for English armies into France .

The first significant English force arrived in May 1342 under Sir Walter Mauny. His small contingent of 234 men successfully relieved Hennebont, where Joan of Montfort was being besieged . This initial success encouraged Edward III to plan a larger expedition.

The Earl of Northampton was appointed to command the first major contingent of the English army, consisting of 1,350 men, with orders to sail from Portsmouth on July 8, 1342. However, the English faced significant logistical challenges. On the appointed date, not a single requisitioned ship was present . Through threats and confiscations, the English eventually assembled a fleet of 440 ships, but even this would require multiple trips to transport the 6,000 men Edward wished to deploy.

By July, the military situation had deteriorated significantly for the Montfortist cause. Joan of Montfort had been forced back to the far west of Brittany and was besieged in the port of Brest, the only remaining fortified place still held by her faction . Charles of Blois had captured most of the major towns, including Auray, Vannes, and Hennebont [4]).

The English fleet finally sailed on August 14-15, 1342, with 140 transports and 120 warships. They arrived off Brest on August 18, just as the town was on the verge of surrender. The English fleet took the Genoese ships blockading the port by surprise, defeated them, and burned most of their vessels .

The arrival of the English fleet had an immediate impact on the strategic situation. Charles of Blois, seeing the large number of English ships and assuming a vast army had arrived, promptly broke off the siege of Brest and withdrew 70 miles to Guingamp . There he concentrated his forces and called up local levies to prepare for the coming English invasion.

## Edward III's Campaign in Brittany (1342–1343)

Edward III himself arrived in Brittany on October 26, 1342, after a storm-wracked three-week passage from England. His army was much smaller than planned—fewer than 5,500 men—due to the difficulties in assembling shipping . The king had been forced to abandon his siege train on the beach at Sandwich and sail with only those troops he could fit on the available vessels.

Upon landing, Edward held a council of war with his commanders and the remaining Montfortist leaders. It was decided to march on Vannes, the second most populous settlement in Brittany, which had a good harbor and strong walls. From Vannes, the English could control much of southern Brittany .

The English army marched unopposed through southern Brittany for 120 miles, halting 12 miles from Vannes to construct siege engines—replacements for those left behind in England. On November 29, they attempted to take the town by storm, but the newly reinforced French garrison successfully repelled the assault, and a siege began .

While the main English force besieged Vannes, raiding parties were sent out to devastate large parts of eastern Brittany. The Earl of Salisbury led an expedition that razed the outskirts of Dinan and devastated the area around Dol, 100 miles northeast of Vannes . Other English forces captured Ploërmel, Malestroit, and Redon, and besieged Nantes, the ducal capital.

However, the English faced significant challenges. Their numbers were insufficient to maintain control of all the territory they had occupied, and the terms of service for many of their troops were running out. It is recorded that 400 Welsh archers left the army on December 17, 1342, because their contracts had expired .

## The French Response and the Truce of Malestroit

King Philip VI responded to the English invasion by assembling a large army under his son John, Duke of Normandy. The French army gathered at Angers, the main supply center, and began a methodical advance westward .

The approach of the French army created a tense situation. Edward III, despite his outward confidence, was alarmed by the size of the French force and the precarious position of his own army. He invited two cardinals who had been sent by Pope Clement VI to attempt a settlement to come to Malestroit, 18 miles from his camp .

The French advance was rapid. On Christmas Day 1342, they relieved Nantes, just in time to foil a plot to open the gates to the English . In early January 1343, they recaptured Redon, Ploërmel, and Malestroit, and established their camp around the latter.

Negotiations between the two sides intensified. Philip VI established his court at Redon and sent emissaries to the cardinals at Malestroit. By January 19, 1343, the terms of a truce had been agreed upon and were sealed in the Chapelle de la Madeleine at Malestroit [3].

The **Truce of Malestroit** was remarkable for its terms, which historian Jonathan Sumption described as "astonishingly favorable" to the English . The truce was to last until September 29, 1346, and both sides were to retain the territory they held at the time of the truce's signing . This meant that:



* The English would garrison the fortified places they held in Brittany

* Vannes would be held by the papacy for the duration of the truce

* Philip VI would immediately free John of Montfort

* There would be a general exchange of prisoners

John of Montfort was released from prison in September 1343 and retired to England, where he remained until March 27, 1345 [5]. Edward III returned to England, arriving on March 1, 1343, after enduring another storm that scattered his fleet and sank several ships .

## Aftermath and Legacy

The Truce of Malestroit brought an end to the intense phase of the Breton Civil War (1341–1343), but it did not resolve the underlying succession dispute. The war would continue in a more sporadic fashion until 1364, when it was finally settled by the battle of Auray, in which Charles of Blois was killed [6].

The truce was short-lived in practice. Despite its three-and-a-half year duration, both sides began preparations for renewed hostilities almost immediately. The English garrisoned their positions in Brittany, with Brest remaining in English hands for the next 30 years . Late in 1343, Montfortist partisans in Vannes rose against the papal authority, expelled the garrison, and delivered the town to the English, who held it until the end of the war in 1364 .

The French, meanwhile, executed several Breton lords who were accused of supporting the English. Olivier IV de Clisson was executed in Paris on August 2, 1343, and on November 29, fourteen Breton lords were executed without trial, including Geoffroi de Malestroit, Jean de Montauban, and Alain de Quédillac [7].

The Breton Civil War had significant implications for the broader Hundred Years' War. It demonstrated the importance of Brittany as a strategic prize and showed how local disputes could draw in the major powers of Europe. The war also highlighted the changing role of women in medieval warfare, as both Joan of Montfort and Joan of Penthièvre demonstrated remarkable military and political capabilities.

The war finally ended in 1365 with the signing of the first Treaty of Guérande, which established John IV (the son of John of Montfort and Joan of Montfort) as the legitimate heir to the Duchy of Brittany . John IV subsequently signed a treaty of alliance with England, ensuring that Brittany would remain a valuable ally of England for the duration of the Hundred Years' War.

The Breton Civil War (1341–1343) thus represents a pivotal moment in medieval European history, demonstrating how personal ambitions, legal disputes, and international rivalries could combine to create prolonged conflict. The war's resolution would shape the political landscape of western France for centuries to come.
