# Vorlesungen über die Entwicklung der Mathematik im 19. Jahrhundert

**Vorlesungen über die Entwicklung der Mathematik im 19. Jahrhundert** (Lectures on the Development of Mathematics in the 19th Century) is a posthumously published work by the German mathematician Felix Klein (1849–1925) . The book was published in two volumes as volumes 24 and 25 of the Grundlehren der mathematischen Wissenschaften (Fundamental Principles of Mathematical Sciences) series in 1926 and 1927 by Julius Springer Verlag in Berlin . Based on lectures Klein delivered during World War I, the work provides his analysis of the development of mathematics in the 19th century, particularly regarding the rise of non-Euclidean geometries and the development of group theory .

## 1. Background and Publication

### 1.1 Felix Klein's Life and Work

Felix Christian Klein was born on April 25, 1849, in Düsseldorf, Prussia (Germany), and died on June 22, 1925, in Göttingen, Germany [1]. He was one of the most important mathematicians of the late 19th and early 20th centuries [2]. Klein is best known for his Erlangen Program, which emphasized the use of groups in geometry . His contributions spanned multiple mathematical fields including geometry, group theory, and function theory [3].

Klein studied at the University of Bonn, where he earned his Ph.D. in 1868 under the supervision of Julius Plücker . After Plücker's death, Klein worked with Alfred Clebsch at the University of Göttingen . In 1872, at the young age of 23, Klein was appointed professor at the University of Erlangen, where he developed his famous Erlangen Program [4]. He later taught at the Institute of Technology in Munich (1875–80) and the University of Leipzig (1880–86) before moving to Göttingen in 1886, where he remained until his retirement in 1913 .

At Göttingen, Klein played a crucial role in establishing it as one of the world's leading mathematical research centers . He recruited David Hilbert to Göttingen in 1895, a decision that would prove pivotal for the development of 20th-century mathematics . Klein was also interested in the history of mathematics and purchased relevant books for the Göttingen library . One of his students, Conrad Müller, studied for a Ph.D. in the history of mathematics under Klein and later received the first habilitation degree in this field at Göttingen .

### 1.2 The Genesis of the Lectures

Klein had planned to lecture on the development of mathematics in the 19th century in the winter semesters of 1910/11 and 1912/13, but was unable to do so on both occasions . Instead, he taught these classes privately during World War I . The winter 1914/15 courses were not announced in the Vorlesungsverzeichnis (list of lectures), while the summer 1915 and winter 1915/16 courses were announced as "privatissime and free of charge" .

The first semester of lectures was attended by 24 people, including 13 male students, 9 faculty members, and 2 women . The women were Iris Runge and Klein's daughter Elisabeth Staiger . The first two semesters of lecture notes were edited and typed by the recently widowed Staiger, while the third course was worked on by Käthe Heinemann and Helene Stähelin .

Starting in 1916, Klein taught his lectures in his own house to avoid having to walk to the university . The notes were taken by his assistant Walter Baade . Klein's lectures from 1916 onwards were concerned with relativity, and he postponed working on content regarding the works of Henri Poincaré and Sophus Lie . Klein sent a version of his summer 1917 lectures on general relativity to Albert Einstein, who dismissed the approach as overemphasizing the formal over the heuristic point of view .

### 1.3 Editorial Process

After Klein's death in 1925, the lecture notes were edited for publication . The first volume was edited by Richard Courant and Otto Neugebauer, while the second volume was edited by Courant and Stefan Cohn-Vossen [5]. In their preface, the editors explained that they had changed as little as possible in the original text and admitted it was closer to a draft than to a thorough and balanced presentation of the history .

The editors noted that some content Klein had originally envisioned as part of the text was missing . Klein had planned to include full chapters on Poincaré and Sophus Lie, but these are absent from the published version . Additionally, he had planned to discuss set theory, the 1900 International Congress of Mathematicians, and Hilbert's problems .

### 1.4 Publication Details

The first volume was published in 1926 with 386 pages (XIII + 385) [6]. The second volume was published in 1927 . The work was published as part of the Grundlehren der mathematischen Wissenschaften series, which is commonly known as the "yellow series" due to its distinctive yellow covers .

The first volume was later reprinted in 1979 by Springer-Verlag Berlin with 594 pages (XXIV + 594) [7]. This edition included a preface by Robert Hermann, who provided a lengthy appendix on "Kleinian mathematics from an advanced standpoint" .

## 2. Content Overview

### 2.1 Volume I: The Development of Mathematics in the 19th Century

The first volume is organized into eight chapters that provide a comprehensive overview of 19th-century mathematics :

**Chapter 1: Gauss** - This chapter is concerned with the life and work of Carl Friedrich Gauss and is split into sections on applied and pure mathematics . Klein discusses Gauss's contributions to both applied mathematics (including astronomy, geodesy, and electromagnetism) and pure mathematics (including number theory, geometry, and analysis) [2].

**Chapter 2: France and the École Polytechnique in the First Decades of the Nineteenth Century** - This chapter contains sections on mechanics and mathematical physics (including the work of Poisson, Fourier, and Cauchy), geometry (Monge and his followers), and analysis and algebra (focusing on Cauchy and Galois) . Klein examines how the École Polytechnique became a center for mathematical innovation in early 19th-century France [2].

**Chapter 3: The Founding of Crelle's Journal and the Rise of Pure Mathematics in Germany** - This chapter is concerned with mathematicians connected to Crelle's Journal (the analysts Dirichlet, Abel, and Jacobi and the geometers Möbius, Plücker, and Steiner) . Klein discusses how the establishment of this journal helped foster the development of pure mathematics in Germany [2].

**Chapter 4: The Development of Algebraic Geometry After Möbius, Plücker, and Steiner** - This chapter includes sections on projective geometry, invariant theory, and n-dimensional spaces and general complex numbers, including content on Grassmann and Hamilton . Klein traces the development of algebraic geometry from its origins to its more abstract forms [2].

**Chapter 5: Mechanics and Mathematical Physics in Germany and England Until About 1880** - In this chapter, Klein discusses Hamilton's and Jacobi's works on mechanics and works of English mathematicians including Green, Stokes, and Maxwell . The chapter covers both theoretical mechanics and its applications to physics [2].

**Chapter 6: The General Theory of Functions of Complex Variables According to Riemann and Weierstraß** - This chapter contrasts Riemann's approach to complex analysis with that of Weierstraß . Klein examines the two different philosophical approaches to complex function theory [2].

**Chapter 7: Deeper Insight into the Nature of Algebraic Varieties and Structures** - This chapter discusses algebraic geometry (including contributions by Clebsch and Noether) and algebraic number theory (Kummer, Dedekind, and Hilbert, among others) . Klein explores the connections between different areas of mathematics [2].

**Chapter 8: Group Theory and Function Theory; Automorphic Functions** - In this final chapter, Klein discusses group theory in connection with the works of Lagrange, Galois, and C. Jordan . In the section on automorphic forms, he treats hypergeometric functions, conformal mappings, the icosahedron, and elliptic functions, including a few pages on Poincaré .

### 2.2 Volume II: The Fundamental Concepts of Invariant Theory and Their Infiltration into Mathematical Physics

The second volume is organized into three chapters with a more systematic and factual focus :

**Chapter 1: Elementary Content Regarding the Fundamentals of Linear Invariant Theory** - This chapter is split into part A about general linear invariant theory and a "freer" part B about linear invariant theory including comments on the Erlangen Program and the development of vector and tensor analysis .

**Chapter 2: The Special Theory of Relativity in Mechanics and Mathematical Physics** - This chapter has three parts: part A is concerned with classical celestial mechanics, part B with Maxwell's electrodynamics and the Lorentz group, while part C treats the adaptation of mechanics to the relativity theory of the Lorentz group .

**Chapter 3: Transformation Groups on the Basis of a Quadratic Differential Form** - This chapter has five parts. Part A treats Lagrangian mechanics, part B the intrinsic differential geometry of surfaces following Gauss. The remaining parts are concerned with Riemannian geometry: part C contains the formal background of Riemannian manifolds, part D normal coordinates and geometric interpretations, and part E reports on the further development after the time of Riemann, including the work of Beltrami, Lipschitz, and Christoffel .

A planned fourth chapter on the general theory of relativity and Hamiltonian mechanics with a focus on contact transformations and Lie groups was not finished . The editors stated that none of the existing drafts could have been turned into a printable form without adding an additional author's thoughts to Klein's approach .

### 2.3 Notable Features and Absences

Klein's approach to writing the history of mathematics is characterized by his personal connections to many of the mathematicians he discusses . As G.A. Miller noted in his review, Klein was "the most eminent mathematician to write a general history" and had personal acquaintance with most leading mathematicians .

The work contains many biographical and historical remarks, especially in the first volume . Klein discusses the work of prominent mathematicians of the time, including Riemann, Gauss, and Lobachevsky, and their contributions to the field . He also introduces the concept of the Erlangen Program, which is a framework for understanding the various geometries in terms of their underlying symmetries .

However, the work has some notable absences. As mentioned earlier, Klein had planned to include full chapters on Poincaré and Sophus Lie, but these are missing . Additionally, he had planned to discuss set theory, the 1900 International Congress of Mathematicians, and Hilbert's problems .

## 3. Reception and Legacy

### 3.1 Contemporary Reviews

The books were received enthusiastically and especially the first volume has been widely celebrated . Contemporary reviews praised the work for its unique perspective and valuable insights:

G.A. Miller, reviewing the first volume in Science, saw Klein as the most eminent mathematician to write a general history and found the "extremely difficult task... well begun" . Miller particularly praised Klein's personal acquaintance with most leading mathematicians .

David Eugene Smith, writing for the Bulletin of the American Mathematical Society, called Klein a "master" and emphasized the lack of national prejudice in the work . This was particularly noteworthy given the patriotic atmosphere of the time.

F.P. White's admiring review in the Mathematical Gazette called the book "fascinating" and mentioned several "curious and entertaining" episodes .

Josef Lense's review praised the editors and found Klein the most suited person to the task .

The historian of mathematics Heinrich Wieleitner reviewed both volumes for ISIS. His review of the first volume warmly welcomed its publication and the information coming from Klein's personal involvement, but criticized some of Klein's methods as not up to the standards of scientific historiography . The overall verdict was that the book is "eine sehr wertvolle Vorarbeit zu einer wirklichen Geschichte der Mathematik des 19. Jahrhunderts" (a very valuable preliminary work for a true history of 19th-century mathematics) .

### 3.2 Long-term Influence

The books had a great influence on Dirk Jan Struik, who had helped the editors in preparing the original manuscripts . In his later book A Concise History of Mathematics, Struik called them "the best history of nineteenth century mathematics" .

The Soviet mathematician Vladimir Arnold deeply valued the books and remarked in an interview that much of what he had learned about mathematics ("one-half of the mathematics I know," in his own words) came through their study . He often recommended them to his students .

The work has been particularly influential in the development of the history of mathematics as a field. The books provide a unique perspective on 19th-century mathematics from someone who was personally connected to many of the key figures . The lectures give a highly personalized account of mathematical "high culture" .

### 3.3 Modern Assessment

Modern reviews have acknowledged both the value and limitations of Klein's work. The French mathematician Jean Dieudonné, reviewing the English edition, praised the idea of a new edition and noted that Klein's book has long been the only one on its topic and still among the best . Nevertheless, he gave a long list of omissions and lamented the lack of an index .

Pierre Dugac, in a French review of the German reprint of both volumes, called the book "irreplaceable" and praised Klein's expressions of the "mood" of 19th-century mathematics, but criticized the lack of a modern introduction putting the book in context of newer historical works .

Hans Wussing, welcoming the reprint, called the book a part of the fundamental mathematical-historical literature, while noting that some of Klein's opinions are subjective and have been superseded .

The mathematician and historian Detlef Laugwitz quotes Klein's Vorlesungen in his book about Bernhard Riemann, calling Klein "important as a witness of the effect of Riemann's ideas in the last decades of the 19th century" .

The historian of mathematics David E. Rowe describes them as "a highly personalized account of this period" and notes that the original lecture notes "were sometimes even more opinionated, expressing his misgivings about recent modernist trends toward abstraction and axiomatics" .

## 4. Translations

The work has been translated into several languages, indicating its international significance:

### 4.1 Russian Translations

The book was translated into Russian twice, both times as "Лекции о развитии математики в XIX столетии" (Lectures on the Development of Mathematics in the 19th Century) .

The first Russian translation appeared in 1937 and was translated by B. Livshits, A. Lopshits, Y. Rabinovich, and L. Tumerman with a preface by the Soviet historian of mathematics Mark Vygodskii . However, this translation contained some errors. For example, it mistranslated Jacobi dying from "blattern" (smallpox) as dying in a place called "Blattern" .

A second Russian translation appeared in 1989, translated by N.M. Nagorny and edited by M.M. Postnikov . In the preface, which builds on Vygodskii's, they noted that their main focus was to be as faithful as possible to Klein's thought and the spirit of his work .

In 2003, the second volume was also translated into Russian by V.A. Antonov and edited by B.P. Kondratyev .

### 4.2 English Translation

The book was translated into English by M. Ackerman as Development of Mathematics in the 19th Century in 1979 . This edition was edited by Robert Hermann, who provided a lengthy appendix on "Kleinian mathematics from an advanced standpoint" .

### 4.3 Chinese Translation

Both volumes have been translated into Chinese as "数学在 19 世纪的发展" (The Development of Mathematics in the 19th Century) as part of the "数学翻译丛书" (Mathematical Translations Series) edited by Shing-Tung Yau for Higher Education Press .

The first volume was published in 2010, translated by Qi Min You . The second volume was published in 2011, translated by Li Peilian . The first volume includes a translation into Chinese of Hermann's preface to the English translation .

## 5. Legacy and Cultural Impact

### 5.1 Historical Significance

Klein's Vorlesungen represent a unique contribution to the history of mathematics. As one of the most prominent mathematicians of his time, Klein was in a unique position to provide an insider's perspective on the developments of 19th-century mathematics . His personal connections to many of the key figures of the era give the work a distinctive character that cannot be found in more modern historical treatments.

The work is particularly valuable for its emphasis on the interconnectedness of different areas of mathematics. Klein's Erlangen Program, which classified geometries according to their underlying transformation groups, exemplifies this unifying approach . This perspective is evident throughout the Vorlesungen, where Klein consistently emphasizes the relationships between different branches of mathematics.

### 5.2 Educational Value

The books have had significant educational impact. Many mathematicians, including Vladimir Arnold and Dirk Struik, have credited them with shaping their understanding of mathematics . The work continues to be recommended as valuable reading for students and researchers interested in the history of mathematics.

The Chinese translation has made the work accessible to a large audience in China, where it has been used in mathematics education and research . The fact that the work has been translated into multiple languages and continues to be reprinted demonstrates its enduring educational value.

### 5.3 Limitations and Criticisms

Despite its many virtues, the work has some limitations. As noted by several reviewers, Klein's approach is highly personal and subjective . He sometimes expresses strong opinions about mathematical developments, particularly regarding modernist trends toward abstraction and axiomatics .

The work also has some notable gaps. Klein's failure to include chapters on Poincaré and Sophus Lie, despite planning to do so, is particularly regrettable given their importance to 19th-century mathematics . Additionally, the work focuses primarily on European mathematics, with little attention to developments in other parts of the world.

### 5.4 Modern Relevance

Despite these limitations, Klein's Vorlesungen remain relevant today. The work provides valuable insights into the historical development of many mathematical concepts that are still central to modern mathematics. The emphasis on the connections between different areas of mathematics is particularly relevant in an era when interdisciplinary approaches are increasingly valued.

The work also serves as a reminder of the importance of understanding the historical context of mathematical developments. In an age of rapid technological change, Klein's emphasis on the human side of mathematics—the personalities, the controversies, the gradual evolution of ideas—provides a valuable perspective.

## 6. Conclusion

**Vorlesungen über die Entwicklung der Mathematik im 19. Jahrhundert** stands as a landmark work in the history of mathematics literature. Written by one of the most prominent mathematicians of the era, Felix Klein, and based on lectures delivered during World War I, the work provides a unique insider's perspective on the mathematical developments of the 19th century .

The two-volume work, published posthumously in 1926 and 1927, covers a wide range of topics from the work of Gauss to the development of group theory and invariant theory . While the work has some limitations—including missing chapters on Poincaré and Lie and a highly personal perspective—it remains an invaluable resource for understanding the mathematical culture of the 19th century .

The enthusiastic reception of the work by contemporary reviewers and its lasting influence on subsequent generations of mathematicians, including figures like Dirk Struik and Vladimir Arnold, attest to its enduring value . The fact that the work has been translated into multiple languages, including Russian, English, and Chinese, demonstrates its international significance .

In conclusion, **Vorlesungen über die Entwicklung der Mathematik im 19. Jahrhundert** is not merely a historical document but a living contribution to our understanding of mathematics. As David E. Rowe notes, it provides "a highly personalized account" of a crucial period in mathematical history . For anyone interested in the development of modern mathematics, Klein's lectures remain an indispensable source.
