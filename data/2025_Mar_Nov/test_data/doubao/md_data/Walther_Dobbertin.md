# Walther Dobbertin

Walther Alexander Dobbertin was a German photographer, publisher, and author who documented colonial life in German East Africa during the early twentieth century . His extensive body of work provides one of the most comprehensive visual records of German colonial activities in East Africa, particularly during World War I, when he served as the sole combat photographer on the German side [1].

## Early Life and Background

Walther Alexander Dobbertin was born on August 28, 1882, in Berlin, Germany, to a working-class family [2]. His ancestors originated from Mecklenburg, where the Kloster Dobbertin monastery is located . In Rostock, he completed a photography apprenticeship and attended painting courses [2].

## Career in German East Africa

In 1903, Dobbertin emigrated to German East Africa (Deutsch-Ostafrika), settling initially in Dar es Salaam [1]. After working for several years in Carl Vincenti's photography studio in Dar es Salaam, a dispute arose between the two men in 1907 . Vincenti accused Dobbertin of stealing or embezzling photographic materials, resulting in Dobbertin's conviction and a three-day prison sentence .

Following this incident, Dobbertin established his own businesses. He founded book and art shops in Dar es Salaam, Tanga, and Moshi [2]. During this period, he developed into one of the most prominent photographers in German East Africa . His photographs documented various aspects of colonial life, including political, social, economic, and military events . His subjects ranged from landscapes and wildlife to portraits of indigenous people and German settlers .

## World War I Documentation

The outbreak of World War I in 1914 presented a significant opportunity for Dobbertin's career as a photographer. When all German men were mobilized in August 1914 to defend the colony, he became, in effect, the only combat photographer on the German side [3]. His documentation of military campaigns, daily life in the colony, and the activities of the German Schutztruppe (colonial defense force) provided an invaluable visual record of the conflict in East Africa .

In 1916, Dobbertin was captured by British forces while serving as a member of the German Schutztruppe [2]. He remained in British captivity until the end of the war. Following the German defeat, all Germans were expelled from East Africa and their property was confiscated . However, Dobbertin's wife, Alwine, managed to smuggle his photographic plates out of the country [2].

## Return to Germany and Later Life

After his release from captivity, Dobbertin returned to Germany and settled in Wiedenhof, now part of Jesteburg, south of Hamburg [2]. There, he established another bookshop [2].

In 1932, Dobbertin published a collection of glorifying portraits of German colonial soldiers titled "Die Soldaten Lettow-Vorbecks. Ein Buch vom deutschen Wehrwillen und deutscher Waffenehre" (Lettow-Vorbeck's Soldiers: A Book of German Fighting Spirit and Military Honor) [4]. This work contained 120 photogravures of his photographs from German East Africa . An English-language edition was published in Nashville in 2005 [5].

During the Nazi era (1933-1945), Dobbertin was a member of the Sturmabteilung (SA) and served as Kreisleiter (district leader) of the Reichskolonialbund (Reich Colonial League) [2]. After World War II ended in 1945, the British occupation authorities revoked his license to operate his business due to these activities [2]. He later regained permission to operate his bookshop, which he ran until 1960 [2].

Dobbertin died on January 12, 1961, in Jesteburg-Wiedenhof, shortly before a planned trip to Africa, which would have been his first visit since his return in 1920 [2].

## Legacy and Collections

After Dobbertin's death, his widow sold his personal documents, photographic plates, and photographs to the Bundesarchiv (German Federal Archives), where they were preserved . In 2014, the Bundesarchiv published 982 of Dobbertin's photographs on Wikimedia Commons [2].

The German National Archives in Koblenz holds Dobbertin's original glass negatives (913 cm) and makes medium-resolution scans available to the public . The high quality of his work is evident in the large format of his negatives, which produce very sharp images with pronounced depth of field .

Dobbertin's photographs continue to be used in contemporary research and exhibitions. The Usambara Knowledge Project at Utah State University uses more than thirty of his early twentieth-century landscape photographs from the Usambara Mountains in northeastern Tanzania as a foundation for community-based history and oral history collection .

## Notable Publications

In addition to his 1932 book on Lettow-Vorbeck's soldiers, Dobbertin published or illustrated several other works:



1. "Hermann Löns, Grün ist die Heide. Eine Auswahl von 25 der besten Novellen, Jagd-, Tier- und Naturgeschichten. Mit 112 Lichtbildern aus dem gleichnamigen Film und Nachaufnahmen von Walther Dobbertin" (Hannover 1932)

2. "Alfred Funke, Schwarz-Weiß-Rot über Ostafrika. Roman. Mit 126 Lichtbildern von Walter Dobbertin" (Hannover 1933)

## Conclusion

Walther Dobbertin's work as a photographer provides an extensive visual record of German colonialism in East Africa during a crucial period of history. His documentation of military campaigns, daily life, and landscapes offers valuable insights into the colonial experience from a European perspective. While his Nazi-era activities must be acknowledged, his photographic legacy remains significant for historical research and contemporary understanding of German colonial history.

His photographs are preserved in the Bundesarchiv and continue to be studied by historians, anthropologists, and photographers. The high technical quality of his work, combined with its historical importance, ensures his place as a significant figure in the documentation of German colonialism in East Africa.
