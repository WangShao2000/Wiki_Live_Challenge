# Archaeology of North Korea

## 1. Introduction

The archaeology of North Korea represents the scientific study of human activity in the northern half of the Korean Peninsula from prehistoric times to the present day. This field encompasses the investigation of ancient settlements, burial sites, historical monuments, and cultural artifacts that provide insights into the development of Korean civilization [1]. As one of the most politically isolated nations in the world, North Korea presents unique challenges and opportunities for archaeological research, with limited international access creating both obstacles to academic collaboration and the preservation of sites from modern development pressures [2].

The Korean Peninsula has a long archaeological history spanning over 600,000 years, from the earliest evidence of human occupation in the Paleolithic period to the sophisticated kingdoms of the medieval era [3]. North Korea's territory contains numerous significant archaeological sites, including the World Heritage-listed Complex of Goguryeo Tombs and Historic Monuments and Sites in Kaesong, which demonstrate the region's importance in East Asian history [4].

Despite the challenges, North Korean archaeology has produced substantial research 成果，with institutions such as the Institute of Archaeology at the Academy of Social Sciences and the Faculty of History at Kim Il Sung University conducting excavations and publishing academic work . The primary academic journal, "Joseon gogo yeongu" (Joseon Archaeological Research), has been published quarterly since 1986 and contains over 1,600 articles covering various periods of Korean history [5].

## 2. Historical Development of North Korean Archaeology

### 2.1 Early Period (1945-1960s)

The development of archaeology in North Korea began shortly after the country's establishment in 1945. In 1946, the "Law on the Preservation of Treasures, Relics, and Natural Monuments" was promulgated, establishing a national framework for cultural heritage protection [6]. This was followed by the creation of provincial "Relic Preservation Committees" in 1947 and the Central "North Korean Relic Preservation Committee" [6].

The first systematic archaeological investigations in North Korea focused on overcoming colonial perspectives and establishing a national historical narrative. Archaeologists concentrated on prehistoric sites and remains related to Gojoseon, the first Korean kingdom, which had been neglected during the Japanese colonial period (1910-1945) [6]. The adoption of Marxist-Leninist methodology influenced research approaches, emphasizing the scientific study of ancient and medieval remains to demonstrate the legitimacy of Korea's historical development [6].

A significant breakthrough occurred in 1963 with the discovery of Paleolithic lithic artifacts at the Gul Pori site, marking the first known Paleolithic site in Korea . This discovery fundamentally altered understanding of early human occupation on the Korean Peninsula and established the foundation for subsequent Paleolithic research.

### 2.2 Political Transformation (1970s-1990s)

The 1970s marked a critical turning point in North Korean archaeology as the field became increasingly politicized. Unlike South Korea, which experienced rapid academic advancement through large-scale excavations, North Korean archaeology began to demonstrate a markedly political character [7]. This transformation was particularly evident in the interpretation of Bronze Age and Iron Age sites associated with Gojoseon.

In the 1990s, North Korea declared the "Daedong River Culture," which represented a major ideological shift in archaeological interpretation [7]. This concept emphasized the cultural superiority and continuity of the Daedong River region, particularly around Pyongyang, and became a central tenet of North Korean historical narrative. The declaration of this culture created a complete divergence between North and South Korean archaeological interpretations, with the two traditions sharing almost no common perspectives afterward [7].

The most controversial archaeological event of this period was the 1993-1994 discovery and excavation of the alleged Tomb of Dangun, the mythical founder of Korea who according to legend established Gojoseon in 2333 BCE [8]. In January 1993, Kim Il Sung instructed officials to discover and restore the tomb, stating that it was necessary to "correct the history of Tangun and ancient Korea which had been distorted by the Japanese imperialists" [9]. The excavation reportedly yielded human remains dated to approximately 5,000 years ago, along with Goguryeo-style artifacts [8]. However, the international archaeological community has largely rejected these findings due to methodological concerns and the refusal to share detailed excavation data .

### 2.3 Contemporary Era (2000s-present)

Since the 2000s, North Korean archaeology has continued to develop within its unique political framework while maintaining some degree of international engagement. The publication of "Joseon gogo yeongu" has continued uninterrupted since 1986, with bibliometric analysis revealing the emergence of key researchers such as Seo Guktae (specializing in Neolithic and Bronze Age) and Jang Woojin (specializing in Paleolithic and human evolution) .

International recognition of North Korean archaeological sites has grown with the inscription of three World Heritage Sites: the Complex of Goguryeo Tombs in 2004, Historic Monuments and Sites in Kaesong in 2013, and Mount Kumgang in 2025 . These designations acknowledge the exceptional universal value of these sites while providing a framework for international cooperation in their preservation.

Despite political constraints, there have been limited instances of North-South Korean archaeological cooperation. The most notable example was the joint excavation of the Goryeo palace remains in Kaesong from 2007, conducted in preparation for the World Heritage nomination . This collaboration demonstrated the potential for archaeological cooperation across the Korean divide, though such opportunities remain rare.

## 3. Archaeological Periods and Sites

### 3.1 Paleolithic Period

The Paleolithic period in North Korea extends from approximately 600,000 years ago to around 10,000 years ago, representing the earliest evidence of human occupation on the Korean Peninsula . The most significant early discovery was the Heukyu-ri site in Pyeongyang's Sariwon County, found in 1966, which dates to 600,000-400,000 years ago and represents the earliest known Paleolithic site in Korea [10].

Major Paleolithic sites in North Korea include:

**Komun Moru Cave** in South Hwanghae Province near Pyongyang is one of the most important Paleolithic sites. Excavated between 1966 and 1970, the cave contains stone implements including hand axes, trapezoidal tools, and edged chisels, along with fossil bones of 29 animal species, 17 of which are extinct [11]. North Korean archaeologists date the site to approximately one million years ago, though international scholars consider the supporting evidence to be limited .

**Chongphadae Cave** in North Hwanghae Province has produced important evidence for human occupation during the late Middle and Late Pleistocene periods. Layers 12 and 13 of the cave contain human fossils, fireplaces, numerous lithic artifacts, and mammal remains, representing new evidence for paleoenvironment and human activities during the late MIS 3 and MIS 2 periods [12]. The site suggests that the central Korean Peninsula may have served as a refugium during glacial periods .

**Ryonggok Cave** near Pyongyang is perhaps the most important Pleistocene hominin fossil site in North Korea [13]. The site contains remains of at least five individuals, with two fairly well-preserved crania (designated #3 and #7) . Analysis suggests these remains represent early modern humans, with the earliest cultural deposits dating to older than 40,000 years ago .

Human fossils discovered in North Korea include the "Ryokpo Man," "Tokchon Man," and "Hwadae Man" from the Middle Paleolithic period, as well as "Seungnisan Man," "Mandal Man," and "Hwangju Man" from the Late Paleolithic [11]. These remains provide evidence for the anthropological succession from early humans to Paleoanthropic and Neanthropic humans in Korea [11].

### 3.2 Neolithic Period

The Neolithic period in Korea began around 10,000-8,000 BCE and is characterized by the appearance of pottery and microlithic tools [14]. In North Korea, the Neolithic is particularly associated with comb-patterned pottery (Chulmun), which appears around 3000 BCE [15].

Major Neolithic sites in North Korea include:

**Sop'ohang Shell Mound** is one of the most extensively studied Neolithic sites in North Korea. Located on the west coast, the shell mound contains evidence of early sedentary communities and provides insights into subsistence strategies, including fishing and shellfish gathering [16].

**Guryongpo Site** in South Hamgyong Province has produced evidence of Neolithic settlement patterns and pottery styles. The site demonstrates the transition from hunter-gatherer to agricultural societies in the region [14].

The Neolithic period witnessed the development of increasingly sophisticated pottery traditions, with Chulmun pottery characterized by brownish-gray ware decorated with engraved oblique lines [15]. These ceramics are found in lake and shoreline sites, though during the later Neolithic period they also appear inland, suggesting changes in subsistence strategies [15].

### 3.3 Bronze Age

The Bronze Age in Korea is conventionally dated from around 1500-1000 BCE, though recent archaeological evidence suggests it might have started as early as 2500 BCE [17]. The transition from Neolithic to Bronze Age began around 2300 BCE and continued over 800-900 years [18].

North Korean Bronze Age archaeology has been significantly influenced by political narratives, particularly regarding the location of Gojoseon, the first Korean kingdom. In the 1960s, Lee Jirin developed the "Liaodong theory" proposing Gojoseon's location in present-day China, but following political pressure, North Korean archaeologists later developed the "Pyongyang theory," which places Gojoseon's capital in Pyongyang [19].

Important Bronze Age sites include:

**Chongam-ri Earthen Castle** near Pyongyang is a fortification thought to date to the Gojoseon period. The site is similar to other earthen fortifications of the period and provides evidence for early state formation .

**Pyo-dae Site** in the Daedong River basin has revealed important stratigraphic sequences for Bronze Age chronology. The site demonstrates changes in dwelling architecture and pottery styles over time .

The Bronze Age saw the development of complex social structures and the emergence of organized settlements. Mumun pottery culture (1500-300 BCE) is associated with early Gojoseon society, with excavated walled towns and fortifications suggesting organized settlements [20].

### 3.4 Iron Age and Ancient Kingdoms

The Iron Age in Korea began around 300 BCE and witnessed the development of sophisticated kingdoms, particularly Gojoseon, which is traditionally dated from 2333 BCE to 108 BCE . The kingdom is said to have been founded by Dangun Wanggeom according to Korean mythology, though the historical existence and location of Gojoseon remain subjects of scholarly debate .

#### 3.4.1 Gojoseon

Gojoseon is considered the first kingdom on the Korean Peninsula, though its historical reality and geographical extent are contested . According to Korean tradition, the kingdom was established by the legendary King Dangun in 2333 BCE .

North Korean archaeology has developed a particular interpretation of Gojoseon's history, emphasizing Pyongyang's role as the kingdom's center. The "Pyongyang theory" proposes that Gojoseon's capital Wanggeom-seong was located in present-day Pyongyang, with a secondary capital in the Liaodong Peninsula [19]. This theory was developed to reconcile historical records of Han Dynasty invasions with the political imperative to maintain that Pyongyang was never occupied by China [19].

#### 3.4.2 Goguryeo

Goguryeo (37 BCE - 668 CE) was one of the Three Kingdoms of Korea and the most powerful kingdom in Northeast Asia during its height. The kingdom's territory included parts of present-day China and Korea, with its capital initially in what is now North Korea before being moved to Pyongyang in 427 CE [21].

North Korea contains numerous important Goguryeo sites:

**Complex of Goguryeo Tombs** (inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2004) consists of 63 individual tombs from the later Goguryeo kingdom, located in the cities of Pyongyang and Namp'o [22]. The site includes the Tomb of King Tongmyong and the Anak Tomb No. 3, which contains well-preserved murals depicting daily life and mythology [22].

**Pyongyang City Walls** (National Treasure No. 1 of North Korea) were first constructed during the Goguryeo period and rebuilt during the Goryeo and Joseon periods [23]. The walls represent a combination of plain and mountain fortress styles, with the outer walls spanning 16 km and the inner walls extending 23 km [23].

**Goguryeo Palace Sites** in Pyongyang have been extensively excavated. From 1991 to 2020, North Korean archaeologists unearthed and identified historical sites and relics dating to the Goguryeo period in the Pyongyang area, confirming that Goguryeo regarded the area as a major regional base from the 1st century [24].

#### 3.4.3 Balhae

Balhae (698-926 CE) was established by former Goguryeo nobles after Goguryeo's fall to the Silla-Tang alliance. The kingdom is considered by North Korean historiography to be the successor to Goguryeo and an important part of Korean history [25].

North Korean archaeological sites related to Balhae include:

**Cheonghae Earthen Castle** and **Cheongjin Bugori area ruins** in North Korea are among the 261 Balhae cultural heritage sites identified in the country [25]. These sites demonstrate Balhae's territorial extent and cultural influence in the region.

The study of Balhae in North Korea has become politically significant, with the history of Balhae now an object of active political games related to territorial claims based on alleged archaeological and historical material [26].

### 3.5 Medieval and Later Periods

#### 3.5.1 Goryeo Dynasty

The Goryeo Dynasty (918-1392) unified the Korean Peninsula under a single government and established its capital in Kaesong (present-day North Korea). The dynasty's archaeological remains in North Korea are particularly well-preserved and significant.

**Historic Monuments and Sites in Kaesong** (inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2013) consists of 12 separate components that together testify to the history and culture of the Goryeo Dynasty from the 10th to 14th centuries [4]. The site includes:



* **Manwoldae Palace** archaeological site - the main palace of the Goryeo Dynasty, completely destroyed during the Red Turban invasions of the 14th century [27]

* **Kaesong City Walls** - a triple-walled defense system including the Palocham Wall (896 CE), Outer Wall (1009-1029), and Inner Wall (1391-1393)

* **Kaesong Chomsongdae** - an astronomical and meteorological observatory

* **Koryo Songgyungwan** - a former high state education institute that educated Goryeo national officials

* **Royal Tombs** - including the Mausoleum of King Wang Kon (founder of Goryeo) and the Mausoleum of King Gongmin

The site embodies the political, cultural, philosophical, and spiritual values of the Goryeo period, particularly the transition from Buddhism to Neo-Confucianism in East Asia .

#### 3.5.2 Joseon Dynasty

The Joseon Dynasty (1392-1910) established its capital in Seoul but maintained Pyongyang as an important provincial center. Archaeological remains from this period in North Korea include:

**Pyongyang during Joseon** served as the provincial capital of Pyeongan Province . The city's historical monuments include reconstructed gates and walls from the Joseon period, though many were damaged during the Korean War and subsequently rebuilt.

**Military Sites** from the Imjin War (1592-1598) when Japan invaded Korea are scattered throughout North Korea. These include fortress remains and defensive structures that 见证了 the Korean resistance against Japanese invasion.

### 3.6 Modern Period and the Korean War

The Korean War (1950-1953) had a devastating impact on archaeological sites and cultural heritage in North Korea. Many historical buildings and artifacts were destroyed during the conflict [28].

Archaeological evidence of the Korean War includes:

**Demilitarized Zone (DMZ)** excavations have recovered over 20,000 artifacts, including American body armor, Chinese gas masks, French dog tags, and hundreds of bone fragments [29]. These finds provide insights into the multinational nature of the conflict and the human cost of the war.

**War Damage Sites** such as the Twin Column Tomb, where the DPRK alleged that US forces used the tomb "to lock up and torture our innocent civilians, during which the frescos were ruthlessly destroyed" . Many historical buildings were destroyed during bombings and have been rebuilt, such as the Paeksang Pavilion, which was destroyed during Korean War bombings and rebuilt afterward .

## 4. Archaeological Institutions and Research

### 4.1 Major Institutions

North Korea has established a comprehensive system of archaeological institutions that conduct research, manage cultural heritage, and publish academic work. The two primary organizations are:

**Institute of Archaeology, Academy of Social Sciences** (established in 1954) is the most important archaeological research institution in North Korea [30]. The institute publishes "Joseon gogo yeongu" (Joseon Archaeological Research), which has been published quarterly without interruption since 1986 [5]. The journal contains 1,656 articles between 1986 and 2019, categorized into eight types: research articles, site reports, lectures, data, news, knowledge, political rhetoric, and guidelines .

**Faculty of History, Kim Il Sung University** (founded in 1946) is North Korea's first university and offers degrees in archaeology . The university has approximately 16,000 students and 2,800 faculty members . The Human Evolution and Development History Research Group within the Faculty of History has conducted important research on Paleolithic cave sites .

Other important institutions include:



* **National Bureau for Cultural Property Conservation (NBCPC)** - responsible for implementing the Law on the Protection of Cultural Property (1994) and its Regulations (2009)

* **Korean Cultural Preservation Centre (KCPC)** - authorized by NBCPC to prepare management plans for cultural heritage sites

* **Provincial Cultural Heritage Preservation Committees** - established in each province to manage local archaeological sites and cultural properties [6]

### 4.2 Academic Publications and Research Output

North Korean archaeological research is primarily disseminated through academic journals and publications. The most important publication is:

**"Joseon gogo yeongu" (Joseon Archaeological Research)** has been published quarterly by the Institute of Archaeology since 1986 and is considered North Korea's preeminent archaeology journal [5]. Analysis of this journal from 1986 to 2019 reveals several key findings:



* Research articles comprise approximately two-thirds of published content (1,062 articles)

* Site reports account for 252 articles

* Key researchers include Seo Guktae (specializing in Neolithic and Bronze Age) and Jang Woojin (specializing in Paleolithic and human evolution), who have each published over 30 research articles

* Kim Incheol is the third most active researcher with 25 research articles and has produced the greatest number of site reports (14)

Other important publications include:



* **"Yoksa kwahak" (Historical Sciences)** - began publication in 1955 and carries interpretative articles, chiefly by To Yuho, director of the Center [31]

* **"North Korean Periodicals Archaeology Literature Catalogue"** - lists approximately 4,300 academic papers from eight different publications covering archaeology and cultural heritage [32]

* **"Overview of Korean Archaeology"** - published by the Institute of Archaeology in 1983, providing a comprehensive survey of North Korean archaeological research [33]

### 4.3 Research Methods and Techniques

North Korean archaeology employs various research methods and techniques, though the degree of international standardization is difficult to assess due to limited access to research facilities and methods.

**Excavation Techniques** are conducted by teams from the Institute of Archaeology and Kim Il Sung University. Major excavations have included:



* The Goguryeo palace sites in Pyongyang (1991-2020) [24]

* The Goryeo palace remains in Kaesong (joint North-South excavation, 2007)

* Various Paleolithic cave sites, including Chongphadae Cave and Ryonggok Cave [34]

**Dating Methods** used in North Korean archaeology include:



* Radiocarbon dating - used for organic materials [18]

* Electron Spin Resonance (ESR) dating - notably used controversially on the Dangun tomb remains [8]

* Stratigraphic analysis - particularly important for establishing chronological sequences at sites like Pyo-dae

**Conservation and Preservation** efforts are governed by the Law on the Protection of Cultural Property (1994) and its Regulations (2009) . The management plan for the Historic Monuments and Sites in Kaesong, prepared by KCPC and approved by the DPRK government in 2011, includes 5 and 10-year objectives .

### 4.4 International Collaboration and Challenges

International collaboration in North Korean archaeology faces significant challenges due to political isolation, economic sanctions, and restricted access. However, some limited cooperation has occurred:

**North-South Korean Collaboration** has been rare but significant when it occurs. The joint excavation of the Goryeo palace remains in Kaesong (2007) was conducted to prepare for the World Heritage nomination . This collaboration demonstrated the potential for archaeological cooperation despite political tensions.

**China Collaboration** has been more frequent, given geographical proximity and political relations. In the 1960s, a rare joint Chinese-North Korean archaeological excavation took place in China's Liaoning Province at a massive Gojoseon royal necropolis [35]. More recently, Chinese archaeologists have visited North Korea for academic exchanges, such as the 2019 visit by a delegation from the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences [30].

**UNESCO Engagement** has provided a framework for international recognition and cooperation. North Korea acceded to the World Heritage Convention on July 21, 1998 [36], and has since had three sites inscribed:



* Complex of Goguryeo Tombs (2004)

* Historic Monuments and Sites in Kaesong (2013) [4]

* Mount Kumgang (2025)

The World Heritage designation requires 缔约国 to submit regular reports on the state of conservation, providing a mechanism for international monitoring .

**Challenges to International Collaboration** include:



* **Sanctions Impact**: International sanctions have limited North Korea's access to global markets and academic resources [37]

* **Restricted Access**: Foreign archaeologists face significant barriers to conducting fieldwork in North Korea [2]

* **Information Sharing**: North Korea has been reluctant to share detailed excavation data and methods, as seen in the Dangun tomb controversy [38]

* **Political Interference**: Archaeological interpretations are often influenced by political narratives, making objective scientific collaboration difficult [7]

## 5. Cultural Heritage Protection and World Heritage Sites

### 5.1 Legal Framework

North Korea has established a comprehensive legal framework for cultural heritage protection. The primary legislation is the **Law on the Protection of Cultural Property** (1994), which was amended in 2013 . This law is implemented through the **Regulations on the Implementation of the Law on the Protection of Cultural Property** (2009) .

Key elements of the legal framework include:



* **National Level Protection**: All property components of the World Heritage sites are protected at the national level

* **Buffer Zone Protection**: The mountains and forests in buffer zones are protected by the Law on Environmental Protection (1986) and the Forest Law (1992)

* **Urban Planning Controls**: Urban land within buffer zones is administered under the Land Law (1977) and the Law on City Management (1992)

* **Management Guidelines**: Specific guidelines for protection and management of the Historic Monuments and Sites in Kaesong were prepared and approved in September 2013

### 5.2 World Heritage Sites

North Korea currently has three sites inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List:

#### 5.2.1 Complex of Goguryeo Tombs (2004)

The Complex of Goguryeo Tombs was inscribed as North Korea's first World Heritage Site in July 2004 [39]. The site meets UNESCO criteria (i), (ii), (iii), and (iv) and covers 233 hectares with a buffer zone of 1,701 hectares [40].

**Description**:

The site consists of 63 individual tombs from the later Goguryeo kingdom, located in the cities of Pyongyang and Namp'o [22]. The tombs are particularly renowned for their:



* **Mural Paintings**: Considered masterpieces of the Goguryeo period, the murals provide unique perspectives on daily life, mythology, and court culture [22]

* **Architectural Ingenuity**: The tombs demonstrate ingenious engineering capabilities, with some featuring sophisticated ceiling designs that require no supports [22]

* **Historical Significance**: The site provides exceptional testimony to the Goguryeo civilization, which ruled parts of China and Korea from 37 BCE to the 7th century CE [21]

**Major Components**:



* Tomb of King Tongmyong - the alleged founder of Goguryeo [40]

* Anak Tomb No. 3 - famous for its well-preserved murals depicting hunting scenes, servants, and astronomical phenomena [22]

* Twin Column Tomb - notable for its architectural features

* Group of tombs at Jinpha-ri

#### 5.2.2 Historic Monuments and Sites in Kaesong (2013)

The Historic Monuments and Sites in Kaesong were inscribed as a World Heritage Site during the 37th session of the World Heritage Committee in Phnom Penh in June 2013 [4]. The site meets criteria (ii) and (iii) and covers 494.2 hectares with a buffer zone of 5,222.1 hectares .

**Description**:

The site consists of 12 separate components that together testify to the history and culture of the Goryeo Dynasty (918-1392) [4]. The site embodies the political, cultural, philosophical, and spiritual values of the Goryeo period, particularly the transition from Buddhism to Neo-Confucianism in East Asia .

**Key Components**:



* **Manwoldae Palace Site** - the main palace of the Goryeo Dynasty, completely destroyed during the Red Turban invasions [27]

* **Kaesong City Walls** - a triple-walled defense system including:


  * Palocham Wall (896 CE) - the innermost wall [41]

  * Outer Wall (1009-1029) - connecting mountains for geomantic protection

  * Inner Wall (1391-1393)

* **Kaesong Chomsongdae** - an astronomical and meteorological observatory

* **Koryo Songgyungwan** - a former national academy that educated Goryeo officials

* **Sungyang Sowon** - a Confucian private school

* **Royal Tombs** - including the Mausoleum of King Wang Kon and the Mausoleum of King Gongmin

#### 5.2.3 Mount Kumgang (2025)

Mount Kumgang (Diamond Mountain from the Sea) was inscribed as North Korea's third World Heritage Site in 2025 . This natural heritage site is known for its scenic beauty and cultural significance.

### 5.3 Challenges in Heritage Protection

North Korean cultural heritage faces numerous challenges in protection and preservation:

**War Damage**: The Korean War caused extensive damage to historical buildings and archaeological sites. Many structures, such as the Paeksang Pavilion, were destroyed during bombings and have been rebuilt . The National Museum of Korea exhibition "6·25 War and the National Museum" displays damaged artifacts including a Joseon military map with North Korean military boot prints and broken ceramics [28].

**Development Pressure**: As North Korea develops its infrastructure, there is concern that archaeological sites may be damaged or destroyed. The proposed economic cooperation projects between North and South Korea have raised concerns about potential impacts on cultural heritage [42].

**Lack of International Cooperation**: The isolation of North Korea has limited international assistance for heritage preservation. The country's cultural heritage protection efforts have long been in a closed state, with few cooperative projects with UNESCO, the World Bank, and other international institutions [2].

**Political Influence on Interpretation**: Archaeological sites are often subject to political narratives that may compromise their historical integrity. The Dangun tomb controversy exemplifies how political considerations can influence archaeological interpretation and public presentation of findings [8].

**Climate and Environmental Factors**: Natural disasters and environmental degradation pose ongoing threats to archaeological sites. Some sites, such as the petroglyphs at Bangu-dae, require protection from weathering and erosion.

## 6. Notable Archaeological Discoveries

### 6.1 Prehistoric Discoveries

North Korean archaeology has made several significant prehistoric discoveries that have contributed to understanding human evolution and early Korean civilization:

**Paleolithic Discoveries**:



* The **Heukyu-ri site** (1966) in Pyeongyang's Sariwon County is the earliest known Paleolithic site in Korea, dating to 600,000-400,000 years ago [10]

* **Komun Moru Cave** has produced stone implements and fossil bones dating to approximately one million years ago, though this dating is contested

* **Chongphadae Cave** has revealed human fossils, fireplaces, and lithic artifacts from the late MIS 3 and MIS 2 periods, suggesting the Korean Peninsula served as a refugium during glacial periods [12]

* **Ryonggok Cave** contains remains of at least five early modern humans, with cultural deposits dating to over 40,000 years ago

**Neolithic Discoveries**:



* **Sop'ohang Shell Mound** provides evidence of early sedentary communities and subsistence strategies [16]

* Neolithic pottery traditions, particularly comb-patterned (Chulmun) pottery, have been documented across North Korea [15]

**Bronze Age Discoveries**:



* The transition from Neolithic to Bronze Age began around 2300 BCE and continued over 800-900 years [18]

* Mumun pottery culture (1500-300 BCE) associated with early Gojoseon society has been identified at sites like Chongam-ri [20]

### 6.2 Ancient Kingdom Discoveries

**Goguryeo Discoveries**:



* The **Complex of Goguryeo Tombs** includes 63 tombs with exceptional mural paintings that provide unique insights into Goguryeo court life, mythology, and daily activities [22]

* **Pyongyang City Walls** (National Treasure No. 1) demonstrate the sophisticated fortress architecture of the Goguryeo period [23]

* Extensive palace excavations in Pyongyang (1991-2020) have confirmed Goguryeo's long-standing occupation of the area [24]

**Balhae Discoveries**:



* North Korea has identified 261 Balhae cultural heritage sites, including Cheonghae Earthen Castle and Cheongjin Bugori area ruins [25]

* These sites demonstrate Balhae's territorial extent and cultural connections to Goguryeo

**Goryeo Discoveries**:



* **Manwoldae Palace** in Kaesong, though destroyed in the 14th century, has been extensively excavated and provides insights into Goryeo court architecture and administration [27]

* The **Royal Tombs of Goryeo** include the mausoleums of King Wang Kon (founder) and King Gongmin, demonstrating the dynasty's sophisticated burial traditions

* **Kaesong City** as a whole represents a well-preserved medieval Korean capital with its triple-walled defense system and administrative buildings

### 6.3 Recent Important Finds

Recent archaeological discoveries in North Korea include:

**Buddhist Artifacts**: In 2025, North Korean archaeologists unearthed a 7-meter-tall Buddha statue at an archaeological site in Yak Jon-ri, South Pyongan Province. The statue was discovered by the Korean Archaeology Institute of the Academy of Social Sciences and the National Authority for the Protection of Cultural Heritage [43].

**Paleolithic Finds in Southwest North Korea**: In 2019, faculty and researchers from Kim Il Sung University discovered Paleolithic remains including five stone tools, three bone tools, and 732 fossil bones near the Ryesong River [44].

**Goryeo Era Artifacts**: At Manwoldae Palace, numerous valuable relics from the late Goryeo period have been uncovered, including ceramics, metal items, ancestral tablets, mirrors, and over 3,000 roofing tiles .

## 7. Contemporary Issues and Controversies

### 7.1 Political Influence on Archaeological Interpretation

North Korean archaeology is significantly influenced by political narratives, creating challenges for objective scientific research and international credibility.

**The Dangun Tomb Controversy** represents the most significant example of political interference in North Korean archaeology. In 1993-1994, North Korea claimed to have discovered and excavated the Tomb of Dangun, the mythical founder of Korea who is said to have established Gojoseon in 2333 BCE [8]. The excavation yielded human remains dated to approximately 5,000 years ago using electron spin resonance (ESR) dating [8]. However, this dating method was controversial as ESR is typically used for dating materials over 100,000 years old, not 5,000-year-old remains .

International archaeologists have raised several concerns:



* The refusal to share detailed excavation data and measurement methods [38]

* The political timing of the discovery, coming shortly before Kim Il Sung's death in 1994 [45]

* The association of the tomb with the "Pyongyang theory" of Gojoseon's location, which serves political objectives [19]

**The Daedong River Culture Theory** (declared in the 1990s) represents another example of politically motivated archaeological interpretation. This theory emphasizes the cultural superiority and continuity of the Daedong River region around Pyongyang, creating a complete divergence from South Korean archaeological interpretations [7].

**Balhae Archaeology and Territorial Claims** has become an object of "active political games which are related to territorial claims based on alleged archaeological and historical material" [26]. The interpretation of Balhae history serves both historical and contemporary political purposes.

### 7.2 International Perception and Academic Freedom

North Korean archaeology faces significant challenges in terms of international recognition and academic freedom:

**Academic Freedom Constraints**: The North Korean government severely restricts academic freedom, controlling artistic works and curriculum [46]. The government has restricted academic travel and maintains tight control over all media [47].

**Information Sharing Issues**: North Korea's reluctance to share detailed excavation data and methods has damaged its credibility in the international archaeological community. The Dangun tomb controversy exemplifies this problem, where North Korea refused to disclose excavation data and measurement methods requested by the archaeological community [38].

**Bias in Interpretation**: South Korean archaeologists view North Korean archaeological claims with what they term a "dual perspective" - recognizing the importance of North Korean data while questioning its reliability due to political influence [7].

**Limited International Engagement**: The isolation of North Korea has limited its participation in international archaeological conferences, collaborative research projects, and data sharing initiatives.

### 7.3 Impact of Sanctions and Isolation

International sanctions have had a significant impact on North Korean archaeology:

**Economic Impact**: Sanctions have limited North Korea's access to global markets and academic resources [37]. This has affected the country's ability to purchase modern archaeological equipment, analytical tools, and conservation materials.

**Collaboration Restrictions**: Sanctions have made international academic collaboration difficult, limiting North Korean archaeologists' participation in international conferences, joint research projects, and exchange programs.

**Technology Access**: Restrictions on technology transfer have limited North Korea's ability to adopt modern archaeological techniques and analytical methods.

**Funding Limitations**: The sanctions regime has constrained government funding for archaeological research and site preservation, potentially threatening the long-term conservation of cultural heritage.

### 7.4 Preservation Challenges

North Korean cultural heritage faces multiple preservation challenges:

**War Damage and Reconstruction**: Many historical buildings were destroyed during the Korean War and have been rebuilt, sometimes without complete archaeological documentation of the original structures .

**Development Pressure**: Proposed infrastructure development and economic cooperation projects raise concerns about potential damage to archaeological sites [42].

**Environmental Threats**: Natural disasters, erosion, and climate change pose ongoing threats to archaeological sites and artifacts.

**Lack of Resources**: Limited funding and technical resources make it difficult to implement modern conservation techniques and protective measures.

**Isolation from International Best Practices**: Limited international engagement means North Korea has been unable to benefit from global advances in archaeological preservation and conservation techniques.

## 8. Conclusion

The archaeology of North Korea represents a unique field of study that combines significant scientific potential with complex political challenges. The country's territory contains numerous sites of exceptional importance for understanding Korean and East Asian history, including three UNESCO World Heritage Sites: the Complex of Goguryeo Tombs, Historic Monuments and Sites in Kaesong, and Mount Kumgang.

North Korean archaeology has produced substantial research 成果，with institutions like the Institute of Archaeology and Kim Il Sung University conducting excavations and publishing academic work through journals like "Joseon gogo yeongu." The field has made important contributions to understanding human evolution in East Asia, early Korean civilization, and the development of ancient kingdoms like Goguryeo and Goryeo.

However, the discipline faces significant challenges. The politicization of archaeological interpretation, particularly evident in the Dangun tomb controversy and the Daedong River Culture theory, has created tensions between scientific objectivity and political narrative. The isolation of North Korea has limited international collaboration, information sharing, and access to modern archaeological techniques. International sanctions have further constrained resources and cooperation opportunities.

Looking forward, the future of North Korean archaeology will likely depend on several factors:



1. **Political Developments**: Improvements in North-South relations and international engagement could open new opportunities for collaborative research and information sharing.

2. **Technological Access**: Greater access to modern archaeological tools and techniques could enhance the quality and credibility of North Korean research.

3. **Heritage Protection**: Continued efforts to protect and preserve North Korea's rich cultural heritage will be essential, particularly as development pressures increase.

4. **Scientific Standards**: Adherence to international scientific standards and greater transparency in research methods and results will be crucial for building international credibility.

The archaeology of North Korea remains a field of great potential that could significantly advance our understanding of Korean and East Asian history. However, realizing this potential will require a balance between academic freedom, political realities, and international cooperation. As the Korean Peninsula continues to evolve politically, the archaeological record stands as a shared heritage that could one day serve as a bridge for reunification of knowledge and understanding.

The field's challenges also offer important lessons for the broader archaeological community about the intersection of politics and science, the importance of academic freedom, and the universal value of cultural heritage. As international cooperation gradually increases, there is hope that North Korean archaeology will emerge as a fully collaborative and respected discipline within the global archaeological community.
