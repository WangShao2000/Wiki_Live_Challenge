# Endorois case

**Centre for Minority Rights Development (Kenya) and Minority Rights Group International on behalf of Endorois Welfare Council v. Kenya**, commonly known as the **Endorois case**, was a landmark decision by the African Commission on Human and Peoples' Rights (ACHPR) that found the Kenyan government had violated the human rights of the Endorois people through their eviction from ancestral lands around Lake Bogoria . The case established important precedents in African human rights law, being the first time an African institution recognized indigenous peoples' collective rights over traditionally owned land and the first global ruling on the right to development [1].

## Background

The Endorois are a semi-nomadic indigenous community of approximately 60,000 people who have lived in the Lake Bogoria area of Kenya's Rift Valley for more than 300 years . They are a subgroup of the Tugen people, who are part of the larger Kalenjin ethnic group . The Endorois have a deep spiritual and cultural connection to Lake Bogoria and the surrounding Mochongoi forest, which serve as important sites for religious ceremonies and traditional practices .

In 1973, without adequate consultation or compensation, the Kenyan government forcibly evicted the Endorois community from their land to create the Lake Bogoria Game Reserve, which was completed in 1978 [2]. The government's stated purpose was to establish a national park and tourist facilities [3]. During the eviction process, the Kenyan Wildlife Service promised 3,150 Kenyan Shillings (approximately 30 British pounds at the time) per family for resettlement, but only 170 of the affected 400 families allegedly received compensation [4].

The Endorois community was relocated to semi-arid land, causing significant hardship. Many of their cattle died due to lack of proper grazing land, and they lost access to ancestral spiritual sites essential to their cultural identity [1]. Despite multiple attempts to seek redress through Kenyan authorities, including appeals to then-President Daniel Arap Moi, the community received no satisfaction [4].

## Legal proceedings

### Domestic litigation

In 2000, the Endorois community took their case to the Kenyan High Court, arguing for recognition of their customary land rights . However, in 2002, the High Court ruled against them, finding that although the Endorois were former bona fide occupants of the land, their customary claim had been extinguished by the 1973 and 1978 gazettement of the land as a game reserve . The court also stated that it could not address collective property rights and viewed Lake Bogoria as a national resource from which communities could not claim revenue .

### African Commission case

Having exhausted domestic remedies, the Endorois community, represented by the Centre for Minority Rights Development (CEMIRIDE) and Minority Rights Group International (MRG), filed a complaint with the African Commission on Human and Peoples' Rights in 2003 . The case was registered as Communication No. 276/2003 [5].

The complainants alleged violations of several provisions of the African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights:



* **Article 8** (freedom of religion) - through denial of access to sacred sites [1]

* **Article 14** (right to property) - through expropriation without compensation [1]

* **Article 17** (right to culture) - through threats to their pastoralist way of life [1]

* **Article 21** (right to freely dispose of wealth and natural resources) - through granting of mining concessions without consultation [1]

* **Article 22** (right to development) - through exclusion from development benefits [1]

The Kenyan government argued that the Endorois were not a distinct community but rather one of four clans comprising the Tugen subgroup of the Kalenjin people [4]. The government claimed the Endorois had received compensation and continued to benefit from the game reserve through county council management .

### Commission's decision

The African Commission issued its decision on November 25, 2009, which was formally adopted on February 4, 2010 [6]. The decision was subsequently approved by the African Union at its January 2010 meeting [7].

The Commission made several key findings:



1. **Recognition as indigenous peoples**: The Commission recognized the Endorois as an indigenous people entitled to collective rights under the African Charter [4]. This was significant as it was the first time the ACHPR had extended individual and peoples' rights to indigenous people [8].

2. **Violations of rights**: The Commission found that Kenya had violated all five articles of the African Charter alleged by the complainants :

* The forced eviction violated their right to religious freedom (Article 8) by denying access to sacred sites

* The expropriation violated their right to property (Article 14) as it was not in accordance with "appropriate laws"

* The resettlement to semi-arid land threatened their pastoralist way of life, violating their right to culture (Article 17)

* The granting of mining concessions without consultation violated their right to natural resources (Article 21)

* The failure to consult or compensate violated their right to development (Article 22)

1. **Proportionality test**: The Commission applied a strict proportionality test, finding that the creation of the game reserve was disproportionate to any public need served [1]. The Commission noted that Endorois access to Lake Bogoria for religious and cultural purposes would not harm the ecosystem of the game reserve .

2. **Right to development**: In a historic first, the Commission held that the right to development required meaningful consultation, free prior and informed consent, and benefit-sharing . The Commission stated that the right to development imparted duties on governments to consult with indigenous peoples in a culturally appropriate manner, obtain their informed consent before development projects, and ensure they share in benefits .

## Recommendations and remedies

The African Commission issued comprehensive recommendations for Kenya to remedy the violations [9]:



1. **Land rights recognition**: Kenya should "recognise rights of ownership to the Endorois and restitute Endorois ancestral land" [9]. The Commission specified that the land must first be delimited and demarcated in consultation with the Endorois and neighboring peoples .

2. **Access rights**: Kenya should ensure the Endorois community has unrestricted access to Lake Bogoria and surrounding sites for religious and cultural rites and for grazing their cattle .

3. **Compensation**: Kenya should pay adequate compensation to the community for all losses suffered, including loss of cultural and religious practices over three decades .

4. **Benefit-sharing**: Kenya should pay royalties to the Endorois from existing economic activities and ensure they benefit from employment opportunities within the reserve .

5. **Institutional measures**: Kenya should grant registration to the Endorois Welfare Council and engage in dialogue with the complainants for effective implementation .

6. **Reporting requirement**: Kenya was required to submit a progress report within three months of notification .

## Implementation challenges

Despite the landmark nature of the decision, implementation has been severely delayed and incomplete . As of 2023, the Kenyan government had not fully granted the Endorois ownership of their ancestral land .

### Partial implementation

The only recommendation fully implemented has been the registration of the Endorois Welfare Council . Two other recommendations - regarding payment of royalties and provision of unrestricted access to Lake Bogoria and surrounding areas - have seen only tokenistic implementation .

### Ongoing issues

The non-implementation has had severe consequences for the Endorois community:



* They continue to face extreme poverty, with women and children walking up to 24 kilometers to fetch water

* They cannot afford to buy water, with some spending up to 500 Kenyan Shillings (\$5) per day on water, which many cannot afford

* Their displacement has resulted in poor access to healthcare, clean water, and education

### International pressure

In April 2022, a coalition of organizations including WITNESS, CEMIRIDE, Endorois Welfare Council, and Minority Rights Group International released a report documenting the failure to implement the decision 12 years after it was issued . The report called on the Kenyan government to:



* Establish a new task force on implementation, including Endorois and indigenous rights experts

* Direct the National Land Commission to fully implement the decision

* Develop an inventory of indigenous peoples in Kenya

* Pass benefit-sharing legislation as required by the 2010 Constitution

In February 2024, more than 30 community representatives and activists delivered letters to Kenya's Attorney General demanding implementation of the decision . The organizations noted that after 14 years, the government continued to deny the Endorois return to their ancestral homes and reparations for their forced eviction .

### Broader implications for African human rights system

The Endorois case exemplifies broader challenges in implementing African human rights decisions. According to Mongabay's analysis of the African Court's 2023 activity report, 52 out of 57 applications had no update on implementation as states had not submitted progress reports [10]. The African Court lacks effective mechanisms to enforce compliance, and states rarely submit progress reports .

## Legal significance and precedents

### Indigenous rights recognition

The Endorois case represents a watershed moment in African human rights law. It was the first time an African institution recognized indigenous peoples' collective rights over traditionally owned land [1]. This recognition established important precedents for:



1. **Collective rights framework**: The decision confirmed that indigenous communities could claim collective rights under the African Charter, even when individual litigation would be impractical for large communities [11].

2. **Land rights protection**: The Commission's ruling established that indigenous property rights should be recognized as communal property rights, drawing on jurisprudence from the Inter-American Court of Human Rights .

3. **Cultural rights protection**: The Commission found that culture "manifests itself in many forms, including... ways of life associated with the use of land resources... in the case of indigenous peoples" [12]. This broad interpretation of cultural rights has implications for other indigenous communities.

### Right to development

The case's most significant global contribution was its pioneering interpretation of the right to development. The African Commission's finding that the right to development requires:



* Meaningful consultation in a culturally appropriate manner

* Free, prior and informed consent before development projects

* Benefit-sharing from development activities on indigenous lands

This interpretation has influenced international understanding of the right to development and has been cited in subsequent cases.

### Proportionality standard

The Commission established a high threshold for limitations on indigenous land rights, stating that the "public interest" test is "met with a much higher threshold in the case of encroachment of indigenous land rather than individual private property" [1]. This standard requires that any limitations be "strictly proportionate," "absolutely necessary," and "the least restrictive measures possible" [1].

## Impact on Kenyan law and policy

The Endorois case had a significant impact on Kenya's legal and constitutional development:



1. **Constitutional recognition**: The case generated important debate about indigenous and land rights during the drafting of Kenya's 2010 Constitution . The new Constitution includes provisions recognizing community land rights.

2. **Community Land Act**: The adoption of the Community Land Act in 2016 represents a significant step forward for securing indigenous communities' land rights in Kenya . However, implementation remains problematic.

3. **Free, prior and informed consent**: The case influenced the development of free, prior and informed consent (FPIC) principles in Kenyan law and policy [13]. The concept of meaningful consultation and consent has become a standard requirement for development projects affecting indigenous communities.

## Academic and international reception

The Endorois case has been extensively analyzed in academic literature and has received recognition from international bodies:



1. **UN Special Rapporteur recognition**: The UN Special Rapporteur on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples has cited the case as establishing important principles for indigenous land rights, including the need for delimitation, demarcation, and titling of traditional territories .

2. **Academic analysis**: Scholars have noted that the decision "combined a liberal interpretation of the African Charter with far-reaching recommendations" [8]. However, some critics argue that the decision may reinforce exclusive notions of ethnic territoriality and neglect broader issues of social justice [8].

3. **Precedent value**: The case has been cited as establishing important precedents for:

* Recognizing indigenous peoples' rights in Africa

* Interpreting the right to development

* Applying proportionality tests to government actions affecting indigenous communities

* Establishing standards for benefit-sharing and consultation [14]
