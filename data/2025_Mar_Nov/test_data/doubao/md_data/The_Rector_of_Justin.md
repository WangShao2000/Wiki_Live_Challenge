# The Rector of Justin

## Lead Section

The Rector of Justin is a 1964 psychological fiction novel by American author Louis Auchincloss that examines the complex life and legacy of Francis Prescott, the founder and headmaster of Justin Martyr School, an elite Episcopal boarding school for boys [1]. Regarded as one of Auchincloss's most accomplished novels, the work explores themes of leadership, religious faith, and the moral contradictions inherent in elite education through the perspectives of six narrators who provide contrasting views of Prescott's character and career [2]. Published during a pivotal period in American society, the novel became a commercial and critical success, spending thirty-five weeks on bestseller lists and earning nominations for both the Pulitzer Prize and National Book Award .

## Plot Summary

The novel's narrative structure is unconventional, employing a composite biography approach where eighty years of Francis Prescott's life unfold through the observations of six narrators, each offering a unique perspective on the man, his motivations, and the roots of his triumphs and failings [2]. The frame narrator is Brian Aspinwall, an English instructor at Justin Martyr who has been asked to write a biography of the school's founder .

The story begins in 1939 when Aspinwall, a Columbia and Oxford alumnus, arrives at Justin Martyr to teach English . As World War II looms in Europe, Aspinwall struggles with personal dilemmas, torn between returning to England to join the war effort and pursuing a calling in the clergy, despite his uncertainties about religious life [3]. His arrival coincides with the twilight years of Prescott's long tenure as headmaster, and he quickly becomes fascinated with the aging rector's complex personality.

Through Aspinwall's investigation, the narrative weaves together multiple perspectives on Prescott's life. The first source of information comes from Horace Havistock's manuscript for his unfinished work "The Art of Friendship," which provides the earliest memories of Prescott as Havistock's boyhood friend . Their friendship began at St. Andrews School in Dublin, New Hampshire, in 1876, where the athletic Prescott provided protection for the less physically gifted Havistock .

The second narrator is David Griscam, a wealthy New York lawyer who was a student at Justin Martyr and later became chairman of the school's board of trustees . Griscam's observations span decades, from his time as a student to his later role persuading Prescott to expand the school, doubling its size and considerably expanding its physical plant .

Prescott's three daughters—Harriet Kidder (the eldest), Evelyn Homans (the middle child), and Cordelia Turnbull (the youngest)—provide intimate family perspectives on the man they knew as "the Great Man" . Cordelia's memories are particularly revealing, including a brief interlude with Charley Strong, a disillusioned World War I veteran whose religious life Prescott had saved .

The final narrator is Jules Griscam, David's son, whose memoir provides a contrasting view as someone who was expelled from Justin Martyr, failed at Harvard, and died an alcoholic suicide in the south of France . His story represents the school's failures and the consequences of its rigid standards.

Through these multiple perspectives, the novel reveals Prescott's complex character. He is portrayed as a man of deep religious faith who founded Justin Martyr School in the early 20th century, transforming it from a struggling institution into one of the most respected educational establishments in New England . His dedication to discipline, intellectual precision, and moral guidance earned him admiration, yet his strictness and unwavering commitment to tradition also created controversy .

## Characters

**Francis Prescott** is the central figure of the novel, the founder and rector of Justin Martyr School. Described as charismatic yet autocratic, Prescott embodies both the ideals and contradictions of elite education [4]. He is portrayed as "a man of intellect and idealism who could be noble, generous and kind but also, by turns, cruel, callous and arbitrary" [4]. His religious faith, though sometimes wavering, serves as a central motivating force in establishing and leading the school [5].

**Brian Aspinwall** serves as the frame narrator and primary observer of Prescott's life. Based on Auchincloss's own mentor Malcolm Strachan, Aspinwall is an English teacher who arrives at Justin Martyr in 1939 [6]. He is characterized by his possessive admiration for Prescott, believing that only he truly understands the rector's greatness [7]. However, he also struggles with the ethical dilemma of whether to include unflattering details about his admired mentor in his biography .

**Harriet Winslow Prescott** is Francis Prescott's aristocratic wife and a grandniece of Ralph Waldo Emerson . She struggles with her role as a headmaster's wife, often feeling overshadowed by her husband's devotion to the school [8]. Her intellectual interests, particularly in the works of Henry James, contrast sharply with her husband's more traditional literary tastes .

**Horace Havistock** is Prescott's boyhood friend from St. Andrews School. Based on University of Cambridge legal historian G.T. Lapsley, Havistock is described as effete and less athletic than Prescott [4]. His manuscript provides insight into Prescott's early life and the formative experiences that shaped his educational philosophy.

**David Griscam** is a wealthy New York lawyer and Justin Martyr alumnus who later becomes chairman of the school's board of trustees . His perspective spans Prescott's entire career, from his student days to his later role in persuading Prescott to expand the school, a decision that both increased the school's prestige and compromised its original intimate character .

## Themes

The Rector of Justin explores several interconnected themes that reflect the social and moral tensions of mid-20th century America.

### Religious Faith and Doubt

Religious faith and its complexities form a central theme in the novel. Prescott's sometimes-wavering commitment to Christian faith as a motivating force in establishing and leading the school is a constant concern [5]. The novel examines how religious ideals interact with practical realities in education, revealing the secular erosion of religious precepts in Prescott's behavior .

### Democracy and Elitism

The novel explores the fundamental contradiction between democratic ideals and elitist education. Commitment to both Christian and democratic values is presented as a cornerstone of the school from its earliest days, yet Auchincloss demonstrates how the school's exclusivity effectively contradicts its ability to fulfill its mission . The institution serves as an incubator for the supposed best and brightest who will become the nation's leaders, yet this very exclusivity undermines its democratic pretensions .

### Hypocrisy and Honesty

The relationship between hypocrisy and honesty is primarily explored through the character of Prescott, who continues to pay lip service to the values by which the school was established even as he supports changes that undermine those original principles [9]. The rector supports a highly unequal system, even in the face of obvious prejudice such as gender discrimination against his own daughters .

### Social Change and Class Dynamics

As the novel covers five decades of history, it follows significant changes in American society during the first half of the twentieth century . The author emphasizes class and gender divisions while largely disregarding race. The character of Cordelia, Frank's daughter, exemplifies the changing roles of women among the privileged classes .

### The Erosion of WASP Values

A central theme is the erosion of White Anglo-Saxon Protestant (WASP) self-belief that values instilled at schools like Groton entitled them to leadership roles in the corporate world and public service . The novel reflects Auchincloss's growing disillusionment with WASP society in the 1930s and 1940s, particularly following the public disgrace of Richard Whitney, the aristocratic president of the New York Stock Exchange, whose 1938 embezzlement conviction shocked upper-class society .

## Critical Reception

Upon publication in 1964, The Rector of Justin received generally positive critical reception, with many reviewers praising Auchincloss's sophisticated character development and narrative structure.

Orville Prescott of The New York Times lauded Auchincloss's "deeply human" characterization, stating that the novel demonstrated "emotional power and psychological fascination rarely found in anybody's work" . Leon Edel, writing in Life magazine, declared that the book placed Auchincloss "in the front rank of mid-century American novelists," praising the novel for "subtly taking its legends apart before our eyes" . Time magazine wrote that the novel "finally fulfilled \[Auchincloss's] longtime promise of major distinction as a novelist," noting that "Auchincloss writes in the manner of Henry James, finding great moral dilemmas in small events" .

The novel was recognized with major literary honors, becoming a finalist for both the Pulitzer Prize for Fiction and the National Book Award for Fiction . However, it lost both awards—to Shirley Ann Grau's The Keeper of the House for the Pulitzer and to Saul Bellow's Herzog for the National Book Award . Literary critics have suggested that Auchincloss's focus on WASP society may have contributed to these losses, as the judges may have been averse to his subject matter .

Not all reviews were positive. Robert M. Adams of The New York Review of Books criticized Auchincloss's critique of the boarding school system as "fake criticism" and "afraid to ask the questions that hurt" . Several reviewers, including Edward Weeks of Atlantic Monthly, commented that the six narrators were too similar in voice and perspective . Orville Prescott, while generally laudatory, quipped that "by a happy chance and a familiar literary convention, the six observers are all expert novelists" .

## Commercial Success

The Rector of Justin achieved remarkable commercial success upon publication. It spent thirty-five weeks competing for first place on bestseller lists with Saul Bellow's Herzog . By 1966, the novel had sold approximately 2 million copies [10]. It was ranked as the sixth-best-selling fiction book of 1964, though third-placed Herzog sold considerably more hardcover copies (142,000 to 80,000) [10].

The novel's commercial success helped establish Auchincloss as one of America's most prominent literary figures of the 1960s. It was part of a productive decade for the author, during which he wrote five novels including The Embezzler, Portrait in Brownstone, A World of Profit, and House of Five Talents [11]. Of these, The Rector of Justin was the most successful [11].

## Literary Significance

The Rector of Justin is widely considered to be one of Louis Auchincloss's finest works and a significant contribution to American literature. Christopher Dahl, in his study of Auchincloss's work, notes that the novel's strength lies in its ability to present a complex, multifaceted portrait of its central character [12]. The novel "supports widely divergent conclusions" about Prescott, with Auchincloss "going out of his way to present the negative as well as positive aspects of Prescott's character" [12].

The novel's narrative structure, employing multiple narrators to construct a composite biography, was innovative for its time and has influenced subsequent works of fiction. The technique allows for a more nuanced exploration of character and theme than a single narrative perspective could provide.

In the context of American literary history, The Rector of Justin represents a significant examination of WASP society during a period of profound social change. The novel captures the anxieties and contradictions of an elite class struggling to maintain its traditional authority and values in the face of modern challenges.

## Adaptation Attempts

Despite its literary success, The Rector of Justin has never been adapted for film or television, though there was a notable attempt to bring it to the screen. Film producer Walter Wanger optioned the film rights for Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer (MGM) [13]. He assembled an impressive team for the project, recruiting George Cukor to direct, Samuel A. Taylor to write the screenplay, and Spencer Tracy and Katharine Hepburn to play the lead roles of Francis Prescott and his wife Harriet [14].

However, the project never came to fruition. Tracy's declining health in the mid-1960s, combined with other production challenges, led to the eventual abandonment of the film [14]. Tracy died in 1967, effectively ending any possibility of the original cast reuniting for the project.

## Legacy

The Rector of Justin continues to be studied in academic settings and remains in print through various publishers. The novel has been translated into multiple languages and has inspired scholarly analysis of Auchincloss's work and its place in American literary history.

The novel's themes remain relevant to contemporary discussions about education, privilege, and social change. Its examination of how institutions claim to promote democratic and religious values while maintaining elitist structures continues to resonate in modern society.

In 2005, Louis Auchincloss received the National Medal of Arts in recognition of his contributions to American literature . This honor, coming late in his career, acknowledged the lasting impact of works like The Rector of Justin on American culture and letters.

The novel has influenced subsequent writers who have explored similar themes of elite education and social privilege. Authors such as John Knowles (A Separate Peace) and Donna Tartt (The Secret History) have been compared to Auchincloss in their examinations of moral complexity within academic settings .

## Author Background

Louis Auchincloss, the author of The Rector of Justin, was born on September 27, 1917, in Lawrence, New York . He came from a distinguished family with connections to the New York legal establishment, though the family had no substantial fortune. As he noted in his memoir, "each generation of Auchincloss men either made or married its own money" .

Auchincloss attended several prestigious educational institutions that would later influence his writing. He began his education at St. Bernard's School, then attended Groton School from 1929 to 1935 . His experiences at Groton, particularly under the leadership of headmaster Endicott Peabody, provided the inspiration for much of The Rector of Justin . After Groton, he enrolled at Yale University, where he was editor of the Yale Literary Magazine [15].

However, Auchincloss's undergraduate education was interrupted when his first novel, a 400-page work about love, suicide, and prep-school life modeled on Madame Bovary, was rejected by Scribners . Disillusioned with his literary aspirations, he left Yale before his senior year and enrolled at the University of Virginia School of Law in 1938 [14]. The University of Virginia Law School did not require a bachelor's degree at that time, allowing him to complete his legal education and begin his career in the autumn of 1941 [14].

Auchincloss was admitted to the New York state bar in 1941 and began his legal career at the prestigious firm of Sullivan & Cromwell [13]. His legal practice was interrupted by World War II, during which he served in the U.S. Navy in both the Atlantic and Pacific theaters of operations . After the war, he returned to private practice, working first at Sullivan & Cromwell and then at Hawkins, Delafield & Wood, where he became a partner and headed the trust and estates department [13].

Throughout his legal career, Auchincloss maintained an extraordinary literary output. He wrote over sixty books in as many years, often writing on a typewriter during breaks in his legal work . His legal background and experiences in elite New York society provided rich material for his fiction, which often explored the moral complexities of wealth, privilege, and power.

In addition to The Rector of Justin, Auchincloss is known for other novels such as The Embezzler (1966), which explored the real-life scandal of Richard Whitney; Portrait in Brownstone (1962); and House of Five Talents (1960) [11]. He also wrote biographies of American presidents Woodrow Wilson (2000) and Theodore Roosevelt (2002), as well as numerous collections of essays and literary criticism .

Auchincloss died on January 26, 2010, at the age of 92, in New York City . At the time of his death, he was acknowledged as the dean of American letters, having made significant contributions to both fiction and non-fiction literature. His work continues to be studied and appreciated for its insightful portrayals of American upper-class society and its moral complexities.
