# Meléndez–Quiñónez dynasty

The Meléndez–Quiñónez dynasty was a political dynasty in El Salvador that held the presidency from 1913 to 1927. It is known as a series of presidential periods exercised in El Salvador by members of the same wealthy family from Santa Ana, beginning in 1913 with Carlos Meléndez and ending in 1927 [1]. The dynasty consisted of Carlos Meléndez, his younger brother Jorge Meléndez, and their brother-in-law Alfonso Quiñónez Molina . While not a dynasty in the traditional sense, it is referred to as such because of the succession of family members in presidential periods, combined with a series of legal and electoral irregularities [1].

## 1. History and origins

The dynasty began on 9 February 1913, when Salvadoran President Manuel Enrique Araujo died from wounds sustained after being attacked by three farmers with machetes in San Salvador during an assassination attempt . After Araujo's death, his vice president, Carlos Meléndez Ramírez, became president .

The Meléndez clan was part of the original Salvadoran landowning oligarchy dating from the early nineteenth century [2]. The family belonged to the large Salvadoran oligarchy that, along with the Quiñónez clan, dominated the Salvadoran political landscape between 1913 and 1927, supported by their enormous wealth [3]. The dynasty represented the restrictive, elitist nature of Salvadoran politics, with the Meléndez presidents' fourteen-year occupancy of the nation's highest office being the most obvious example of this [2].

## 2. Notable members

### 2.1 Carlos Meléndez

Carlos Meléndez (San Salvador, El Salvador, 1 February 1861 – San Francisco, California, United States, 8 October 1919) was a Salvadoran politician who served as president of El Salvador from 1913 to 1914 and from 1915 to 1918 . He was the initiator of the period known in the history of El Salvador as the Meléndez–Quiñónez dynasty (1913–1927), a period in which power remained in the hands of members of this landowning family [1].

During his first term, from 9 February 1913 to 29 August 1914, Carlos Meléndez reached power as the first delegate to the presidency. After the assassination of President Manuel Enrique Araujo and the resignation of Vice President Onofre Durán, the National Legislative Organ designated him as provisional president, a position he resigned from in order to be a candidate in the 1915 elections. He then deposited power in his vice president and brother-in-law, Alfonso Quiñónez Molina [1].

Carlos Meléndez won the 1915 elections and resumed power, serving from 1 March 1915 to 21 December 1918. He resigned from the presidency for health reasons, again depositing power in his brother-in-law's hands [1].

His tenure in office is most notable for two policies: his decision to keep El Salvador neutral in World War I, despite heavy pressure from the United States, and his claim to the Gulf of Fonseca as a condominium territory [2]. Other notable events during his presidential period included the creation of the National Police and neutrality during the First World War [1].

### 2.2 Jorge Meléndez

Jorge Meléndez (San Salvador, 1871–1953) was a Salvadoran politician who exercised dictatorial power as president of the Republic of El Salvador between 1919 and 1923 . He belonged to the Meléndez dynasty, a clan of the enormous Salvadoran oligarchy that, alongside the Quiñónez clan, dominated the Salvadoran political landscape between 1913 and 1927, supported by their colossal wealth .

In 1919, after the disappearance of his older brother Carlos Meléndez (who had served as president between 1913 and 1918), Jorge was elevated to the first magistracy of the Republic thanks to electoral fraud, which had become routine throughout the period of his political clan's dominance . His primary electoral accompaniment was the Liga Roja (Red League), a vertical composition trade union organization built in 1918 by Carlos Meléndez and Alfonso Quiñónez Molina to offer a facade of legality to electoral fraud .

Jorge Meléndez was a worthy continuator of his predecessor's policy: his administration (1919–1923) was characterized by the continuous and also unpunished violation of constitutional guarantees, by the mechanism of electoral fraud, and by the economic plundering of the enormous oligarchy, which monopolized without restraint the financial elements of the Central American country .

Before becoming president, Meléndez promised to allow trade unions to organize. This did occur and Meléndez's government actively encouraged urban laborers and artisans to unionize. These unions led to the spread of ideologies such as socialism among Salvadoran workers .

### 2.3 Alfonso Quiñónez Molina

Alfonso Quiñónez Molina (Suchitoto, 11 January 1874 – San Salvador, 22 May 1950) was a Salvadoran politician and lawyer who served as provisional president of the Republic of El Salvador from 29 August 1914 to 1 March 1915 and from 21 December 1918 to 1 March 1919, and as constitutional president from 1 March 1923 to 1 March 1927 .

Quiñónez Molina began his long political career in 1892 when he was elected deputy to the National Assembly. He also served as a judge of first instance and magistrate in the judicial branch . In the governments of Presidents Jorge Meléndez (1919–1923) and Alfonso Quiñónez Molina (1923–1927), he held the positions of Minister of War and Minister of Government .

In 1918, Quiñónez founded the organization known as the Liga Roja (Red League) with the objective that it would act as a mediator between the government and the workers, including peasants, thereby attempting to compensate for the lack of unions and political parties. However, despite his intentions, his belonging to the coffee bourgeoisie did not allow him to convert the league into a minimally satisfactory form of popular representation, although he used the structure of the Red League to promote the election of Jorge Meléndez, which can be considered the first attempt in the history of El Salvador to create an official party [1].

## 3. Political and economic policies

### 3.1 Political structure

The political structure of the Meléndez–Quiñónez dynasty was characterized by a succession of family members in presidential positions, often through irregular legal and electoral means [1]. The dynasty represented the oligarchical nature of Salvadoran politics, with power concentrated in the hands of wealthy landowning families [2].

The dynasty's rule was marked by the continuous violation of constitutional guarantees and the use of electoral fraud as a mechanism to maintain power . This period saw the development of what could be considered the first attempt in Salvadoran history to create an official party, through the Liga Roja organization [1].

### 3.2 Economic policies

The economic policies of the Meléndez–Quiñónez dynasty had significant consequences for El Salvador. During this period, El Salvador experienced strong economic progress due to advances in coffee exports. However, this enrichment only benefited the enormous oligarchy close to the Meléndez–Quiñónez family and favored the creation of a monoculture regime that would burden the country's subsequent progress .

Jorge Meléndez's administration was particularly harmful to El Salvador's economy. The president adopted measures that severely undermined national sovereignty and established a narrow economic dependence of the country in relation to United States capital [3]. In 1923, Meléndez gave the United States government control over El Salvador's customs revenues as collateral for payment of the large public debt that the Salvadoran government had contracted in previous years with various New York financial entities .

### 3.3 The Liga Roja

The Liga Roja (Red League) was an organization conceived by Alfonso Quiñónez Molina in 1917-1918 that operated with the armed forces and the National Guard as instruments of domination in El Salvador [4]. The organization had a red flag as its emblem, a program impregnated with vague socialist feelings, and its own armed militia that operated with the blessing of the government [4].

The first steps for popular organization in El Salvador were through the creation or formation of the Red League in 1917. The purposes of this popular organization were profoundly political, although it was sometimes considered an organism linked to the revolutionary left [4]. However, this organization conceived by Alfonso Quiñónez Molina was actually an instrument to manipulate the dispossessed classes in favor of the cliques that remained in power [4].

The Liga Roja was used to provide a facade of legality to electoral fraud during the dynasty's period of dominance . It was the primary electoral accompaniment for Jorge Meléndez's rise to power in 1919 .

## 4. End of the dynasty

The political dynasty ended when Quiñónez Molina chose Pío Romero Bosque as his successor in 1927, as there were no other family members who were willing to assume the presidency . This marked the end of the fourteen-year period (1913–1927) in which members of the Meléndez–Quiñónez family had held the presidency of El Salvador .

After the dynasty ended, Pío Romero Bosque served as president from 1927 to 1931 . During his term, Romero Bosque lifted restrictions on the existence of political parties in opposition to the ruling National Democratic Party (PDN) . In 1931, a general election was held in the country, which is considered by historians to be the first free and fair election in Salvadoran history .

## 5. Legacy

The legacy of the Meléndez–Quiñónez dynasty is significant in Salvadoran history. The dynasty represented the oligarchical nature of Salvadoran politics during the early twentieth century and highlighted the restrictive, elitist character of the political system [2].

The dynasty's economic policies, particularly the establishment of a monoculture regime based on coffee exports, had long-lasting consequences for El Salvador's economic development. The enrichment during this period was concentrated among the oligarchy close to the Meléndez–Quiñónez family, while the broader population did not benefit .

The dynasty's use of the Liga Roja as a mechanism for electoral manipulation represented an early attempt at creating an official political party structure in El Salvador [1]. This organization also contributed to the spread of socialist ideologies among Salvadoran workers, as the unions formed during Jorge Meléndez's presidency led to the development of socialist movements .

The end of the dynasty in 1927 and the subsequent presidency of Pío Romero Bosque marked a transition toward more democratic political practices in El Salvador, including the lifting of restrictions on opposition political parties .

## 6. Historical significance

The Meléndez–Quiñónez dynasty is considered a defining period in Salvadoran history, representing the height of oligarchical political control in the country. The dynasty's fourteen-year hold on the presidency (1913–1927) is the most visible example of the restrictive nature of Salvadoran politics during this era [2].

The dynasty's policies and practices had lasting effects on Salvadoran society, including the development of labor movements, the establishment of economic dependence on foreign capital, and the concentration of wealth among a small elite. These factors would later contribute to social tensions and political conflicts in El Salvador, including the 1932 uprising known as La Matanza .
