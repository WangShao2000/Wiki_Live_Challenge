# Edwardsville Amazon warehouse collapse

The **Edwardsville Amazon warehouse collapse** occurred on the evening of December 10, 2021, when an **EF-3 tornado** struck the **DLI4 Amazon delivery facility** in Edwardsville, Illinois, United States, killing six people and injuring one.  The tornado, with estimated peak winds of 150 mph, caused the west-facing walls of the 1.1-million-square-foot warehouse to collapse inward, followed by multiple structural failures including additional wall collapses and the loss of a large segment of the roof. [1]

## Background and facility description

The **DLI4 Amazon delivery station** was a 1.1-million-square-foot warehouse that employed approximately 190 people providing last-mile delivery services to customers in the greater St. Louis region. [1] The facility, which opened in July 2020, was designed by architectural firm Richard L. Bowen & Associates and occupied 25.6 acres (10.4 hectares), with 15,000 square feet (1,400 square meters) dedicated to office space.  The warehouse was approximately 580 feet wide and 1,000 feet long (193 yards by 333 yards). [2]

The facility was located in an area prone to severe weather events, specifically in **Wind Zone IV**, which is designated as FEMA's highest tornado risk area. [3] The applicable building code adopted by the municipality required the project's design professional to design the building to withstand 90-mile-per-hour "straight-line" winds, but the tornadic winds generated by the EF-3 tornado exceeded this specification at 150 miles per hour.

## The tornado event

### Meteorological conditions

The tornado that struck the Edwardsville facility was part of a significant tornado outbreak that affected multiple states on December 10, 2021.  The National Weather Service reported that numerous severe thunderstorms formed ahead of an approaching cold front during the late afternoon and evening, with storm features including tornadoes, large hail, and damaging winds.

The weather conditions on December 10, 2021, were unusually warm for mid-December, with high temperatures reaching the upper 70s Fahrenheit under strong southerly to southwesterly winds—conditions more typical of late April or early May.  Earlier in the week, local meteorologists had predicted severe weather for December 10, including the possibility of tornadoes, and by midday, the area was in the **Moderate category for tornadoes**, the second-highest level on a scale of five.

### Tornado characteristics

The Edwardsville tornado touched down at 8:27 PM CST on the south side of Interstate 270, about three-quarters of a mile west of the I-270/I-255 interchange.  The tornado moved northeast and intensified rapidly after crossing I-255 before striking the Amazon warehouse at approximately 8:29 PM.  The tornado was rated **EF-3 on the Enhanced Fujita Scale**, with estimated peak winds of 150 mph, a path length of 4.28 miles, and a maximum width of 300 yards. [4]

The tornado's path through the Amazon facility resulted in the most severe damage along its entire track.  The storm continued moving northeast after leaving the warehouse, destroying an unanchored mobile home and outbuilding, snapping trees, and downing power poles before lifting back up into the clouds just west of Butler Boulevard at 8:32 PM CST, five minutes after initially touching down.

## Incident and casualties

### Immediate impact

The tornado first struck the **west-facing walls of the warehouse**, causing them to collapse inward. This was followed by multiple structural failures as the tornado moved through the complex, including the collapse of additional walls and the loss of a large segment of the roof. [5] The greatest damage appeared to be on the building's west side, where steel support pillars stood exposed with the walls having collapsed and roofing blown away. [5]

The collapse trapped numerous workers inside the facility.  According to reports, six Amazon employees took shelter in what was described as the "wrong designated bathroom for safety" when the warehouse walls and roof collapsed, killing them. [6] Emergency responders from Edwardsville Police and Fire Departments quickly arrived at the scene to begin rescue operations.

### Victims

Six people were confirmed killed in the collapse, and one person suffered critical injuries. [7] The victims were identified as:



* **Deandre S. Morrow**, 28, of St. Louis, Missouri

* **Kevin D. Dickey**, 62, of Carlyle, Illinois

* **Clayton Lynn Cope**, 29, of Alton, Illinois

* **Etheria S. Hebb**, 34, of St. Louis, Missouri

* **Larry E. Virden**, 46, of Collinsville, Illinois

* **Austin J. McEwen**, 26, of Edwardsville, Illinois

Forty-five people who were in the building at the time were successfully reunited with their families at a reunification center.

### Worker accounts

One of the victims, **Larry Virden**, texted his girlfriend of 13 years, Cherie Jones, just before the storm hit, stating that his bosses had told him he could not leave the facility.  "Amazon won't let me leave until after the storm blows over," he wrote.  When asked by the New York Post if she faulted Amazon for Virden's death, Jones said: "Not really. But it's that what-if situation: what if they would have let him leave? He could have made it home."

## Emergency response and recovery

### Initial response

Edwardsville Police Chief Michael Fillback described the immediate aftermath as "hectic for quite some time."  Once the initial chaos subsided, emergency responders were able to sit down with the fire department to discuss the overall response, with the goal of making future responses to similar incidents more efficient.

The Edwardsville Fire Department worked to clear debris from the site while coordinating with Amazon representatives to transition the property back to their control.  Police Chief Fillback stated: "Unfortunately, six people lost their lives because of this devastating event and one individual remains hospitalized with serious injuries."

### Lessons learned

Fire Chief James Whiteford noted that "in the fire service, we review incidents of all sizes" and that "there are always lessons to be learned from these types of events."  Reliable communication was identified as a necessity in any emergency, leading the department to purchase new responder radios to ensure communication capabilities.

The incident also highlighted challenges in managing public response, including the fact that the dispatch center was inundated with phone calls and emails while trying to handle radio traffic, 911 calls, and other service requests.

## Investigation and findings

### OSHA investigation

The U.S. Department of Labor's **Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA)** opened an investigation into the building collapse on December 11, 2021, with compliance officers on-site to provide assistance.  OSHA had six months to complete its investigation, issue citations, and propose monetary penalties if violations of workplace safety or health regulations were found.

OSHA investigators determined that while Amazon's severe weather emergency procedures met minimal federal safety guidelines for storm sheltering, the company should make improvements to further protect workers and contract drivers in future emergencies.  OSHA does not have a standard that requires severe weather emergency plans but recommends them under the Occupational Safety and Health Act's General Duty Clause, which requires employers to provide a place of employment free from known hazards.

### Key findings

The OSHA investigation revealed several critical issues:



1. **Shelter location confusion**: Based on employee interviews, some Amazon workers did not know where the designated shelter-in-place area was during emergencies.  Specifically, "some employees were unaware the designated tornado shelter was the restroom located in the northern portion of the building and instead took shelter in the restroom located in the southern portion of the building."

2. **Inadequate training**: The investigation raised concerns about the potential risk to employees during severe weather emergencies. [7]

3. **Contractor status**: Five of the deceased and the injured worker were **independent direct service provider delivery contractors** who took shelter in a bathroom on the warehouse's south side, near the loading docks.  Two worked for AB\&C D.A.D Inc. of Belleville, one for Boxify Logistics of St. Louis, and one for XSeed Delivery of Bolingbrook.

### Hazard Alert Letter

On April 26, 2022, OSHA issued a **Hazard Alert Letter to Amazon** requiring the company to review its severe weather emergency procedures. [8] The letter recommended three areas for improvement at the Edwardsville warehouse:



1. **Ensure that all employees are provided training and participate in emergency weather drills**

2. **Include site-specific information in severe weather emergency plans**

3. **All audible warning devices and locations of the device should be clearly identified in the severe weather emergency plan and readily accessible**

## Legal proceedings

### Lawsuits filed

On January 17, 2022, the first of many lawsuits was filed against Amazon, alongside Contegra Construction and Tristar Properties, LLC—a wrongful death suit on behalf of Austin McEwen.  The action claimed that Amazon failed to warn employees of dangerous weather or provide safe shelter before the tornado struck. [9]

Subsequent lawsuits were filed by attorneys Ben Crump and Bob Hilliard on behalf of multiple victims and survivors. [10] Plaintiffs included Deon January, mother and administrator for the estate of Deandre Morrow, who was killed in the collapse, and Amazon drivers Jamarco Hickman, Evan Jensen, Jada Williams, and Deontae Yancey who survived but suffered physical or mental harm. [10]

### Allegations

The lawsuits alleged that Amazon prioritized profits over worker safety. [11] Specifically, they claimed that despite recognizing that tornado warnings had been issued and the situation was "very dangerous," Amazon executives "prioritized profit over safety" and sacrificed human lives. [11] The suits also alleged that Amazon's storm shelter was inadequate and that employees should have been evacuated from the warehouse when the first warnings were issued the previous day. [11]

### Corporate response

Amazon's spokesperson stated that the warehouse was built in compliance with all regulations and that management was monitoring weather conditions. [11] In a statement, the company said: "We believe our team took the right actions immediately after the warning was issued and worked to move employees to safe locations as quickly as possible." [11] The company also stated it would contest the lawsuits while continuing to focus on supporting employees, partners, families of those who died, and the affected community. [11]

## Rebuilding controversy

### Decision to rebuild to pre-loss conditions

Despite the fatal incident, Amazon made the decision to rebuild the DLI4 facility to "pre-loss conditions" without incorporating significant safety improvements. [6] The warehouse, which Amazon leases rather than owns, was rebuilt by the property owner (an anonymous individual) who was contractually required to return the facility to its previous condition. [6]

When DLI4 was rebuilt and reopened in 2024, neither it nor any other facility in the commercial district had any storm shelters, nor was the new structure built to withstand the extreme winds that the initial tornado produced.

### Opposition from officials and families

The decision to rebuild without storm shelters drew significant criticism from lawmakers and victims' families:



1. **Lawmaker intervention**: U.S. Senator Elizabeth Warren (D-Mass.) and Representatives Cori Bush (D-Mo.) and Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez (D-N.Y.) sent a joint letter to Amazon CEO questioning the decision to rebuild without key safety improvements.  The lawmakers noted that despite Amazon's billions in profits, the company reportedly refused to pay for the minimal cost of constructing a storm shelter or safe room at a facility in Wind Zone IV, FEMA's highest tornado risk area. [3]

2. **Families' concerns**: Alice McEwen, mother of victim Austin McEwen, expressed disappointment that the facility was rebuilt without Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) shelters.  She stated: "It was unfortunate to learn that the Edwardsville Amazon facility was again built without Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) shelters when FEMA shelters can prevent a tragedy like this, where six people needlessly died, especially in an area prone to tornadoes."

3. **Safety advocates' criticism**: Safety advocates questioned what level of tragedy would be necessary to prompt the company to invest in appropriate safety measures. [12] One advocate stated: "We've learned that rebuild still does not include appropriate shelters. What level of a tragedy needs to occur to make sure that you have those right resources?" [12]

### Amazon's position

Amazon defended its decision by noting that federal guidelines from OSHA and local building codes do not require storm shelters.  Instead, the company stated it was taking alternative safety measures, including:



1. **Employee training**: Providing training on how to respond to extreme weather

2. **Meteorologist consultation**: Hiring a meteorologist to monitor weather conditions

3. **Facility assessments**: Conducting expert re-assessments at sites nationwide—starting with those where severe weather occurs more often—to determine the most structurally secure shelter-in-place areas [13]

The company spokesperson stated: "We've made adjustments where we need to across facilities in our network to account for that." [14] However, when asked why the new facility would be rebuilt without a storm shelter, the spokesperson did not provide a direct answer. [14]

## Legacy and impact

### Changes in Amazon's safety protocols

Following the Edwardsville incident, Amazon implemented several safety changes across its network:



1. **Expert assessments**: The company began conducting expert re-assessments at sites nationwide, starting with those in areas prone to severe weather, to identify the most structurally secure shelter-in-place areas. [13]

2. **Training improvements**: Amazon enhanced its severe weather emergency training programs to ensure all employees understand proper procedures.

3. **Communication systems**: The company improved communication systems to ensure employees receive clear instructions during severe weather events.

### Community response

The Edwardsville community continues to heal from the December 10, 2021 tornado that killed six people.  City council members pause their meetings for moments of silence in memory of those killed in the storm.  The incident has prompted ongoing discussions about workplace safety and corporate responsibility in the face of natural disasters. [8]

### Broader implications

The Edwardsville Amazon warehouse collapse has become a significant case study in workplace safety during severe weather events. [8] The incident has sparked nationwide discussions on the vital need for comprehensive workplace emergency plans, particularly in areas prone to tornadoes and other severe weather. [8]

The tragedy renewed concerns about suspended rules forbidding Amazon warehouse workers from carrying phones in work areas.  The rule, which was dropped in response to the COVID-19 pandemic, has prompted questions about whether companies should allow workers to have personal communication devices during emergencies.  Amazon has since confirmed that employees and drivers are allowed to have their cell phones.
