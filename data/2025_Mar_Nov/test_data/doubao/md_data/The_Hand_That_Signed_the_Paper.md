# The Hand That Signed the Paper

"The Hand That Signed the Paper" is a war protest poem by Welsh poet Dylan Thomas [1]. Written in 1933 when Thomas was just 19 years old, the poem was first published in 1936 and appears in Jon Stallworthy's edited collection "The Oxford Book of War Poetry" [1]. The work is a socio-political commentary that critiques the ruthless and indifferent exercise of political power, particularly as manifested in wartime decisions .

## Text of the Poem

The complete text of the poem is as follows :



```
The hand that signed the paper felled a city;

Five sovereign fingers taxed the breath,

Doubled the globe of dead and halved a country;

These five kings did a king to death.

The mighty hand leads to a sloping shoulder,

The finger joints are cramped with chalk;

A goose's quill has put an end to murder

That put an end to talk.

The hand that signed the treaty bred a fever,

And famine grew, and locusts came;

Great is the hand that holds dominion over

Man by a scribbled name.

The five kings count the dead but do not soften

The crusted wound nor stroke the brow;

A hand rules pity as a hand rules heaven;

Hands have no tears to flow.
```

## Background and Context

Dylan Thomas (1914-1953) was born in Swansea, South Wales, and is widely regarded as one of the most significant Welsh writers of the 20th century . He wrote prolifically throughout his lifetime and is best known for his poetry, though his work has sometimes been described as overly "florid" by critics .

The poem was written in 1933 during a period of rising totalitarianism in Europe, when Germany was beginning to recover from the Treaty of Versailles and Nazism was gaining influence . This context is crucial to understanding the poem's themes of political power and its devastating consequences. The work reflects an era filled with mass death events, including war and disease outbreaks .

Thomas's poem is a war protest piece that specifically derides "the appalling apathy and ruthlessness of the rulers toward ordinary citizens" . It serves as a haunting critique of the indifferent and devastating power wielded by political figures [2]. The poem does not reference specific historical events but rather provides a general commentary on the nature of political power and its consequences .

## Themes and Analysis

### Political Power and Its Consequences

The central theme of "The Hand That Signed the Paper" is the absolute and destructive nature of political power [3]. The poem condemns political power exercised through signatures, illustrating how a single hand's signed paper can topple cities, multiply death, and divide nations [3].

The opening stanza establishes this theme immediately: "The hand that signed the paper felled a city; / Five sovereign fingers taxed the breath, / Doubled the globe of dead and halved a country" . Here, the "hand" symbolizes political authority, and the act of signing represents the exercise of that authority. The imagery of destruction—cities falling, death doubling, countries being halved—emphasizes the catastrophic consequences of political decisions .

The poem further develops this theme by personifying the hand as a powerful ruler: "Great is the hand that holds dominion over / Man by a scribbled name" . This line highlights the arbitrary nature of power, suggesting that the authority to control others comes from nothing more than a signature on a piece of paper.

### Indifference and Lack of Compassion

Another major theme is the complete lack of empathy or compassion among those in power . The poem repeatedly emphasizes the cold, unfeeling nature of political authority. The final stanza drives this point home: "The five kings count the dead but do not soften / The crusted wound nor stroke the brow; / A hand rules pity as a hand rules heaven; / Hands have no tears to flow" .

This imagery contrasts the powerful with the suffering—the "five kings" (representing the five fingers/political authorities) count the dead but offer no comfort or healing. The phrase "Hands have no tears to flow" emphasizes the emotional barrenness of those in power, their inability to feel sympathy or grief for the human cost of their decisions .

The poem's tone is consistently resentful and melancholy [4], reflecting the speaker's anger and disgust at the ruling class's indifference to the plight of the masses .

### Transformation of Individual into Tyrant

A subtler but significant theme is the transformation of an ordinary individual into a tyrannical figure through the acquisition of power. The second stanza reveals that this mighty hand belongs to an ordinary person: "The mighty hand leads to a sloping shoulder, / The finger joints are cramped with chalk" .

This imagery suggests that the powerful figure is not a mythical or superhuman being but simply a person whose hand has become stiff from writing (perhaps from signing many documents). The reference to a "goose's quill" (a traditional writing instrument) further emphasizes the human, even mundane, nature of this authority figure .

The poem thus explores how power corrupts, transforming ordinary individuals into instruments of destruction and oppression.

## Literary Devices and Structure

### Form and Rhyme Scheme

"The Hand That Signed the Paper" is structured as a non-lyrical Augustan ballad consisting of four quatrains (four-line stanzas) with an ABAB rhyme scheme . This regular structure provides a sense of order and control that contrasts ironically with the chaotic content of the poem.

The first and third lines of each quatrain follow iambic pentameter with an additional unstressed syllable, creating a feminine rhyme . This rhythmic pattern gives the poem a unique passive quality that complements the theme of apathetic authority.

### Personification

Thomas employs extensive personification throughout the poem to amplify the powers of the hand, treating it as an influential entity acting independently . Lines such as "The hand that signed the paper felled a city," "A hand rules pity," and "Hands have no tears to flow" all personify the hand as a powerful yet callous leader .

This literary device serves multiple purposes: it emphasizes the agency of political authority, creates a more vivid and memorable image, and allows the poet to critique power itself rather than any specific individual.

### Metaphor

The poem makes extensive use of metaphor to emphasize the destructive nature of political power . The most prominent metaphor appears in the first stanza: "These five kings did a king to death," where the five fingers are compared to five powerful kings who have the authority to order the execution of another king .

This metaphor underscores the absolute nature of the power being wielded. The "five kings" represent the complete authority of the hand, suggesting that political power operates with the force of royalty or divine right.

### Imagery

The poem is rich with powerful, evocative imagery that creates a picture of horror and devastation . Examples include:



* "Doubled the globe of dead"—suggesting a worldwide increase in death and destruction

* "And famine grew, and locusts came"—evoking biblical plagues and widespread suffering

* "The crusted wound"—conjuring images of untreated injuries and ongoing pain

These images work together to create a comprehensive picture of the catastrophic consequences of political decisions made without compassion or consideration for human suffering.

### Irony

The poem employs irony to highlight the disconnect between the power of political decisions and their human cost. The phrase "A goose's quill has put an end to murder / That put an end to talk"  is particularly ironic. A simple writing instrument (the goose's quill) is credited with ending both murder and peaceful dialogue, suggesting that the tools of bureaucracy and governance can simultaneously create and end violence.

## Critical Reception and Legacy

"The Hand That Signed the Paper" is widely recognized as a significant work in Dylan Thomas's oeuvre and in 20th-century war poetry [1]. The poem is frequently anthologized and appears in major collections such as "The Oxford Book of War Poetry" [1].

Critics have praised the poem for its powerful imagery and its timeless critique of political power. The work is noted for its "haunting" quality and its ability to capture the devastating impact of political decisions on ordinary people [2]. The poem's themes of power, corruption, and the dehumanizing effects of authority remain relevant across different historical periods .

However, the poem also exemplifies Thomas's characteristic ambiguity, as he offers no specific historical context for the events described [5]. This ambiguity has been both praised and criticized—some readers appreciate the universality it provides, while others find it frustrating that the poem doesn't engage with specific political events or historical moments.

The poem has been analyzed extensively in academic contexts, with scholars examining its use of literary devices, its political commentary, and its place within Thomas's broader body of work . It is often taught in literature courses as an example of political poetry and anti-war literature.

## Musical Adaptations

Several musicians and performers have set "The Hand That Signed the Paper" to music or recorded spoken word versions of the poem [6]. Notable adaptations include:



* Recordings by Carleton Hobbs and V.C. Clinton-Baddeley as part of "Anthology of 20th Century English Poetry, Pt. 2" (1961) [6]

* A spoken word recording by Richard Burton [7]

* Various musical settings, including one by composer Gwyneth Walker [8]

These adaptations demonstrate the poem's enduring appeal and its suitability for performance. The rhythmic quality of the poem, with its regular meter and rhyme scheme, makes it particularly well-suited to musical interpretation.
