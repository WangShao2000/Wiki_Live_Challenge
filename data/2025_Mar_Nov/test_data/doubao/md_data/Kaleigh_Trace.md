# Kaleigh Trace

Kaleigh Trace (born 1986) is a Canadian writer, sex educator, and therapist based in Toronto, Ontario, known for her candid discussions of sexuality and disability . She is recognized for her memoir "Hot, Wet, and Shaking: How I Learned to Talk About Sex," which won the 2015 Evelyn Richardson Memorial Non-Fiction Award . Trace's work explores themes of disability, sexuality, and self-acceptance, offering a unique perspective as a queer, disabled woman in the fields of sex education and therapy [1].

## Early life and education

Kaleigh Trace was born in 1986 and grew up in Canada . At the age of eight, she sustained a spinal cord injury in a car accident, which left her permanently disabled . This experience profoundly shaped her perspective on body image, sexuality, and disability rights. She later described growing up feeling "inherently undesirable" due to her disability, as she couldn't relate to mainstream representations of beauty and sex appeal .

Trace completed her Bachelor of Arts degree at Dalhousie University in 2009 [2]. Following her undergraduate education, she pursued a Master's of Science in Couple and Family Therapy at the University of Guelph, which she completed around 2017 [3]. During her graduate studies, she specialized in healing from sexual trauma . She is also a registered psychotherapist with the College of Registered Psychotherapists in Ontario [3].

## Career

### Sex education work

After graduating from Dalhousie University in the midst of the 2009 global recession, Trace needed employment and applied to work at Venus Envy, a queer, feminist sex shop in Halifax . She was initially more interested in the store's collection of feminist critical theory than in the sex toys, but she was hired and began working there at age 23 .

At Venus Envy, Trace worked for five years as a sex educator, spending her days talking to customers about their fantasies, desires, worries, and shame . Her role involved selling sex toys, providing sex education to customers, and giving workshops across Nova Scotia at private events, bachelorette parties, and senior facilities . She described this period as transformative, noting that through these conversations she felt a sense of communion, "like 'Oh, we're all just animals, looking for a home'" .

During her time at Venus Envy, Trace began writing a blog called "The Fucking Facts," where she documented the pleasure and joy of sex for people with disabilities [4]. The blog also addressed deeply personal topics, including an abortion she had two years prior to starting it . Through this blog, she sought to provide representation for disabled people in the world of sex writing, as she felt there was a lack of such representation .

### Writing career

Trace's writing career began when she was introduced to Robbie MacGregor, who ran Invisible Publishing, at a party . Her friend suggested she should write a book, and MacGregor encouraged her to pursue the project. The result was her memoir "Hot, Wet, and Shaking: How I Learned to Talk About Sex," published in 2014 [5].

The book chronicles Trace's journey from being a "critically uncool teenager" to a successful couples and sex therapist, weaving together themes of feminism, sex positivity, and disability justice [6]. It explores her experiences as a disabled, queer, feminist sex educator, examining her personal story with sexuality through the lens of her spinal cord injury and work at Venus Envy .

"Hot, Wet, and Shaking" was well-received and won the 2015 Evelyn Richardson Memorial Non-Fiction Award at the Atlantic Book Awards . In 2024, Invisible Publishing released a 10th anniversary edition with additional essays and a foreword from musician and writer Christa Couture [5].

Trace's written work has appeared in numerous publications, including Toronto Star, Toronto Life, The Coast, Shameless Magazine, The Huffington Post, and on CBC Radio's "Tapestry" [5]. She has also contributed to other publications such as Crit and The Tide [7].

### Therapy practice

After leaving Venus Envy in her early thirties, Trace moved to Toronto in 2017 and commuted to Guelph to complete her master's degree . Following her graduation, she was hired at a couples therapy clinic in Toronto before eventually opening her own private practice .

As a couples and sex therapist, Trace's work centers on the belief that intimate relationships are sites of healing [6]. Her practice promotes the transformative power of compassionate love, and she has witnessed firsthand how relationships can facilitate healing and growth [6]. She is particularly interested in working with queer and disabled communities, offering a unique perspective informed by her own experiences [1].

## Personal life and activism

Trace identifies as a queer, disabled woman [1]. She describes herself as someone who is "older and more tired but still excited, radicalized and eager" . She splits her time between Toronto and Nova Scotia, joking that she "struggles with commitment" [1].

In October 2022, Trace received a diagnosis of rapidly growing cancer in her bladder . Initially scheduled for emergency surgery, she was later informed that the cancer had spread and was terminal, with doctors giving her approximately two years to live [1]. This diagnosis has profoundly impacted her perspective on life and her work, leading her to reflect on her younger self while revising her memoir for its 10th anniversary edition .

Trace is an activist for disability rights and reproductive justice. She has been involved in abortion rights activism in Nova Scotia, described by one source as a "badass feminist" who "knows the sexual health system better than most folks" [8]. Her work promotes the idea that pleasure and sexuality are for all bodies, regardless of ability [9].

## Legacy and influence

Kaleigh Trace's work has had a significant impact on the fields of sex education and disability rights. Through her writing and activism, she has challenged societal norms around disability and sexuality, providing representation for disabled people in discussions about sex and desire [6].

Her memoir has been described as "a frank, poignant and funny look at sex" that is informed by her blog "The Fucking Facts" [10]. Readers have responded to her work with appreciation for its honesty and vulnerability, with many sharing their own personal stories with her after reading the book .

As a disabled woman publicly discussing her sexuality and desire, Trace has helped create space for other disabled people to explore and express their own sexualities. Her work has been influential in promoting the idea that disabled bodies are deserving of pleasure and can be objects of desire [6].

In 2024, as she faces her terminal cancer diagnosis, Trace continues to write and share her experiences, offering insights on living with disability and facing mortality. Her revised edition of "Hot, Wet, and Shaking" represents a full-circle moment, allowing her to engage in "an editorial conversation with \[her] younger self throughout the process of planning for \[her] death" .
