# Hans Otto Roth

**Hans Otto Roth** (29 April 1890 – 1 April 1953) was a prominent Transylvanian Saxon politician and lawyer who served as the parliamentary chairman of the German Party in Romania from 1922 to 1938 . Born in Sighişoara, he studied law across Europe before becoming a key figure in representing German minority interests in interwar Romania . Roth is best known for his unsuccessful attempts to counter the rise of radical Nazism within the German Party and the German ethnic group in Transylvania . His political career was marked by efforts to balance loyalty to Romania with advocacy for German minority rights, ultimately leading to his persecution and death in a communist prison camp .

## Early Life and Education

Hans Otto Roth was born on 29 April 1890 in Schäßburg (present-day Sighişoara), Austria-Hungary, into a Schäßburg patrician family [1]. He pursued legal education at various universities across Europe before graduating with a doctorate from the ELTE Faculty of Law in 1913 . His educational journey took him to multiple European institutions, reflecting the international nature of higher education for Transylvanian Saxon elites during this period .

## Political Career

### Early Political Activities

Roth began his political career in 1920 as the chief advocate of the "Deutschsächsischen Volksrates für Siebenbürgen" (German-Saxon People's Council for Transylvania) [1]. His early political work focused on representing Transylvanian Saxon interests during a critical period following World War I and the dissolution of Austria-Hungary [1].

In 1918, Roth joined the Saxon Central Committee to support the unification of Transylvania with Romania . This decision reflected the political realities following the war, as the Paris Peace Conference recognized the union of Transylvania with Romania. In 1919, he won a seat in the Chamber of Deputies representing the German Party .

### Leadership of the German Party

By 1922, Roth had risen to replace Rudolf Brandsch as the parliamentary chairman of the German Party, a position he would hold until 1938 . As the parliamentary spokesman for the Transylvanian Saxons in the Romanian Parliament, Roth "outshone his ten years older opponent Rudolf Brandsch, with whom he was otherwise connected by a liberal-conservative concept" [1].

Both Roth and Brandsch attempted to secure for Germans the rights promised in the Karlsbad Decrees and minority protection treaties through loyal behavior toward the consolidating Romanian national state and skillful negotiation within parliamentary possibilities [1]. These rights included German schools, use of the mother tongue, equality of nationalities in the economy, preservation of the property of the Saxon National University, and maintenance of land ownership [1].

Roth's political position was strengthened by his election as regional church curator and his good relationship with Bishop Viktor Glondys [1]. This religious dimension was significant in Transylvanian Saxon politics, as the Lutheran church played a central role in community life.

### Economic and Cultural Activities

Beyond his parliamentary role, Roth held several other influential positions. From 1928 to 1932, he served as the temporary president of the Hermannstädter Allgemeine Sparkasse (Hermannstadt General Savings Bank), a credit institution that gave him significant influence in the economic field .

As chairman of the board of the "Siebenbürgisch-Deutschen Tageblatts" (Transylvanian German Daily), he held a key position in German-language publishing in the country [1]. This newspaper was one of the most important German-language publications in Transylvania and served as a platform for discussing community issues and political developments.

From 1931 to 1934, Roth also served as chairman of the Association of German Minorities in Europe . Initially, he advocated for pan-Germanism in this role, but his views evolved significantly after meeting Adolf Hitler in June 1933 .

### Opposition to Nazism

Roth's political stance underwent a significant transformation following his meeting with Hitler in 1933. While he initially supported pan-Germanism, this encounter altered some of his views . Many Jews in Transylvania still felt betrayed by Roth, suggesting that his anti-Nazi stance was not absolute or consistent .

In the following years, Roth became increasingly anti-Nazi with the advent of the "Renewal Movement" (Erneuerungsbewegung) within the German Party . His views were generally considered Christian liberal, which put him in opposition to many of the Nazi Party's views .

The "Renewal Movement" with its politicization of the masses and rejection of the conservative policy of parliamentarians and the church led to a bitter internal Saxon dispute, from which the adoption of the Third Reich was seen as a way out [1]. Roth's attempts to maintain a moderate position and counter the rise of radical Nazism were ultimately unsuccessful .

Roth was approached many times to serve as a minister in various Romanian cabinets, but declined because the Iron Guard always had positions in these cabinets . In 1943, this opposition to Nazism culminated in a conflict between Andreas Schmidt (a Nazi sympathizer) and Roth, resulting in Roth's expulsion from the party .

## Later Life and Persecution

### Activities During World War II

After his expulsion from the German Party in 1943, Roth continued working as the regional church curator of the Evangelical Church of the Augsburg Confession in Romania, a position he had held since 1932 . His various attempts to stop the self-Nazification of the church led to failures .

On 23 August 1944, King Michael of Romania staged a coup d'état, joining the Allies and allowing Soviet troops to enter the country. The German Volksgruppenführung (ethnic group leadership) was abolished [1]. In this critical moment, Roth once again intervened decisively: in a proclamation to the Germans of Romania, he exhorted them to "prudence and loyalty to the state" [1].

Dr. Hans Otto Roth wrote in the Siebenburgisch-Deutsches Tageblatt: "Do not leave your farm or work place but remain strong and calm" [2]. This position placed him in opposition to SS General Artur Phelps, who encouraged the arrest of Roth, accusing him of holding a "communist-friendly" stance because he sought to prevent the evacuation of the Saxon population .

### Post-War Persecution

Following the communist takeover of Romania in 1945, Roth was classified by the government of Petru Groza as a "bourgeois reactionary" [1]. In 1948, he was arrested and sentenced to six months in prison in the context of a currency case against the administrative members of the Hermannstädter Sparkasse [1].

After his release, Roth achieved acquittal in a retrial and lived subsequently in retirement [1]. However, in early 1952, following an intensification of the party line, Roth was arrested again [1].

### Imprisonment and Death

Hans Otto Roth died on 1 April 1953 in the Ghencea concentration camp near Bucharest at the age of 62 . His death in a communist prison camp marked the tragic end of a political career that had spanned more than three decades [3].

According to a 1953 obituary by Alfred Hönig in the Südostdeutsches Heimatblatt: "Dr. Hans Otto Roth †" [1]. His legacy as a Transylvanian Saxon politician who attempted to navigate between loyalty to Romania and advocacy for German minority rights, while resisting the rise of Nazism, remains significant in the history of interwar Romania.

## Legacy

Hans Otto Roth's life and career reflect the complex political challenges faced by ethnic minorities in interwar Eastern Europe. His attempts to maintain a moderate, pro-Romanian stance while advocating for German minority rights ultimately proved unsuccessful in the face of rising nationalism and later communist persecution .

The scholarly community has recognized Roth's significance. Thomas Frühmesser's 2013 biography "Hans Otto Roth: Biographie eines rumäniendeutschen Politikers (1890-1953)" was published as volume 43 of the "Studia Transylvanica" series [4]. This academic work provides a comprehensive examination of Roth's life and political career, including his early years, his role in the German Party, his opposition to Nazism, and his persecution under communism [4].

Roth's papers and collections are preserved at the Black Church Archives in Brașov, Romania [5], serving as an important resource for historians studying Transylvanian Saxon history and minority politics in interwar Romania.
