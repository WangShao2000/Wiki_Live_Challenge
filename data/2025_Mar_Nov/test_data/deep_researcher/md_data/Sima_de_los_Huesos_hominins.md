# Sima_de_los_Huesos_hominins

Lead section:
The Sima de los Huesos hominins are a population of early human ancestors found in the Sima de los Huesos site in Atapuerca, Spain. Dating back to approximately 430,000 years ago, they are believed to be transitional forms between Homo antecessor and Neanderthals. Their discovery has provided significant insights into the evolution of humans, particularly the development of Neanderthals.

Discovery section:
The Sima de los Huesos site, also known as the "Pit of Bones," has yielded an extensive collection of human fossils. The first human fossils were discovered in 1976 by T. Torres, and systematic excavations began in the 1980s. Over 28 hominid individuals have been identified in the site, representing a rich fossil record of the earliest human beings in Europe.

Characteristics section:
The Sima de los Huesos hominins are believed to have been large-bodied with an average cranial capacity of 1232 cubic centimeters. They exhibit a combination of Neanderthal and early human traits, with features such as large volumes of the tympanic cavity and the mastoid process in the ear. They may have been overall large-bodied like other archaic humans, with dimensions of about 170 cm (5 ft 7 in) and 90 kg (200 lb) for both males and females.

Significance section:
The Sima de los Huesos hominins are significant due to their relationship to Neanderthals and their transitional characteristics. Analysis of nuclear DNA from these hominins has provided evidence of their relationship to Neanderthals, suggesting they are more closely related to them than to Denisovans. This has important implications for our understanding of human evolution and the development of Neanderthals.

Timeline section:
The Sima de los Huesos hominins lived approximately 430,000 years ago during the Middle Pleistocene. They are estimated to be about 1.2 million years old and are the oldest known human fossils on the European continent.
