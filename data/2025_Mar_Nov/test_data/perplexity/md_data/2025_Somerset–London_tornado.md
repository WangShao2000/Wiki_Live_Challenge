# Meteorological synopsis
The tornado was produced by a long-lived supercell thunderstorm that originated in the Ozarks region of southern Missouri earlier in the day on May 16. As the system moved eastward across Kentucky, atmospheric conditions became highly unstable during the evening hours due to a lull in earlier storm activity, which allowed for the development of discrete supercells ahead of a cold front.[3]

The parent supercell entered Russell County, Kentucky, late on May 16. The tornado first touched down approximately 4 miles (6.4 km) southeast of Russell Springs. It initially tracked eastward as a narrow funnel causing EF2 to low-end EF3 damage to structures and trees in rural Russell County before crossing into Pulaski County. As the tornado approached the city of Somerset, it intensified and widened, maintaining a track along the southern periphery of the city.[3]

After exiting Somerset, the tornado traversed the rugged terrain of the Daniel Boone National Forest, where it reached its maximum width of 1,700 yards (1,600 m). Radar analysis indicated that debris was lofted to an altitude of at least 27,000 feet (8,200 m) as the storm moved through the forested area. The tornado continued eastward into Laurel County, crossing Interstate 75 and striking the southern communities of London just before midnight local time. The tornado dissipated approximately 6 miles (9.7 km) southeast of London in the early hours of May 17.[2][3]

# Impact
The tornado caused catastrophic damage across three counties, with the most severe impacts concentrated in Pulaski and Laurel counties.

### Russell and Pulaski Counties
In Russell County, the tornado caused significant damage to homes and outbuildings in the southeastern portion of the county, resulting in one fatality. As the storm moved into Pulaski County, it struck the southern side of Somerset. The headquarters of the Southern Kentucky Rural Electric Cooperative Corporation (RECC) sustained considerable damage, as did the Redeemer Lutheran Church.[3]

The tornado impacted a commercial district near Somerset, striking the Somerset Cinemas 8 theater. Approximately 100 people were inside the theater at the time; the building lost power and sustained structural damage, but those inside were able to shelter in interior hallways. Although the cinema and nearby businesses such as the Wedding Shoppe suffered heavy damage from the broader storm system—including earlier straight-line winds—the tornado itself leveled nearby residential structures and snapped electrical poles throughout the area. One fatality was recorded in Pulaski County.[1][3]

### Laurel County
The most devastating damage occurred in Laurel County, particularly in the Sunshine Hills subdivision located southwest of the London city limits. In this community, the tornado reached its peak intensity of EF4. Well-constructed homes were leveled or swept from their foundations, and vehicles were thrown large distances.[2][3]

Seventeen of the nineteen total fatalities occurred in London, with the majority concentrated in the Sunshine Hills neighborhood. The tornado also caused significant damage to the London-Corbin Airport, where hangars were destroyed and helicopters were overturned. London Mayor Randall Weddle reported that 817 homes in the county were affected, with 280 completely destroyed and another 195 sustaining major damage.[2][3]

# Aftermath
Immediately following the tornado, officials in Laurel County declared a mass casualty incident. Emergency responders from surrounding counties and the Kentucky State Police deployed to the area to conduct search and rescue operations, which were hampered by darkness, downed trees, and power lines. Chainsaw crews were requested via public broadcasts to help clear roads for emergency vehicles.[2]

The First Baptist Church in London opened as a shelter for displaced residents. CHI Saint Joseph Health in London reported treating 108 patients for injuries related to the storm. The sheer scale of the damage left a "scar" across the landscape visible from space, which was subsequently documented by NASA's Earth Observatory.[4][5][3][2]

In the days following the event, the National Weather Service in Jackson conducted extensive ground and aerial surveys to determine the path and intensity of the tornado. The survey confirmed the EF4 rating and established the path length at 60.02 miles (96.59 km). The event highlighted the dangers of nocturnal tornadoes, which are statistically more likely to result in fatalities due to the difficulty of spotting them and the likelihood of residents being asleep.[1][3]
