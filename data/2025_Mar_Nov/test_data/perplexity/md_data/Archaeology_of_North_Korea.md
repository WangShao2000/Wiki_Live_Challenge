# Archaeology of North Korea

The archaeological record of what is now North Korea stretches from the first human presence in the peninsula during the Middle Pleistocene to the medieval Goryeo period. The Korean peninsula contains abundant evidence of human habitation across multiple prehistoric and historic periods, including distinctive pottery traditions, monumental stone structures known as dolmens, and richly decorated royal tombs. Modern North Korean archaeology is characterized by distinctive methodological approaches derived from Juche ideology, which emphasizes Korean cultural independence and development, and incorporates elements of Marxist historiography. This ideological framework influences interpretations of the archaeological record and diverges from scholarly practices in other regions.

# Archaeological Record

### Paleolithic Period

The earliest human presence in the Korean peninsula dates to the Early Paleolithic, though the precise chronology remains disputed. Objects resembling stone tools have been found at Hukwuri Cave, part of the Komun Moru site in Sangwon County southeast of Pyongyang, though questions persist regarding their dating and human attribution.[1] While disputed Homo erectus remains have been found in South Korea, they are well attested in northeastern China, suggesting human presence in Korea likely dates to the Middle Pleistocene.[1] Homo erectus likely dispersed throughout the peninsula after entering from Northern China via the coastal plains.[1]

Claims of Neanderthal sites dating to the Riss-Würm interglacial period (approximately 120,000 years ago) have been made for both North and South Korea, but without conclusive supporting evidence.[1] Later specimens from the Last Glacial Period have been designated as Homo sapiens.[1] The paleolithic inhabitants of Korea subsisted through hunting of wild boar and deer, fishing, and foraging, though the absence of projectile points and storage containers in the archaeological record makes identifying specific foods consumed difficult.[1] Climate conditions during the Pleistocene remained relatively mild and stable within the peninsula, though rising sea levels during interglacial periods flooded vast coastal plains and restricted inhabitants to mountainous and forested interior regions.[1]

In Korean archaeological chronology, the period from approximately 15,000 to 8,000 BCE is termed the Terminal Paleolithic.[1] The peninsula's population remained dispersed into small hunter-gatherer groups; one scholarly study suggested a total population of no more than 40,000 during the last two millennia of the Paleolithic.[1]

### Jeulmun Pottery Period (c. 8000–1500 BCE)

The Jeulmun pottery period represents the Mesolithic and Neolithic cultural stages in Korea.[2] The period is named after the decorated pottery vessels—juman (즐문) meaning "comb-patterned"—that form a large part of the pottery assemblage.[2] The earliest Jeulmun pottery, termed Yunggimun or raised-design pottery, dates to approximately 5,000 BCE and is characterized by flat-bottomed wares with relief designs and raised horizontal lines.[2] The Early Jeulmun period (c. 6000–3500 BCE) is characterized by deep-sea fishing, hunting, and small semi-permanent settlements with pit-houses.[2] Samples from settlements such as Seopohang, Amsa-dong, and Osan-ri demonstrate evidence of sedentary occupation.[2]

In North Korea, prominent Jeulmun sites include Chitamni on the Chaeryong River, noted for the presence of grain remains, and Chonghori just east of Pyongyang, which contains both Jeulmun and Yunggimun pottery.[1] A cluster of sites found along the Yalu River exhibits distinctions from classical Jeulmun pottery, while another cluster around the Tumen River, on the borders with China and Russia, shows stronger affiliation with Neolithic cultures of southeastern Siberia.[1]

### Mumun Period (c. 1500–300 BCE)

The Mumun pottery period, named for undecorated or plain cooking and storage vessels, spans approximately 1500 to 300 BCE.[3] The Mumun culture, likely spreading into the peninsula from northeast China around the second millennium BCE, is attested by megalithic dolmens and large villages of pit-houses.[1] This period saw the introduction of bronze-working technology and the establishment of fully agricultural subsistence patterns.[1] The Early Mumun period (c. 1500–850 BCE) is characterized by shifting cultivation, fishing, hunting, and discrete settlements with rectangular semi-subterranean pit-houses, predominantly located in river valleys of west-central Korea.[3] By the end of this sub-period, larger settlements began appearing, and long-standing traditions related to Mumun ceremonial and mortuary systems, including megalithic burials and red-burnished pottery production, took shape.[3]

Dolmens (Korean: goindol, literally "propped stone"), which serve as burial sites representative of the Bronze Age, are found throughout the Korean peninsula in high concentrations. Approximately 30,000 dolmens have been identified in South Korea and between 10,000 and 15,000 in North Korea, together accounting for over 40 percent of all dolmens identified worldwide.[4] Dolmens take two main forms: "table" dolmens constructed with upright stone slabs surrounding an above-ground burial cist and a flat capstone roof, and "checkerboard" dolmens featuring underground burial chambers made with piled stone fragments covered by a capstone. The considerable labor required to move massive capstones—estimated at 200 workers needed for a 32-ton stone—suggests that table dolmen construction required concentrated social organization and power.[4]

Burial offerings including earthenware, stone tools, and bronze wares recovered from dolmens provide insights into Bronze Age society and economy.[1]

### Wiman Joseon and Lelang Commandery Period (109 BCE–313 CE)

Wiman Joseon, an ancient Korean kingdom that emerged in the area around modern Pyongyang, was conquered by the Chinese Han dynasty in 109 BCE.[1] Following this conquest, the Han established the Lelang Commandery as part of the Four Commanderies of Han.[1] The Lelang Commandery dominated portions of North Korea for approximately 400 years, though it left relatively few archaeological remains.[1] Tombs and burial mounds constitute the most common archaeological sites from the Lelang period.[1] Some dates can be determined from Lelang artifacts; for example, a lacquer bowl recovered from Lelang contexts is dated by inscription to 69 CE.[1]

The interpretation of Lelang Commandery archaeological remains has become contested within North Korean scholarship, which reinterprets artifacts such as tombs, jewelry, and lacquerware as remains of Gojoseon or Goguryeo rather than Han colonial sites, reflecting Juche-influenced scholarly perspectives.[5]

### Goguryeo Period (37 BCE–668 CE)

Goguryeo, one of the Three Kingdoms of Korea, developed a distinct material culture incorporating influences from both China and nomadic groups of the northwest.[1] The kingdom, traditionally dated to a foundation around 37 BCE, eventually established its capital at Pyongyang in 427 CE.[6] Royal tombs initially took the form of pyramidal structures constructed from cut stone, while later examples consist of hemispherical tumuli containing stone burial chambers.[1] Although nearly all were plundered in antiquity, many tombs still contain elaborate murals.[1]

Archaeological evidence from Goguryeo sites includes horse equipment and some of the earliest known examples of stirrups.[1] Over 10,000 Goguryeo tombs of various types exist throughout the region, with approximately 90 unearthed in China and Korea containing preserved wall paintings.[6] These murals depict scenes of daily life, religious practices, warfare, and mythology, offering invaluable insights into Goguryeo society.[6] The murals show vivid coloration and are concentrated in several regions, with approximately 70 murals documented primarily in the Taedong river basin near Pyongyang, the Anak area in South Hwanghae province, and in Ji'an in China's Jilin province.[6]

The Anak Tomb No. 3, discovered near Pyongyang in 1949, ranks among the most significant Goguryeo monuments.[1] Constructed from basalt and limestone blocks, the tomb's internal configuration consists of an entryway, foreroom, antechamber with side-rooms, and burial chamber aligned on a south-north axis.[7] The tomb's wall paintings are particularly important for understanding the development of Goguryeo mural traditions.[7] Inscriptions within the tomb allow dating to approximately 357 CE, during the reign of King Gogukwon.[7] The site was excavated immediately following Korean independence with the intention of preserving cultural heritage, and continuous investigation and study have continued since the first formal report in 1958.[7]

### Unified Silla and Balhae Periods (668–926 CE)

Following the conquest of Baekje in 660 and Goguryeo in 668 by Silla with Tang Dynasty assistance, the Unified Silla kingdom controlled most of the peninsula. The northern region of the defeated Goguryeo later reemerged as Balhae (698–926 CE), which ruled Manchuria and northern Korean territories. Both kingdoms eventually fell to the emerging Goryeo state by 935 CE. Archaeological evidence from these periods indicates advanced material culture, with Buddhism and aristocratic society forming the twin pillars of Unified Silla civilization.[8] The period from 668 to 926 is sometimes termed the "North-South States period" (nambukguk sidae) by historians, reflecting the simultaneous rule of these two polities.[8]

### Goryeo Period (918–1392 CE)

Goryeo, which succeeded Balhae and eventually unified the peninsula, developed distinctive ceramic traditions that form important components of the archaeological record.[9] Goryeo ceramic production was initially influenced by Yuezhou kilns from southern China but later integrated elements from northern China, including concurrent production of celadon and white porcelain, unique vessel forms, and decoration techniques such as inlaying and iron-painting.[9] These innovations were influenced by interactions with the Liao and Jin dynasties. The influence of northern China introduced technological advances such as secondary firing and new vessel designs, creating a fusion of diverse cultural influences in Goryeo ceramic evolution.[9]

# Methodological Approaches and Institutional Framework

### North Korean Archaeological Practice

The development of archaeology in North Korea followed a distinctive trajectory shaped by political ideology and institutional factors. Initial academic practice in early North Korea drew heavily from Soviet models, which emphasized Marxist interpretive frameworks.[1] Among the first major archaeological discoveries of the newly independent North Korean state were the excavation of a shell midden in Unggi County in 1947 and the discovery of three Goguryeo tombs, including the richly decorated Anak Tomb No. 3, near Pyongyang in 1949.[1] Initial findings were published in the ethnographic journal Munhwa yumul (Cultural Properties), which appeared from 1949 to 1950, with subsequent work appearing in various historical journals.[1]

Archaeological excavations resumed in 1954 following the Korean War armistice, with early work aimed at documenting and restoring cultural heritage damaged during the conflict.[1] During the 1950s, North Korean archaeologists under leadership (notably the archaeologist To) conducted excavations at important sites including the Bronze Age Odong site in Hoeryong (1954–1955) and the Neolithic Jitapri site in Pongsan (1957).[1]

### Juche Ideology and Historiography

Modern North Korean archaeology is oriented around Juche ideology, which incorporates elements of nationalism and Marxist historiography.[1] This framework reflects the broader political emphasis on Korean independence and self-reliance and is reflected in archaeological interpretation and chronology. The Juche perspective presents Korean civilization as having developed independently, explicitly rejecting interpretations that emphasize Chinese influence on Korean cultural development.[1] This ideological orientation influences methodological choices and chronological assignments.

North Korean archaeologists demonstrate a preference for using traditional historical accounts in lieu of radiocarbon dating, resulting in a less conservative chronology than that adopted by South Korean counterparts, who rely more extensively on scientific dating techniques.[1] A notable example involves the interpretation of Paleolithic remains; North Korean scholars employ different methodological approaches, utilizing object typology and stratigraphy rather than the radiocarbon dating preferred in international practice, which leads to notably earlier dating of archaeological evidence.[5] In the mid-1960s, following ideological shifts emphasizing Juche principles, North Korean archaeologists reported discovery of Paleolithic cultural remains, reflecting the empowerment of archaeological research through alignment with state ideology.[10]

The divergence between North and South Korean archaeological methodologies results in significant differences in chronological assignments for the same periods and sites. This distinction reflects both methodological preference and the broader ideological framework within which North Korean scholarship operates.

### Research Institutions

The Institute of Archaeology of the Academy of Social Sciences and the Faculty of History at Kim Il Sung University serve as the primary archaeological organizations in North Korea.[1] These institutions offer archaeological degree programs, publish academic journals, and conduct archaeological excavations throughout the country. The Faculty of History at Kim Il Sung University maintains educational and research missions focused on training specialists in historical study and conducting original research.[11]
