# Natural Dis-tinction Un-natural Selection

**Natural Dis-tinction Un-natural Selection** is the thirty-third collection by British fashion designer Alexander McQueen, released for the Spring/Summer 2009 season of his eponymous fashion house. The collection reflected McQueen's concerns about human impact on the environment, drawing inspiration from the naturalist Charles Darwin and his theories of evolution. Known for its pioneering use of digital prints, the collection featured designs in which prints were engineered to match the specific patterns and proportions of individual garments.[1][2]

The collection was divided into two distinct sequences that contrasted the beauty of the natural environment with the consequences of human industrialization. The first phase presented soft colors, organic motifs, and flowing silhouettes representing nature undisturbed by human activity. The second phase featured prints manipulated to appear unnaturally perfect, incorporating artificially bright colors and stark black-and-white designs that evoked industrial technology and human intervention. Across both phases, McQueen presented classical tailoring alongside experimental silhouettes. Forty-one looks were presented at the collection's runway show, which was staged on October 3, 2008, at the Cent Quatre building in Paris, a community arts center housed in a former city morgue. The catwalk was framed by exotic taxidermy animals and featured a large circular screen projecting celestial imagery and McQueen's own eye.[1]

Contemporary critical reception was mixed, with fashion journalists and critics expressing varied reactions to the collection. Many preferred the romantic aesthetic of the first half of the show, while some felt disappointed by what they perceived as a reduction in the dramatic runway theatrics for which McQueen had become known. McQueen continued to explore environmental themes and digital printing techniques in his subsequent collections; he is credited with popularizing the use of digital prints in mainstream fashion. Pieces from *Natural Dis-tinction Un-natural Selection* are held by various museums worldwide and have appeared in major exhibitions, including the McQueen retrospective *Alexander McQueen: Savage Beauty*.[1]

# Background

Alexander McQueen established himself as a prominent British fashion designer through his innovative and frequently provocative designs, combined with his theatrical runway presentations. After graduating from Central Saint Martins College of Art and Design with a Master of Arts degree in 1992, McQueen launched his own fashion house in 1993. Early in his career, he gained attention for introducing the "bumster" trouser, a radical reinterpretation of traditional tailoring that exposed the upper portion of the intergluteal cleft and challenged conventional standards of dress. In 1996, McQueen received the British Designer of the Year award and was appointed head designer of Givenchy, the French haute couture house. In 2001, McQueen's company became majority-owned by the Gucci Group (later Kering), allowing him to retain his own brand while maintaining creative independence.[3][1]

Throughout his career, McQueen demonstrated a particular fascination with the intersection of nature, technology, and human existence. His collections frequently incorporated unexpected materials and explored themes of transformation, vulnerability, and beauty alongside brutality. By the mid-2000s, McQueen had solidified his reputation as a designer willing to tackle complex conceptual themes through his work, using the runway as a platform for artistic and social commentary.[1]

# Inspiration and Concept

The *Natural Dis-tinction Un-natural Selection* collection drew primary inspiration from Charles Darwin and his theory of evolution, as well as from the Industrial Revolution and its environmental consequences. In his collection notes, McQueen stated his belief that "we're in danger of killing the planet through greed," although he clarified that he was "not aiming to preach." This tension between artistic expression and advocacy informed the collection's design approach, creating garments that presented the natural world and human technological advancement in provocative juxtaposition rather than through didactic messaging.[2][4][1]

The collection's divided structure—separating natural beauty from industrial transformation—served as a visual metaphor for McQueen's exploration of the distinction between what exists without human intervention and what becomes altered through human activity. This conceptual framework extended to the choice of venue and presentation design, emphasizing the collection's thematic concerns about humanity's relationship with the natural world.

# Runway Show and Venue

The *Natural Dis-tinction Un-natural Selection* collection was presented on October 3, 2008, at the Cent Quatre (104) in Paris, located in the city's 19th arrondissement. Originally constructed as a slaughterhouse and later serving as a municipal morgue, the Cent Quatre had been transformed into a multidisciplinary arts center hosting studios, exhibitions, performances, and community spaces. The venue's history as a site of death and industrial processing added conceptual resonance to McQueen's exploration of human impact and transformation.[5][1]

The runway environment was designed to enhance the collection's thematic concerns. The catwalk was framed by antique taxidermy specimens including an elephant, giraffe, tiger, zebra, and polar bear, creating an immediate visual dialogue between living creatures and their preserved remains—between nature and human documentation of nature. A large circular metal structure at the rear of the sloped concrete runway displayed projected images of celestial bodies and a rotating Earth, along with magnified projections of McQueen's own eye, creating layers of meaning around observation, perception, and human consciousness within the natural world.[2][1]

# Collection Design

### First Sequence: Natural Forms

The initial phase of the collection presented designs celebrating the natural world in its undisturbed state. Garments featured soft color palettes incorporating pinks, whites, and earth tones, constructed from natural fabrics including silk and other traditional materials. Silhouettes remained characteristically McQueen, featuring fitted waists, dramatic proportions, and his signature frock coat shapes, but rendered in a more fluid and organic manner than his typical aesthetic.[2][1]

A signature element of this sequence was the use of digitally engineered prints depicting natural materials and forms. Prints featured patterns inspired by wood grains, meadow flowers, and other raw materials from the earth's natural surfaces. These digital prints were specifically engineered so that the designs matched the contours and proportions of each individual garment, creating a seamless integration between printed pattern and garment form. One notable design featured flowers trapped beneath layers of tulle netting, evoking the appearance of specimens preserved in Victorian specimen jars. Delicate embroidered details included whitework embroidery and beetle-wing sequins, with some designs incorporating acid-yellow enameled buttercups on mini dresses as points of chromatic contrast.[2][1]

### Second Sequence: Technological Transformation

The collection's second sequence shifted toward harder, more angular designs representing the impact of human technology and industrialization on the natural world. While still drawing inspiration from natural forms—flowers, crystals, minerals—these designs were "engineered with a hard edge" to convey the "synthetic qualities associated with modernity and the human touch."[1][2]

Prints in this sequence became angular and geometric, incorporating imagery inspired by crushed crystal, metallic structures such as the Eiffel Tower, and natural landscapes transformed through the lens of industrial aesthetics. One particularly striking design featured a print depicting a diamond pattern that morphed into a human skeleton, directly addressing themes of mortality and human transformation. Bell jar–shaped dresses and bodysuits encrusted with jet, gold, and silver Swarovski crystals conveyed sharp, geometric lines in material form. The color palette incorporated vivid pinks, sapphires, and striking black and white combinations, with synthetic materials including Lycra and bonded leather replacing the natural fabrics of the first sequence.[2][1]

# Digital Print Technology

*Natural Dis-tinction Un-natural Selection* became notable for its innovative approach to digital textile printing at a time when the technology was still relatively new to haute couture. The collection demonstrated advanced technical applications of digital printing, wherein designs were created specifically to complement and interact with the cut and drape of each garment. Rather than printing standard patterns onto pre-cut fabric, McQueen's team engineered prints to account for how the fabric would move, fold, and sit on the human body, creating a unified aesthetic in which pattern and form functioned as integrated elements of design.[1]

This technical sophistication reflected McQueen's background in tailoring—he had trained as a tailor on Savile Row, London's historic center of bespoke men's tailoring—combined with his willingness to embrace emerging technological possibilities. The collection is credited with helping to popularize the use of digital prints in fashion more broadly, demonstrating the aesthetic and conceptual possibilities of the medium to the international fashion industry.[1]

# Critical Reception

Contemporary reviews of *Natural Dis-tinction Un-natural Selection* were mixed. Fashion critics and journalists who attended the show expressed divided opinions about the collection's success. Many in the fashion press responded more positively to the romantic aesthetic and craftsmanship evident in the first half of the presentation, with its emphasis on soft colors, organic forms, and delicate embellishment. Some critics expressed disappointment at the overall level of dramatic spectacle and theatrical elements, noting that the collection appeared less focused on the spectacular catwalk theatrics for which McQueen had become known in previous seasons.[1]

Despite the mixed immediate response, the collection's thematic ambition and technical innovation continued to be reassessed and appreciated by fashion historians and critics in subsequent years. The collection's exploration of environmental concerns and embrace of digital technology were recognized as visionary within the context of 2008–2009 fashion design, prefiguring broader industry trends toward technological innovation and thematic sophistication in haute couture.

## Legacy

McQueen continued to explore environmental themes and digital print technologies in his following two collections—*The Horn of Plenty* (Autumn/Winter 2009) and *Plato's Atlantis* (Spring/Summer 2010)—further developing the conceptual and technical approaches introduced in *Natural Dis-tinction Un-natural Selection*. His sustained engagement with these themes established him as a pioneering figure in the integration of cutting-edge digital technologies with conceptually sophisticated fashion design.[1]

McQueen's death in February 2010, shortly after completing *Plato's Atlantis*, ended his design career but did not diminish interest in his work. Pieces from *Natural Dis-tinction Un-natural Selection* are held in the permanent collections of major fashion museums, including the Victoria and Albert Museum in London and the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York. The collection has been included in retrospective exhibitions of his work, most notably in *Alexander McQueen: Savage Beauty*, which opened at the Metropolitan Museum of Art's Costume Institute in May 2011 and subsequently traveled to the Victoria and Albert Museum in London in 2015.[1]

The collection's pioneering integration of environmental themes, digital textile technology, and refined tailoring established *Natural Dis-tinction Un-natural Selection* as a significant work within McQueen's oeuvre and within the broader history of twenty-first-century fashion design.
