# Love, Anger, Madness

**Love, Anger, Madness** (French: *Amour, Colère et Folie*) is a 1968 collection of three linked novellas by Haitian author Marie Vieux-Chauvet. The work consists of three distinct but thematically connected narratives titled "Love," "Anger," and "Madness," each depicting different aspects of life under political oppression in Haiti. Published by Éditions Gallimard in Paris with the support of French feminist Simone de Beauvoir, the book was quickly suppressed in Haiti due to its critical portrayal of the Duvalier regime and did not reach a wider international audience until decades later. The work is now recognized as a significant contribution to Haitian literature and postcolonial studies.

# Publication and Suppression

Marie Vieux-Chauvet began writing the three novellas in 1964 and initially sent them to Simone de Beauvoir as separate manuscripts around 1967. Beauvoir, impressed by the work, recommended their publication as a single collection to the French publisher Éditions Gallimard, which published *Amour, Colère et Folie* in 1968. The collection was met with scorn from Haitian critics and sparked outrage among government authorities under dictator François Duvalier. Fearing retribution from the regime, Vieux-Chauvet's family, particularly her husband Pierre Chauvet who was in France at the time, intervened to suppress the book's circulation in Haiti. Pierre Chauvet purchased copies in the country and destroyed them to protect the family from political retaliation.[1][2][3]

After the initial suppression, the work remained largely unavailable for three decades. Following Vieux-Chauvet's death in 1973 from a brain tumor while in exile in New York, a pirated copy of the book eventually circulated among Haitian literary circles. The work was officially republished in France in 2005. In 2009, an English translation by Rose-Myriam Réjouis and Val Vinokur was published, making the work accessible to English-speaking audiences. This translation was subsequently released by Modern Library in 2010 with an introduction by Haitian-American author Edwidge Danticat.[1]

# Recognition and Awards

In 1986, thirteen years after Vieux-Chauvet's death, *Love, Anger, Madness* was awarded the Deschamps Prize, a Haitian literary award. This recognition helped establish the work's legitimacy within Haitian letters and contributed to renewed interest in Vieux-Chauvet's literary legacy. The 2009 English translation brought the work to international scholarly attention, and it has since been studied extensively in postcolonial literature courses and Haitian studies programs.[1]

# Content and Structure

*Love, Anger, Madness* is structured as a triptych of novellas that gradually increase in emotional intensity and psychological complexity. Each novella explores different dimensions of oppression under the Duvalier regime, though the historical setting is positioned during and after the American occupation of Haiti in the 1920s-1930s rather than the contemporary 1960s political moment.[1]

### Love

The first novella, "Love," is narrated through diary entries by Claire Clamont, the eldest of three sisters from an upper-class mulatto family. Claire has never married and lives a repressed emotional life, sharing her home with her younger sisters Annette and Félicia, and Félicia's husband Jean Luze. Claire harbors secret desire for Jean Luze, who becomes sexually involved with Annette while Félicia is pregnant. The family lives under constant surveillance and psychological terror inflicted by the local police chief, Commandant Calédu, who employs home searches, disappearances, murder, rape, and torture against the population. The narrative explores how familial jealousy, resentment, and suppressed sexuality interact with external political oppression, creating a toxic emotional environment shaped by class anxiety and colorism.[1]

### Anger

"Anger" (sometimes translated as "Wrath") shifts perspective to a middle-class family whose land is seized by paramilitary forces dressed in black uniforms who plant stakes marking the family's property as confiscated. The family members respond differently to this assault on their material security and dignity: the grandfather and disabled grandson express outrage, while the son and his wife respond with fearful passivity. The elder daughter Rose becomes the focus of state terror when one of the militia leaders compels her into a month-long sexual assault, which she experiences as a form of martyrdom. The novella is structured with the formal qualities of Greek tragedy, depicting how state-sanctioned violence fragments family bonds and produces cascading psychological damage. The narrative emphasizes class resentment as a motivator of the paramilitary violence, with the militia leader—referred to as "the Gorilla"—deriving pleasure from violating a young woman from the class that previously dominated him.[1]

### Madness

"Madness" is the shortest and most surreal of the three novellas. Set in a besieged town, it follows René, a poor poet who barricades himself in a family shack with three other poets as "devils"—the local paramilitary forces—rampage through the village, killing hundreds and depositing bodies at the church door. The four men survive for eight days on rum and syrup while reciting verse, their minds deteriorating under the combined effects of hunger, fear, and alcohol intoxication. René's experience of the siege is rendered in increasingly hallucinatory prose interspersed with poetry, blurring the line between external reality and internal psychological collapse. The novella ends ambiguously when René attempts to escape and repel the devils, but the "invasion" is ultimately revealed to be alcohol-induced delusion, with the body he believed to be human actually being a dead dog. The work suggests that under conditions of extreme oppression, the boundary between interior madness and objective political reality becomes indistinguishable.[1]

# Themes and Context

*Love, Anger, Madness* engages with several interconnected themes central to understanding late-twentieth-century Haiti. The work portrays how the Duvalier dictatorship, which governed Haiti from 1957 onward and intensified its authoritarian control through the 1960s, produced psychological and social fragmentation at every level of society. Vieux-Chauvet explores the complicity of the educated mulatto elite—her own social class—in perpetuating systems of oppression, depicting how class anxiety and colorism make the upper classes vulnerable to moral corruption and collaboration with tyranny.[1]

The novellas also examine gender and sexual violence as state mechanisms of control. In both "Love" and "Anger," female characters experience or witness sexual exploitation as part of the regime's power apparatus. The work suggests that patriarchal violence and state violence are interconnected systems that operate on similar psychological principles.[1]

Vodou, the religious and spiritual tradition of Haiti, appears throughout the work, particularly in "Madness" where the poets invoke the *loas* (spirits) during their siege. However, Vieux-Chauvet presents Vodou not as exoticized folklore but as an integral aspect of Haitian intellectual and spiritual life.[1]

# Literary Style and Reception

Vieux-Chauvet's prose style in *Love, Anger, Madness* has been characterized by scholars as theatrical and psychologically penetrating. The first novella uses intimate first-person narration to render Claire's neurotic interior life; the second employs a choral structure reminiscent of Greek drama to depict collective family trauma; and the third shifts into expressionist and hallucinatory modes as it charts psychological dissolution.[1]

The work was initially dismissed by Haitian literary critics as either too political or too psychologically disturbing. However, with renewed publication and translation, scholars have come to recognize *Love, Anger, Madness* as a masterwork of political fiction that captures the subjective experience of living under dictatorship with unprecedented psychological realism. Literary critic Edwidge Danticat has noted that the fictional siege in "Madness" parallels the historical experience of a group of Haitian writers called *Les Araignées du soir* (Evening Spiders), who continued to meet and produce work in semi-secrecy during Duvalier's repression, and that Vieux-Chauvet was the only woman among this literary collective.[1]

## Adaptations

Beninese playwright José Pliya adapted the novellas into a series of theatrical pieces between 2007 and 2008. Productions of "Amour" (Love) and "Colère" (Anger) were staged at L'Archipel in Guadeloupe in 2008, with a production of "Folie" (Madness) following in 2009.[1]

## Legacy and Significance

*Love, Anger, Madness* has exerted considerable influence on subsequent Haitian literature and diaspora writing. The work served as an inspiration for other Haitian writers, including Edwidge Danticat, whose own novels engage with similar themes of state violence, gender, and exile. Vieux-Chauvet's unflinching portrayal of complicity and psychological damage under dictatorship established a literary model for representing political trauma that extends beyond the Haitian context.[1]

The work's trajectory—from suppression and exile to rediscovery and international recognition—also illustrates the precarious status of politically engaged literature under authoritarian regimes and the importance of preserving and translating works that challenge dominant narratives.
