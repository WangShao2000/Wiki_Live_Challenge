# Red and Purple Modernization Project

The **Red and Purple Modernization** (**RPM**) **Project** is a multi-phase infrastructure reconstruction program coordinated by the Chicago Transit Authority (CTA) on Chicago's North Side, Evanston, and Wilmette. The project replaces aging infrastructure on the North Side main line north of Belmont station and the Evanston branch, known as the Purple Line. Both lines consist of elevated heavy rail infrastructure built between 1900 and 1912, with most of the section north of Wilson station elevated on embankments by 1931. The RPM initiative is part of the broader Red Ahead program, a comprehensive plan to maintain, modernize, and expand the Red Line.

The first phase of the project, completed in July 2025, addressed the most critical infrastructure challenges and included the construction of the Red-Purple Bypass to eliminate a century-old bottleneck at Clark Junction, the full reconstruction of four Red Line stations at Lawrence, Argyle, Berwyn, and Bryn Mawr into fully accessible facilities, and the replacement of 1.9 miles of deteriorated track structure. At a cost of $2.1 billion, Phase One represented the largest capital project in CTA history at its initiation. Future phases will extend modernization efforts further north along the main line to Howard station and along the Purple Line to Linden station in Wilmette.

# Background

The North Side main line of what is now the Red Line began operations on May 31, 1900, when the Northwestern Elevated Railroad Company opened service from the downtown Loop to Wilson Avenue.[1] The Ravenswood branch, now the Brown Line, opened in May 1907, connecting to the main line at Clark Junction, north of Belmont station.[1] In May 1908, the main line was extended north of Wilson Avenue along existing at-grade railroad trackage previously operated by the Chicago and Evanston Railroad to Central station in Evanston.[1] The extension to Linden Avenue in Wilmette was completed in April 1912.[1] From 1908 to 1931, both the North Side main line and the Evanston branch underwent gradual elevation from Wilson to Isabella stations on embankments built to safely accommodate heavy freight trains that operated on the same tracks.[1]

By the early 2010s, the northern sections of both lines faced significant deterioration and capacity challenges. The track and support structures had reached the end of their useful life, having exceeded their design expectations by decades. Structural steel suffered from rust, environmental corrosion, and degradation from stray electrical current.[2] The rail infrastructure supporting the elevated sections showed visible wear, with crumbling retaining walls and brittle viaducts requiring periodic CTA repairs.[2]

The most critical constraint was Clark Junction, the at-grade intersection where the Red, Purple, and Brown Lines merged. At this junction, northbound Brown Line trains crossing the path of Red and Purple Line trains caused regular delays. The junction could accommodate only 40 to 44 trains per hour in each direction, falling significantly short of demand on the CTA's busiest transit line. At least 40 percent of all trains traveling through Clark Junction experienced delays, and the speed-restricted curves near the junction forced trains to slow by an average of over one minute between Addison and Belmont stations.[3] These factors combined to cost Red and Purple Line riders over half a million extra travel hours annually.[3]

Additionally, most stations on the northern Red and Purple Lines lacked full ADA accessibility, predating the Americans with Disabilities Act by decades. Existing platforms were narrow and aging stations provided limited passenger amenities.

# Planning and Initial Vision Study

Early planning for modernization began in 2009 when the CTA commissioned a visioning study to assess conditions on the 9.6-mile corridor between Belmont and the northern terminals.[2] The study examined alternative approaches to address infrastructure deterioration, capacity constraints, and accessibility deficiencies. Following completion of the initial assessment, the CTA formally opted for comprehensive modernization of the corridor in 2011.[1] The environmental review process under the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA) commenced, culminating in the preparation of a Tier 1 Environmental Impact Statement addressing potential impacts across the entire planned project corridor.[4]

# Phase One

### Overview and Funding

Phase One construction began on October 2, 2019, and was substantially completed by July 20, 2025.[1] The $2.1 billion project was funded through a combination of federal and local sources: $957 million in federal Core Capacity funds from the Federal Transit Administration (FTA), a $125 million federal Congestion Mitigation and Air Quality Improvement (CMAQ) grant administered through the Chicago Metropolitan Agency for Planning (CMAP), $622 million in Transit Tax Increment Financing (TIF) funds from the City of Chicago, and CTA financing.[5]

The Chicago Transit Authority selected the joint venture team of Walsh Construction and Fluor Corporation (Walsh-Fluor) as the design-build contractor in December 2018, following an 18-month qualifications review.[6] The CTA issued the Notice to Proceed in February 2019.[7] The design-build approach enabled concurrent design and construction, facilitating complex sequencing requirements while minimizing service disruptions.

### Red-Purple Bypass

The Red-Purple Bypass Project consisted of construction of a new grade-separated junction just north of Belmont station to replace the 114-year-old Clark Junction where Red, Purple, and Brown Line trains had previously intersected at grade.[8] The bypass allowed northbound Brown Line trains from Kimball to cross above the Red and Purple Line tracks on a flying junction structure.[1] The bypass structure was completed and began service on November 19, 2021, representing the first new section of track added to the CTA system in 28 years.[8]

The bypass design incorporated a straddle bent intermediate support structure that straddled the Red-Purple Line tracks, permitting reasonable span lengths while maintaining necessary clearance over the Red-Purple Line and meeting grade limitations.[9] This structural approach avoided lateral track expansion that would have required more extensive reconstruction of surrounding infrastructure.

The Red-Purple Bypass eliminated a critical capacity bottleneck by allowing CTA to add up to eight additional Red Line trains per hour during rush periods, accommodating up to 7,200 additional passengers per hour during peak hours and increasing average train speeds through the intersection by 60 percent.[10] The improved service also saved customers an estimated half-million travel hours annually.[7]

### Track Structure Reconstruction

Following completion of the bypass, CTA proceeded with reconstruction of the Red and Purple Line track structure between Belmont and Newport/Cornelia. This section featured the four-track serpentine configuration on century-old embankments and elevated structures that required complete rebuilding. By May 2025, the elevated track reconstruction was completed along 1.9 miles of structure.[11] The new structures eliminated center-street columns that had previously obstructed sightlines for pedestrians and motorists and incorporated closed-deck design with noise walls to reduce street-level noise.[8]

Temporary track structures were constructed parallel to the existing tracks between Belmont and north of School Street to maintain service throughout the reconstruction period, with trains operating on these temporary tracks for the duration of Phase One construction.[7]

### Station Reconstruction

The four Red Line stations at Lawrence, Argyle, Berwyn, and Bryn Mawr underwent complete reconstruction into new, fully accessible facilities. Each station incorporated elevators and escalators at main entrances, wider platforms for enhanced safety and comfort, translucent overhead canopies for weather protection, upgraded signage and equipment, and improved seating arrangements.[12] The new stations were designed individually to reflect the character and identity of their respective communities.[12] The Vautravers Building, a 127-year-old historic greystone structure, was shifted approximately 30 feet west to allow for a straighter track alignment and improved operations while preserving the architectural landmark.[12] All four stations opened to the public in July 2025.[12]

### Signal System

A new automatic train protection signal system replaced the 60-year-old signal infrastructure across 23 track miles between Howard and Belmont stations. The new system functioned similarly to roadway traffic signals to enhance train flow, spacing, and service reliability. The signal system installation was ongoing throughout Phase One construction and was substantially completed by project conclusion.[13]

### Construction Methods and Community Considerations

The Walsh-Fluor Design-Build Team employed innovative construction techniques suited to the dense urban environment. A custom 285-foot overhead gantry crane, designated the "Windy City Gantry," installed precast concrete segments from above to minimize surface disruption.[12] A 55-ton Mi-Jack rubber-tired gantry was deployed to support top-down construction in spaces with as little as six inches of lateral clearance from adjacent buildings.[12] Construction sequencing was carefully planned to minimize service impacts to commuters while maintaining worker and public safety.

The project included several community benefits elements. Noise walls surrounding the new track structures incorporated wave patterns to soften their visual appearance, and tulip-designed columns with lighting and street pavers improved street-level aesthetics.[8] The CTA Building Small Businesses (BSB) Program, created in 2019 to support small and diverse contractors, secured $5 million in federal loans for local small businesses during 2020-2021 to facilitate their participation in RPM Phase One work.[14]

### Contractor Recognition

In December 2024, the Walsh-Fluor Design-Build Team was named Contractor of the Year by the Chicago Transit Authority for exceptional performance on the RPM Phase One Project.[15] The award recognized their completion of the four new stations, 1.9 miles of track structure replacement, and the new 23-track-mile signal system on schedule and budget.

# Future Phases

The completion of Phase One established the foundation for planned future phases to extend modernization further north along the Red and Purple Lines. The CTA initiated the RPM Next Phases Planning Study in 2021 to develop a comprehensive blueprint for subsequent modernization efforts.[5] The study, scheduled for completion in 2024, would assess infrastructure conditions, recommend priority projects and phasing approaches, and identify potential funding sources for implementation.[16]

Future phases would address the remaining deteriorated infrastructure and inaccessible stations between Cornelia and Howard on the main line (served by the Red Line and Purple Line Express), and along the Purple Line branch from Belmont to Linden in Wilmette. Specific stations identified for future reconstruction include Howard station (the major transfer hub for Red, Purple, and Yellow Lines), Central station in Evanston, Davis station (Purple Line), and other older stations lacking full ADA accessibility.[17] A future phase would also include rebuilding the transfer station at Howard and reorganizing train traffic to support same-level transfers between service lines.[18]

Future RPM phases were anticipated to be funded through a mix of federal, state, and local sources, pending funding availability and prioritization decisions.[5] Potential funding sources included the FTA's Capital Investment Grant program (Core Capacity), Transit Tax Increment Financing from participating municipalities, and state and local government contributions. As multiple municipalities would be served by future phases, planning had to account for TIF fund restrictions requiring that tax revenue generated within a municipality be used within that same municipality.[19]

# Integration with Red Ahead Program

The RPM Project operates as a major component of the CTA's broader Red Ahead initiative, a comprehensive program for maintaining, modernizing, and expanding the Red Line between the proposed 130th Street extension on the south and Linden terminal on the north.[20] Red Ahead encompasses multiple complementary projects: the RPM program for the northern sections, the Red Line Extension Project extending service south to 130th Street, and individual station renovations throughout the system. The interconnected nature of these projects reflected the strategy that improvements in any section of the Red Line benefited system-wide operations and ridership capacity.

# Project Significance

The RPM Project represented a generational infrastructure investment addressing a century of deferred maintenance on critical CTA infrastructure serving one of the nation's busiest transit lines. The Red Line carries approximately 40 million rides annually, representing the highest ridership on any CTA route.[21] By modernizing infrastructure that had far exceeded its design life and addressing longstanding capacity constraints and accessibility deficiencies, the project aimed to sustain reliable service for decades to come while enhancing the experience for millions of annual passengers.

The project's approach of phased implementation over multiple decades reflected the scale and complexity of replacing century-old infrastructure without interrupting service on the system's most utilized route. The successful completion of Phase One through 2025 positioned the CTA to pursue additional federal, state, and local funding for subsequent phases extending modernization through the remainder of the Red and Purple Line network on the North Side and into northern suburbs.
