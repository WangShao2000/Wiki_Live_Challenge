# Endorois case

The **Endorois case** is a landmark human rights decision by the African Commission on Human and Peoples' Rights (ACHPR) concerning the rights of the Endorois, an indigenous pastoralist community in Kenya. The case, formally titled *Centre for Minority Rights Development (Kenya) and Minority Rights Group International on behalf of Endorois Welfare Council v. Kenya*, was submitted to the ACHPR in 2003 and decided on 25 November 2009, with adoption by the African Union on 2 February 2010. The case has been recognized as a foundational decision in African and international human rights law for establishing the first African legal recognition of indigenous peoples' collective rights to ancestral lands and for issuing the first international ruling on the right to development.[1][2][3]

# Background

### The Endorois Community

The Endorois are a semi-nomadic pastoralist community of approximately 60,000 people who lived for centuries in the Lake Bogoria region of the Central Rift Valley in Kenya. Their way of life was inextricably linked to the land around Lake Bogoria, where they grazed cattle, sheep, and goats, and practiced beekeeping. The community relied on the fertile lands surrounding the lake, which provided green pastures and medicinal salt licks essential for raising healthy livestock. Lake Bogoria held profound religious and cultural significance for the Endorois, serving as the location of sacred prayer sites, circumcision ritual sites, and other ceremonial grounds used regularly throughout the year. The Endorois believed that the spirits of deceased community members resided in the lake and held annual festivals there with participation from Endorois across the region. The Monchongoi Forest was considered the birthplace of the Endorois people and the settlement of the first Endorois community.[4][5][6][7]

Under British colonial administration and continuing after Kenyan independence in 1963, the land around Lake Bogoria was held in trust for the Endorois community. The Endorois maintained customary ownership and use of the land without significant challenge until the 1970s.[8]

### Displacement and Initial Remedies

In November 1973, the Kenyan government gazzetted the Lake Bogoria area as a game reserve (initially called the Lake Hannington Game Reserve, later renamed the Lake Bogoria Game Reserve and re-gazetted in 1978) without consulting the Endorois community. The creation of the game reserve was intended to promote wildlife conservation and tourism development. Approximately 400 Endorois families were forcibly removed from their land beginning in 1973, with additional evictions occurring in 1983.[9][10]

When Kenyan Wildlife Service (KWS) officials informed Endorois elders of the eviction, they allegedly promised that 400 Endorois families would receive plots of fertile land as compensation, that the community would receive 25 percent of tourism revenue from the game reserve, that 85 percent of employment opportunities would go to the Endorois, and that the government would construct cattle dips and fresh water dams for the community. These promises were largely unfulfilled. By 1986, only 170 of the 400 families received minimal financial compensation, which officials characterized as relocation assistance rather than compensation for the loss of ancestral lands. The Endorois were relocated to semi-arid areas where the majority of their livestock did not survive, and where access to potable water remained limited.[11][12][13]

In 1986, the Endorois community petitioned President Daniel arap Moi for redress. Following a meeting with Endorois leaders in December 1994 at his Lake Bogoria Hotel, President Moi ordered that the government respect the 1973 compensation agreement and directed that 25 percent of annual income from the game reserve be allocated to community projects. However, these directives were not implemented. When the Endorois requested a follow-up meeting in 1995, President Moi again ordered implementation of his directives, but government compliance remained minimal.[14]

In response to the government's failure to implement presidential directives, the Endorois filed a case with the High Court of Kenya in 2000. The High Court decided the case on 19 April 2002, ruling in favor of the government. The court found that the procedures for establishing the game reserve had been followed properly and that the minimal compensation paid in 1986 fulfilled any government obligation to the Endorois. The High Court explicitly stated that it could not address the question of a community's collective right to property, focusing instead on individual claimants. The court concluded that Kenyan law did not provide special protection for land claims based on historical occupation and cultural rights.[15][16][17]

### Additional Grievances

After the High Court decision, conditions for the Endorois deteriorated further. In 2002, the Kenyan government began granting concessions to private ruby mining companies to extract minerals from Endorois ancestral lands. This mining activity created significant risks of contaminating water sources used by the Endorois for drinking and livestock watering. Additionally, the government demarcated and sold portions of Endorois land to third parties without consultation with the community.[18][19]

# Proceeding Before the African Commission

### Filing and Admissibility

On 22 May 2003, the Centre for Minority Rights Development (CEMIRIDE), a Kenyan civil rights organization, filed a formal letter of intent with the African Commission on Human and Peoples' Rights, submitting a communication on behalf of the Endorois Welfare Council (EWC). The communication was supported by Minority Rights Group International (MRG), a London-based international organization focused on minority and indigenous rights, and received an amicus curiae brief from the Centre on Housing Rights and Evictions (CORE). The complainants alleged that Kenya had violated Articles 8, 14, 17, 21, and 22 of the African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights through its forced removal of the Endorois, failure to provide adequate compensation, and denial of participation in decisions affecting their land.[20][21][22]

The Kenyan government initially failed to submit written responses on admissibility despite repeated reminders from the African Commission. At its 37th Ordinary Session in May 2005, the ACHPR declared the communication admissible, finding that the Endorois community had exhausted available domestic remedies and that the case met the admissibility criteria under Article 56 of the African Charter. The decision on admissibility was issued despite Kenya's failure to cooperate with the procedural requirements.[23]

### Consideration of Merits

Following the admissibility decision, both parties submitted arguments on the merits of the case. The African Commission heard oral testimony from expert witnesses and representatives of both parties at its 40th Ordinary Session in November 2006. The case involved complex questions about the nature of indigenous peoples' rights, the definition of the "right to development," and the extent to which international human rights law protected collective land rights. Consideration of the merits was deferred through subsequent sessions before the African Commission rendered its final decision in November 2009 at its 46th Ordinary Session.[24][25]

# Decision

### Recognition of Endorois as a "People"

A foundational aspect of the ACHPR's decision was its recognition of the Endorois as a distinct indigenous "people" entitled to bring collective claims for rights under the African Charter. Kenya argued that the Endorois were merely a sub-tribe of the larger Tugen or Kalenjin groups and therefore not entitled to protection as a distinct people under the Charter. The ACHPR rejected this argument, finding that the Endorois had satisfied the criteria for recognition as an indigenous people: they had a continuous historical presence in the Lake Bogoria region, maintained a distinct cultural identity and pastoralist way of life dependent on the land, had a traditional system of governance through elders and the EWC, and had been marginalized in relation to state power. The commission relied on precedent from the Ogoni case (*The Social and Economic Rights Action Centre for Economic and Social Rights v. Nigeria*), which established that indigenous peoples and other communities could bring collective claims before the ACHPR.[26][27][28]

### Violations Found

The ACHPR found that Kenya violated five articles of the African Charter:

**Article 8 (Right to Practice Religion)**: The commission ruled that Kenya violated the Endorois' right to freedom of conscience and religion by denying the community access to Lake Bogoria and surrounding sacred sites essential to their religious practices. The commission rejected Kenya's argument that restricting access was necessary for conservation purposes, finding no significant public interest justification that would warrant such a comprehensive denial of religious practice. The commission noted that the religious sites had not been properly demarcated or protected within the game reserve.[29]

**Article 14 (Right to Property)**: The ACHPR recognized that the Endorois' ancestral land held under the Kenyan Constitution's trust land system constituted "property" within the meaning of the African Charter, even though ownership was not documented through written title. The commission found that Kenya violated this right by encroaching on Endorois property without a legal basis proportionate to any legitimate government objective. The commission held that the government had failed to adequately negotiate with the Endorois before limiting their access and had provided compensation that was "wholly inadequate" and "[flew] in the face of common sense and fairness." The commission also found that Kenya's eviction of the Endorois violated the requirement of legality and proportionality, as conservation of the game reserve could have been achieved through the involvement of the Endorois rather than their removal.[30][31]

**Article 17 (Right to Culture)**: The commission found that Kenya violated the Endorois' right to develop, protect, and promote their culture by removing them from the land and severely restricting their access to sites essential to their cultural identity and traditional pastoralist way of life. The commission noted that the African Charter requires states to provide for "the conservation, development and diffusion of culture" without exceptions that would allow eviction of indigenous communities.

**Article 21 (Right to Free Disposition of Natural Resources)**: The ACHPR found that Kenya violated the Endorois' right to use and benefit from their natural resources by restricting their access to salt licks, pastures, and other resources on their ancestral land. The commission ruled that the restriction was not in the public interest, as a meaningful way to achieve conservation would have been to involve the Endorois, who, as inhabitants of the land, would be "best equipped to maintain its delicate ecosystems." The commission found that Kenya had also failed to provide the Endorois with any benefit from economic activities on their land, including tourism revenue and ruby mining concessions.

**Article 22 (Right to Development)**: The ACHPR's analysis of the right to development was landmark, as it represented the first international human rights tribunal decision finding a violation of this right. The commission formulated a definition of the right to development as both "constitutive and instrumental," meaning it must satisfy tests of both procedural and substantive law. The commission held that development activities on Endorois land violated this right because: the Endorois were not consulted or granted free, prior, and informed consent; they had no meaningful participation in decisions affecting the land; and the development resulted in harm rather than empowerment of the community, causing loss of access to resources necessary for their economic and social development. The commission rejected Kenya's argument that indirect participation through the democratic system was sufficient, finding that meaningful participation required direct dialogue with and explicit consent from the affected community.

### Remedies

The ACHPR recommended that the Kenyan government take the following actions:

1. Recognize the Endorois' ownership of their ancestral lands and ensure their restitution with legal title and clear demarcation.
2. Ensure the Endorois have unrestricted access to Lake Bogoria for religious ceremonies, cultural practices, and grazing of livestock.
3. Provide adequate compensation to the community for all losses suffered through the loss of property, disruption of pastoral enterprise, and denial of religious and cultural rights.
4. Pay royalties to the Endorois from economic activities on the land, including tourism revenue and any mining operations.
5. Register the Endorois Welfare Council as a representative organization.
6. Engage in dialogue with the Endorois community to discuss implementation of these remedies and submit a report on implementation within three months.

The ACHPR offered its good offices to facilitate implementation of its decision.

# Significance and Impact

### Precedent for Indigenous Rights

The Endorois case is widely regarded as a watershed moment for indigenous rights in Africa. It was the first decision by an African human rights body to legally recognize indigenous peoples' collective rights over traditionally owned land. The decision affirmed that indigenous communities could hold land rights even in the absence of written documentation and that customary tenure systems were entitled to protection under international human rights law. The case established that states must obtain free, prior, and informed consent from indigenous peoples before undertaking development projects or extracting resources from their lands.

### Right to Development

The ACHPR's articulation of the right to development was landmark in global human rights law. Before the Endorois decision, the right to development had been recognized in international instruments such as the UN Declaration on the Right to Development, but had not been the subject of a binding judicial decision by an international tribunal. The Endorois case established important principles: that development must be participatory and benefit the affected community; that development cannot be imposed without the consent of indigenous peoples; and that the right to development includes both procedural components (consultation and participation) and substantive components (ensuring that development actually empowers communities rather than harming them).

The decision has influenced subsequent cases on indigenous land rights in Kenya and across Africa, including the Ogiek case against Kenya. Both the Endorois and Ogiek decisions established precedent that conservation and development objectives do not justify the denial of indigenous peoples' rights to their ancestral lands and resources.

### Constitutional Impact

The ACHPR's decision contributed to the debate surrounding Kenya's constitutional reform process. In 2010, Kenya adopted a new constitution that recognized collective land rights and indigenous peoples' rights. The National Guidelines for Free, Prior, and Informed Consent, issued by Kenya's Ministry of Environment in 2016, explicitly reflected the ACHPR's jurisprudence from the Endorois case.

### International Advocacy

The case generated significant international attention and advocacy concerning indigenous land rights. The decision was adopted by the African Union and endorsed by various United Nations human rights bodies. In 2016, the UN Committee on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights expressed concern in its Concluding Observations on Kenya that the government had not implemented the Endorois decision and recommended immediate implementation. In 2017, the UN Committee on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination similarly called upon Kenya to ensure legal recognition of the Endorois' collective rights and to implement the African Commission's decision.

# Implementation

### Limited Compliance

Despite the ACHPR's clear recommendations and the African Union's adoption of the decision, Kenya's implementation has been limited. As of 2024, the Kenyan government had not fully complied with the decision's core requirements.

Access and Management: The Endorois have been granted limited access to Lake Bogoria without entrance fees, but access remains discretionary and is managed by the game reserve authorities rather than being an assured right. In 2011, Lake Bogoria was designated as a UNESCO World Heritage Site without consultation with the Endorois, which prompted advocacy and a State of Conservation report from the World Heritage Committee in 2014 requiring the inclusion of the Endorois in management and benefit-sharing arrangements. Following advocacy efforts, the Endorois signed a memorandum of understanding with the Kenya Wildlife Service and Baringo County Council recognizing Lake Bogoria as Endorois ancestral land and requiring Endorois inclusion in land management decisions. However, implementation of this memorandum in practice has been incomplete.

Compensation: The government has provided limited financial compensation and revenue sharing, but not at the levels recommended by the ACHPR. The Endorois were promised 25 percent of tourism revenue, but revenue-sharing arrangements remain far below this level and are subject to government discretion.

Land Restitution: The Endorois have not been granted full ownership of their ancestral lands or complete restitution, despite the clear recommendation in the ACHPR decision. Some land has been allocated to the Endorois for specific purposes, such as a cultural center at the entrance to the reserve, but full recognition of ancestral land rights has not occurred.

### Advocacy and International Pressure

Since the 2009 decision, civil society organizations and indigenous rights advocates have pursued ongoing advocacy to pressure the Kenyan government to implement the ACHPR's recommendations. The African Commission issued a resolution in 2013 calling on Kenya to implement the Endorois decision and faced criticism for lack of government compliance. In 2014, Kenya established a government task force to study implementation of the Endorois decision with an initial one-year mandate, though this task force did not lead to comprehensive implementation. Organizations including Minority Rights Group International, the Centre for Minority Rights Development, the Kenya Human Rights Commission, and the Endorois Welfare Council have coordinated advocacy efforts, conducted community workshops, and worked with the ACHPR and United Nations bodies to press for implementation.

The Endorois community has continued to face challenges with implementation efforts. As of 2025, fourteen years after the ACHPR decision, many of the commission's core recommendations remained unfulfilled, including full land restitution, adequate compensation, and assured revenue sharing.

### Related Cases

The precedent established in the Endorois case influenced subsequent indigenous land rights litigation in Africa. The Ogiek case, brought against Kenya for the eviction of the Ogiek people from the Mau Forest, was directly informed by the Endorois decision. In 2017, the African Court of Human and Peoples' Rights issued a decision on the Ogiek case recognizing the Ogiek's rights to their ancestral lands, and in 2022, the African Court ordered Kenya to pay reparations to the Ogiek. Both the Endorois and Ogiek decisions have become reference points for indigenous peoples' land rights litigation across the African continent.
