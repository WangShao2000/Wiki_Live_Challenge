# Dulles International Airport Main Terminal

The **Dulles International Airport Main Terminal** is the primary passenger terminal at Washington Dulles International Airport, located in Loudoun and Fairfax counties in Virginia, approximately 26 miles west of downtown Washington, D.C. The original structure, completed in 1962 and measuring 600 feet (180 m) long and 150 feet (46 m) wide, was designed by Finnish-American architect Eero Saarinen as the first terminal at the airport. The terminal underwent substantial expansion in the 1990s, with annexes added to the west and east bringing its total length to 1,240 feet (380 m), slightly exceeding Saarinen's originally planned dimensions. The structure is operated by the Metropolitan Washington Airports Authority (MWAA), which manages Dulles Airport as a whole.[1][2][3][4][5]

The Main Terminal is recognized for its distinctive mid-century modern architectural design, characterized by a suspended cable roof supported by dramatically sloping concrete columns spaced 40 feet apart. The catenary roof structure creates a column-free interior space and has been compared to a "huge, continuous hammock suspended between concrete trees," a description Saarinen himself used. The building was designated to the National Register of Historic Places in 1978 and received a First Honor Award from the American Institute of Architects in 1966.[6][7][8][9]

# Design and Planning

In May 1958, the Civil Aeronautics Administration (CAA) selected Eero Saarinen of Bloomfield Hills, Michigan, to design the main terminal and control tower for Dulles Airport. The 1950 Washington Airport Act had provided federal funding for the airport's construction, and land acquisition was completed in January 1958. Saarinen collaborated with structural engineering firm Ammann & Whitney (who led the airport's overall design), mechanical engineering firm Burns & McDonnell, and airport consultant Charles Landrum to develop the terminal design.[10][11][12]

Saarinen initially sketched his concepts on legal paper. During the design phase, Boyd Anderson of Ammann & Whitney proposed a system using suspension cables between ground and roof; after Saarinen approved this concept, the design team refined it into a cable-supported roof system. The CAA reviewed Saarinen's preliminary ideas in October 1958, and the Federal Aviation Agency (FAA) announced the final design in January 1960.[13][14]

Saarinen's design philosophy reflected his belief that the terminal should serve as a monumental gateway to the nation's capital while symbolizing the jet age. He wanted the structure to express movement and the excitement of travel while maintaining compatibility with Washington's classical architectural tradition. The plan called for a 600-by-150-foot double-level structure with concrete piers supporting a concave curved roof, designed with the flexibility to double in size.[15][16]

# Architectural Features

The Main Terminal's most distinctive feature is its suspended catenary roof system. Concrete roof panels are suspended between steel cables that span the width of the building, running from concrete piers on the north side to piers on the south side. The north-side columns, rising 65 feet above the landside (passenger approach), are taller than the 40-foot south-side columns on the airfield side, creating the catenary curve visible in the building's silhouette.[17][18]

The parallel slanted colonnades on the terminal's north and south facades are spaced 40 feet apart. These columns taper and lean outward, with their bases flaring purely for decorative effect. The outward lean serves a structural function, allowing the cantilever columns to resist the tension from the suspended cable roof. Large glass walls fill the voids between the columns, providing transparency and allowing views between the landside and airfield approaches.[19][20][21]

The interior was originally divided into two levels to separate passenger flows. The upper level accommodated departing passengers with ticketing counters, baggage facilities, and concessions. The lower level served arriving passengers with baggage claim and ground transportation areas. The interior bays measure 40 by 150 feet (12 by 46 m), spanning the terminal's entire width. Each bay was designed to accommodate a ticket counter, baggage facility, concessions, and loading points for mobile lounges.[22][23]

# Mobile Lounge System

A key innovation in the terminal's design was the integration of mobile lounges, self-powered elevated vehicles designed to transport passengers directly between the terminal and aircraft parked on the apron. Saarinen and his colleagues promoted the system through a promotional film created by designers Ray and Charles Eames, who were also friends of Saarinen. The mobile lounges eliminated the need for traditional finger-like concourses and allowed aircraft to park at remote locations, reducing the size and cost of the main building.[24][25][26]

The system originally included 24 mobile-lounge gates, with each lounge capable of carrying up to 90 passengers. The lounges would ferry passengers from the terminal to planes approximately 15 minutes before departure, enabling efficient aircraft positioning on the apron. Though the concept was considered revolutionary, adoption of mobile lounges never became widespread in the aviation industry due to labor costs and changing passenger preferences, though the system remains in limited use at Dulles.[27][28][29]

# Construction

The Corbetta Construction Company of New York City won the construction contract with a bid of $4,432,000 in early 1960. Site preparation began in April 1960, with workers grading and laying foundations. By June 1961, the airport's budget had grown significantly, doubling from an original $85 million to $175 million, prompting a reduction in the planned terminal length from 1,200 feet to 600 feet.[30][31][32]

The structural work proceeded rapidly, with concrete for the lower level poured and steel framework erected for the columns by October 1960. Construction challenges included the fabrication of the sloped glass wall panels, which required custom manufacturing to accommodate their outward slope. Workers developed a custom protractor tool to cut the glass accurately, and gaskets were fitted around each panel to prevent tipping. During construction of the foundation, workers poured concrete around multiple tons of steel rebar within each column, with workers overseeing the process until the concrete nearly filled the form before removing the metal cage.[33][34][35]

Eero Saarinen died on September 1, 1961, at age 51 while undergoing surgery for a brain tumor in Ann Arbor, Michigan, and never saw his completed design. Following his death, contractors accelerated work, implementing double shifts from April 1962 onward after FAA administrator Najeeb Halaby threatened penalties for missing an October deadline. Humphreys & Harding installed the terminal's interior finishes, including distinctive terrazzo flooring throughout the facility.[36][37][38]

# Opening and Early Operations

The airport and Main Terminal were formally dedicated on November 17, 1962, in a ceremony attended by President John F. Kennedy and former President Dwight D. Eisenhower, with an estimated 60,000 people present. Kennedy praised Saarinen's "distinguished ornament of a great country," while noting the appropriateness of naming the airport after Secretary of State John Foster Dulles. Commercial operations began on November 19, 1962, with Braniff, Delta, Eastern, Northwest, and TWA serving as the first airlines to operate from the terminal.[39][40][41]

On its first full Sunday of operation, the terminal processed approximately 25,000 travelers, though 15% of the work remained incomplete at the time of opening. The terminal's innovative design generated substantial recognition, earning a First Honor Award from the American Institute of Architects in 1966 for its "vigorous, free and graceful movement which we associate with flight, while avoiding literal and obvious analogy."[42][43]

## Later Expansions

Following initial construction, the terminal received several expansions to accommodate growing passenger traffic. In 1978, the FAA hired Frank Briscoe Co. to expand the structure, with work commencing that September. Two small annexes measuring approximately 50 feet (15 m) wide, designed in a style similar to the original terminal, were added along the southern facade to provide additional mobile-lounge gates. These were designed by Hellmuth, Obata and Kassabaum (HOK).[44][45][46]

In the 1990s, as passenger traffic increased substantially, the Metropolitan Washington Airports Authority commissioned Skidmore, Owings and Merrill (SOM) to develop a comprehensive expansion plan. The redesigned annexes, created by architects David Childs, Michael McCarthy, and Marilyn Taylor of SOM, more than doubled the terminal's length while respecting Saarinen's original design language. The expansion, completed between 1995 and 1997, extended the terminal to 1,240 feet (380 m)—exactly Saarinen's originally planned length before budget constraints forced its truncation. The expansion replicated the distinctive catenary cable roof structure, using concrete finishes, window patterns, and terrazzo floors consistent with the original building.[47][48][49]

An international arrivals addition was constructed in 1989, with a further expansion of the international arrivals facility completed in 2009, adding 41,400 square feet (3,850 m²) of space for customs and immigration processing with capacity for 2,400 arriving passengers per hour. In September 2009, a new central security checkpoint was added on a security mezzanine level, accommodating both standard security screening and TSA PreCheck operations, with direct connections to the AeroTrain people-mover system.[50][51]

## Current Operations and Facilities

The Main Terminal currently contains ticketing, baggage claim, U.S. Customs and Border Protection facilities, security screening areas, and passenger support facilities. The building includes Concourse Z, consisting of four gates (Z6, Z7, Z9, and Z10) equipped with jet bridges, primarily serving regional carriers Air Canada Express and Frontier Airlines. Additionally, the terminal includes "H" gates serving as waiting areas for airlines utilizing mobile lounges to reach aircraft parked at hard-stand locations.[52][53]

The terminal features a two-basement level structure below the main concourse. The upper basement level houses security screening facilities and the AeroTrain station, while the lower basement contains further passenger processing areas. In January 2010, the AeroTrain automated people-mover system, a 3.78-mile (6.08 km) underground system costing approximately $1.4 billion, began operations, transporting passengers between the Main Terminal and Concourses A, B, and C. Mobile lounges continue to operate for international arrivals and service to Concourse D, with the fleet currently used for specialized functions rather than routine passenger movement.[54][55][56]

## Architectural Significance and Heritage

The Main Terminal was recognized by the American Institute of Architects in 1966 for its design concept, specifically praising the suspended catenary roof that provides a wide enclosed area unimpeded by columns. The building was determined eligible for the National Register of Historic Places in 1978, acknowledging its historical importance and great artistic value.[57][58]

Saarinen's design has exerted considerable influence on airport terminal design worldwide. The original terminal at Taiwan Taoyuan International Airport was modeled after Saarinen's Dulles design, and the terminal influenced later notable works by architects including Renzo Piano and Santiago Calatrava. The Main Terminal has been featured in numerous films, including Die Hard 2 (1990), Airport (1970), The X-Files, and Body of Lies (2008).[59][60]

Saarinen considered the Dulles terminal his finest achievement, stating that "I think this airport is the best thing I have done. Maybe it will even explain what I believe about architecture." The design has been described as a "Jet-Age Parthenon," combining modernist engineering with classical architectural gestures to create a structure that functions as both a practical transportation hub and an enduring symbol of postwar American design.[61][62]
