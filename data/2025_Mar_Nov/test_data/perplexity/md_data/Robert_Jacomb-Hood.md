# Robert Jacomb-Hood

Robert Jacomb-Hood MICE (25 January 1822 – 10 May 1900) was a British civil engineer associated with the development of the London, Brighton and South Coast Railway (LB&SCR) and a range of other railway and civil engineering projects in Britain and overseas. Over a career of nearly sixty years, he worked as a resident engineer and later as a consulting engineer and company director, contributing to the construction and improvement of stations, branch lines, harbour works, and other infrastructure. He also served for many years on the board of the LB&SCR, maintaining a long connection with the company in both technical and managerial roles.[1]

# Early life and education

Robert Jacomb-Hood was born on 25 January 1822 at Riseley, Bedfordshire, the eldest son of Robert Jacomb, who later assumed the additional surname Hood after inheriting the Bardon Park estate in Leicestershire. He received his early education at Christ’s Hospital in London.[2][1]

In accordance with his father’s wishes, Jacomb-Hood was prepared for a legal career and entered Trinity College, Cambridge, keeping several terms there in 1840 and 1841 with a view to being called to the Bar. During this period, however, he came into contact with young engineers engaged in railway construction in the Midlands, which encouraged him to pursue engineering rather than law.[1]

After his father’s initial objections were overcome, he left the path toward the Bar and in 1841 became an articled pupil of George Watson Buck, then chief engineer—alongside Robert Stephenson—of the Manchester and Birmingham Railway.[1]

# Early engineering career

### Training under George Watson Buck

Under Buck’s supervision, Jacomb-Hood was first employed as an assistant on sections of the Manchester and Birmingham line. On the Holmes Chapel length, he worked under William Baker, who later became chief engineer of the London and North Western Railway, and on the Crewe length he served under William Henry Barlow, a future president of the Institution of Civil Engineers.[1]

When the line opened through to Crewe in 1843, Buck left the company for private practice in Manchester and engaged Jacomb-Hood as his chief assistant. In that capacity, Jacomb-Hood worked on projects including the rebuilding of the New Bailey Bridge at Salford.[1]

### Roles on Manchester-area lines

Jacomb-Hood subsequently rejoined the engineering staff of the Manchester and Birmingham Railway as an assistant to William Baker, being appointed to the construction of the Macclesfield branch. Between 1844 and 1846 he acted as resident engineer on the Manchester, South Junction and Altrincham Railway, overseeing works on that suburban line.[1]

# London, Brighton and South Coast Railway

### Resident engineer

In 1846 Jacomb-Hood was appointed resident engineer of the newly formed London, Brighton and South Coast Railway, taking charge of the company’s entire system. The appointment marked the start of a connection with the LB&SCR that would last, in various capacities, for more than fifty years.[1]

In the early years of his tenure he was responsible for completing stations and works already in progress, arranging maintenance contracts, and undertaking new construction for the company. Among the principal works during this period were:[1]

- The construction of the new London Bridge station for LB&SCR traffic.[1]
- The Bricklayers Arms goods station, an important freight facility in south London.[1]
- Improvements and extensions to the company’s network of branches, including the lines to Newhaven, Eastbourne and Hailsham, Epsom, Croydon and Wandsworth, Littlehampton, and Steyning.[1]

Between about 1850 and 1860 the LB&SCR concentrated on expanding and improving its infrastructure, and Jacomb-Hood was responsible for planning and executing a series of new lines and station enlargements. Works of this period included:[1]

- The Crystal Palace branch, serving the relocated Crystal Palace in south London.[1]
- Branch lines to East Grinstead and Midhurst.[1]
- The Lewes and Uckfield and the Shoreham and Henfield routes.[1]
- The Victoria Station project in London, where the LB&SCR was one of the partner companies.[1]
- Enlargement works at Brighton station, the company’s principal coastal terminus.[1]

The volume of parliamentary and engineering work associated with these schemes led Jacomb-Hood to resign the post of resident engineer in September 1860, after which he retained responsibility for the company’s parliamentary and new works as a consulting engineer while entering private practice in Westminster.[1]

### Board membership

In March 1883, following the retirement of Sir Arthur Otway, Jacomb-Hood was invited to join the board of the LB&SCR. He accepted a seat as a director, thereby renewing his formal connection with the company. For the last seventeen years of his life he devoted most of his professional effort to the railway’s affairs, applying his technical and managerial experience to the company’s business.[1]

He remained a director until his death in 1900, by which time he had been associated with the LB&SCR, in engineering and later in governance, for more than half a century.[1]

# Consulting engineer and private practice

### British railway projects

After stepping down as resident engineer in 1860, Jacomb-Hood established himself as a consulting engineer in Westminster, continuing to undertake work both for the LB&SCR and for other clients.[1]

In the 1860s he was responsible for the design and construction of a number of lines connected with or working in association with the LB&SCR, including:[3][1]

- The Mid-Sussex and South London lines.  
- The Croydon and Balham Railway.  
- The Horsham and Guildford Railway.  
- The Horsham and Dorking Railway.  
- The Seaford extension.  
- The Brighton, Uckfield and Tunbridge Wells route.  
- The Bognor branch.

In 1863, working with George Parker Bidder, he prepared schemes for the Peckham and Sutton and Leatherhead and Dorking lines, as well as for Newhaven harbour improvements. During the same period he was involved in promoted schemes for a number of other railways and tramways, including the Hadlow line, the East London Railways, the Axminster and Lyme line, the Southend and Maldon line, the Ouse Valley and Hastings line, the Kingsland and Finsbury line, and tramways at Southsea.[1]

### Partnership with William Jacomb

In 1865 Jacomb-Hood formed a partnership with his cousin and former pupil, William Jacomb. Under the name of their joint practice they promoted and deposited plans for schemes in several parts of Britain, among them:[1]

- The Surrey and Sussex Railway.  
- The Hornsey and Kingsland line.  
- The West Kent Railway.  
- The Chichester and Midhurst line.  
- The Peckham and Dartford line.  
- The Norwood and Crystal Palace High Level line.  
- A branch line from Bridport to Lyme Regis.

Engineering work undertaken after the formation of the partnership included detailed design of the ironwork for the National Gallery in London and the rebuilding of Portcreek Viaduct on the route to Portsmouth.[1]

The partnership ended in 1870 when William Jacomb was appointed chief engineer of the London and South Western Railway, after which Jacomb-Hood continued to practise independently.[1]

# Involvement with the Crystal Palace and other companies

In 1869 Jacomb-Hood became a director of the Crystal Palace Company, which operated the Crystal Palace and its associated attractions at Sydenham. He played an active part in the construction and organisation of the Crystal Palace Aquarium and participated more broadly in the company’s affairs until resigning from the board in 1879.[1]

In 1870 he joined the board of the Anglo-Maltese Hydraulic Dock Company, being called upon on several occasions to investigate and report on the company’s undertakings at Malta.[1]

From the 1870s onward he also held directorships in a number of other enterprises, often connected with railways, shipping, or utilities. These included:[1]

- The Alabama Great Southern Railway Company.  
- The Thames Haven Petroleum Storage Company.  
- The New Gas Company.  
- The Sydney and Louisburg Railway and Coal Company.  
- The Assam Railways and Trading Company.

His later career was thus divided between consulting work and board-level involvement in railway and related undertakings in Britain and overseas.[1]

# Overseas work

### South America and the United States

In 1872 Jacomb-Hood was commissioned by Samuel Laing, chairman of the LB&SCR, to travel to South America in order to negotiate a contract for the Córdoba and Tucumán Railway in Argentina.[1]

A few years later he was engaged by the banking house of Erlanger and Company to examine the property and finances of the Alabama and Chattanooga Railroad in the United States, which was being considered for purchase. His investigations contributed to the decision to form the Alabama Great Southern Railway Company in 1877, and he became a director of the new company, serving on its board until 1886.[1]

In 1879 Erlanger and Company again employed him to investigate railway and other undertakings in the West Indies and the southern United States.[1]

### India and other interests

As a director and consulting engineer, Jacomb-Hood was also associated through the Assam Railways and Trading Company with railway and commercial development in north-eastern India. His responsibilities there combined technical assessment with oversight of commercial operations.[1]

## Landownership and Bardon Park

Beyond his engineering and corporate roles, Jacomb-Hood was a landowner. Through his father’s inheritance he was associated with Bardon Park, a former deer park of about 1,500 acres forming part of the manor of Whitwick in Charnwood Forest, Leicestershire.[2][1]

By the mid-nineteenth century the Bardon estate included valuable deposits of hard igneous rock. In 1858, acting as owner of Bardon Park, Jacomb-Hood granted Ellis and Everard—a partnership of coal merchants and quarry operators—a lease of approximately 40 acres within the park for the quarrying of what was known locally as “Bardon Good Rock”. The terms included an annual rent of £145 and royalties of 1 shilling and 5 pence per tonne of stone extracted. The lease area was adjacent to Bardon Hill station on the Leicester and Swannington Railway, providing a direct rail connection via a siding into the quarry.[2]

The arrangement enabled Ellis and Everard to develop Bardon Hill Quarry as a source of aggregate for road construction and railway ballast, and by about 1860 the quarry rights extended over nearly 300 acres of the estate, although only a small portion had been worked. The subsequent growth of Bardon Hill Quarry contributed to the large-scale exploitation of Charnwood “granite” as a roadstone in the later nineteenth century.[2]

After Jacomb-Hood’s death in 1860, the Bardon estate was sold in 1864 to William Perry Herrick of the Beaumanor estate, who continued to lease quarrying rights to Ellis and Everard and their successors.[2]

## Professional recognition and writings

Jacomb-Hood was elected a member of the Institution of Civil Engineers (ICE) on 2 March 1847, making him one of the earlier members of the institution from the railway profession.[1]

He contributed technical papers to the ICE and received formal recognition from the institution. In 1850 he was awarded a Council Premium of books for a paper entitled “Description of the Lift Bridge over the Grand Surrey Canal, on the line of the Thames Junction Branch of the London, Brighton and South Coast Railway”. In 1858 he received a Telford Medal for his paper “The Arrangement and Construction of Railway Stations”, reflecting his experience in planning and designing station layouts and associated works.[1]

## Personal life

Jacomb-Hood came from a family whose surname combined “Jacomb” and “Hood”, a usage that originated when his father, born Robert Jacomb, inherited the Bardon estate on condition of adopting the additional surname. Through this connection the family became associated with Bardon Park and with landownership in Leicestershire.[3][2][1]

He married and had several children; one of his sons was the painter and illustrator George Percy Jacomb-Hood (1857–1929), who later wrote of his family background. George Percy recalled his father’s work as engineer to the LB&SCR and his own childhood in Redhill, Surrey, where the family lived during part of Robert’s railway career.[4][3]

## Death

On 10 May 1900, while at Tunbridge Wells and still active in LB&SCR business, Jacomb-Hood died suddenly in what contemporaries described as nearly full vigour of health. He was 78 years old. At the time of his death he had been engaged earlier in the day on company matters, and he remained a serving director of the LB&SCR.[1]

An obituary published by the Institution of Civil Engineers later that year summarised his career as spanning early work on the Manchester and Birmingham Railway, long service with the LB&SCR, a varied private practice, and numerous directorships, and emphasised his continued involvement in railway affairs up to his death.[1]

## Legacy

Jacomb-Hood’s contributions to nineteenth‑century British railway development lay both in engineering design and in corporate governance. As resident engineer of the LB&SCR he helped to consolidate the company’s early infrastructure and to lay out important branches and London terminal works. As a consulting engineer he took part in the planning of railway schemes across southern England and in harbour and structural work, including Newhaven harbour improvements and the ironwork for the National Gallery.[1]

Internationally, his advisory and directorial roles in undertakings such as the Córdoba and Tucumán Railway and the Alabama Great Southern Railway connected British engineering and finance with railway expansion in South America and the United States. At Bardon Hill, the lease he granted for quarrying helped initiate the large‑scale extraction of Charnwood hard rock that later underpinned road construction across much of England.[2][1]

His professional standing within the Institution of Civil Engineers, including the award of the Telford Medal, and his long service on the LB&SCR board reflected the regard in which contemporaries held his technical and administrative abilities.[1]

## Family and descendants

Robert Jacomb-Hood’s descendants remained active in British public and cultural life. His son George Percy Jacomb-Hood became a painter, etcher and illustrator, and a founding member of the New English Art Club and the Royal Society of Portrait Painters. George Percy’s writings, including his 1925 autobiography, contain references to his father’s career and to the family’s background, providing a perspective on the domestic life of a mid‑Victorian railway engineer’s household.[4][3]
