# 2025 Pennsylvania Supreme Court election

The **2025 Pennsylvania Supreme Court election** was a set of statewide judicial retention elections held on November 4, 2025, in the U.S. state of Pennsylvania. Voters decided whether three justices of the Supreme Court of Pennsylvania—Christine Donohue, Kevin Dougherty, and David Wecht—would receive additional ten‑year terms on the court. All three had first been elected as Democrats in 2015, and their simultaneous appearance on the ballot drew sustained attention from state and national political organizations because of the potential impact on the ideological balance of the court.[1][2][3]

The elections took place against the backdrop of a 5–2 Democratic majority on the seven‑member court, which had been in place since 2015. Under the state constitution, the justices did not face opponents on the ballot; instead, each was subject to a separate yes‑or‑no retention question. If a justice had failed to secure a majority of “yes” votes, the justice’s seat would have become vacant and an interim replacement would have been selected through a joint process involving the governor and the Pennsylvania Senate.[2][3][1]

Although judicial retention contests in Pennsylvania have historically attracted little public interest and almost always result in incumbents being retained, the 2025 Supreme Court retention elections were the focus of significant spending and organized campaigns by outside groups that linked the outcome to future decisions on election law, redistricting, and abortion rights.[3][1][2]

# Background

### Supreme Court of Pennsylvania

The Supreme Court of Pennsylvania is the highest court in the Commonwealth’s unified judicial system. It consists of seven justices who hear appeals on civil and criminal matters and exercise administrative supervision over the state court system. Justices are initially chosen in statewide partisan elections to ten‑year terms; subsequent terms are obtained through nonpartisan retention elections in which voters are asked to vote “yes” or “no” on whether a justice should remain in office for another ten‑year term.[4][5][2]

As of 2025, the court had five justices elected as Democrats and two elected as Republicans, giving it a 5–2 Democratic majority. The Democratic majority dates to the 2015 elections, when Donohue, Dougherty, and Wecht were elected to the court and shifted its partisan balance. Republican P. Kevin Brobson was later elected in 2021, and Democrat Daniel McCaffery was elected in 2023, but those elections did not change the overall partisan tilt.[6][7][1][2][4]

Under the Pennsylvania Constitution and implementing statutes, a justice who is not retained vacates the office at the end of the term. The governor may then appoint a temporary replacement, subject to confirmation by a two‑thirds vote of the Pennsylvania Senate; a partisan election for a full ten‑year term is subsequently held at the next odd‑numbered year statewide election. This process applied to the three seats on the ballot in 2025.[1][2][3]

### Justices up for retention

The three justices on the ballot in 2025 were first elected in 2015 and reached the end of their initial ten‑year terms.[2][3][1]

- **Christine Donohue** was elected in 2015 on the Democratic ticket. She previously served on the Superior Court of Pennsylvania. Her 2015 victory helped contribute to the court’s subsequent Democratic majority.[3][1][2]

- **Kevin Dougherty** was also elected as a Democrat in 2015. Before joining the Supreme Court, he served on the Philadelphia Court of Common Pleas. Like Donohue and Wecht, he was first elected in a year when three Supreme Court seats were simultaneously on the ballot.[1][2][3]

- **David Wecht**, a Democrat, was elected in 2015 after serving on the Superior Court. His election, together with Donohue’s and Dougherty’s, was widely described in contemporaneous coverage as pivotal in shifting the court’s partisan balance.[2][3][1]

All three were required by state law to stand in nonpartisan retention elections in 2025 to continue serving.[3][1][2]

### Historical context of Pennsylvania judicial retention

Pennsylvania adopted retention elections for appellate judges and justices in 1968 as part of a broader judicial article reform. In these elections, there are no opposing candidates; the ballot simply asks whether the judge or justice shall be retained for an additional term.[5][2]

Historically, statewide appellate retention contests in Pennsylvania have been low‑salience events that almost always resulted in incumbents winning new terms by wide margins. Since the adoption of retention elections, only one statewide appellate judge has failed to be retained: Supreme Court Justice Russell Nigro, who lost his retention bid in 2005 following criticism over controversial legislative pay raises.[1][2]

Commentary from state news organizations and legal observers in 2025 noted that the Supreme Court’s decisions over the previous decade on issues such as congressional redistricting, election procedures, and workplace and tort law had intensified political interest in the composition of the court and in the 2025 retention contests in particular.[2][1]

# Political significance

### Court’s recent role in high‑profile cases

Since gaining a Democratic majority in 2015, the Supreme Court of Pennsylvania has decided a series of high‑profile cases involving partisan gerrymandering, voting procedures, and access to abortion that attracted national attention. Among other actions, the court has:[7][1][2]

- Struck down congressional maps as unconstitutional partisan gerrymanders and ordered new maps.[7][2]
- Intervened in election‑law disputes involving mail‑in ballots and certification procedures during federal and statewide elections.[7][2]
- Considered litigation related to abortion access under the Pennsylvania Constitution after changes in federal constitutional doctrine.[7][2]

Business groups, labor organizations, and advocacy organizations on both sides of these issues argued that the 2025 retention elections could shape the court’s approach to such cases through at least the mid‑2030s.[2][7]

### Control of the court

Because Donohue, Dougherty, and Wecht were all elected as Democrats, a failure to retain one or more of them could have altered the ideological makeup of the court. If all three had been removed and the governor and Senate had not promptly agreed on replacements, the court would have been reduced to a smaller set of justices and could have faced deadlocks in closely divided cases.[3][1][2]

Reporting on the 2025 elections explained that if any of the justices were not retained, Governor Josh Shapiro, a Democrat, would be empowered to appoint interim replacements, subject to the concurrence of two‑thirds of the Republican‑controlled state Senate. In that scenario, the composition of the court could have become a subject of negotiation between the executive and legislative branches, particularly in light of upcoming statewide and federal elections, including the 2028 presidential race.[1][3][2]

Analyses by state and national outlets described the 2025 retention contests as part of a broader national trend in which state supreme court elections drew increased investment from political parties and outside groups focused on litigation over elections, reproductive rights, and regulatory matters.[7][1][2]

# Campaign

### Nature of retention campaigns

Under Pennsylvania’s Code of Judicial Conduct and related rules, sitting justices are permitted to engage in limited political activity in support of their own retention but are generally prohibited from campaigning in a partisan manner or making commitments about how they would decide specific cases. Consequently, retention contests tend to center on outside efforts by political action committees, bar associations, and advocacy organizations that highlight the justices’ records and the perceived consequences of retaining or removing them.[2]

In 2025, observers noted that the three Supreme Court retention contests drew substantially greater organized activity and spending than is typical for such elections in Pennsylvania. Coverage from state news outlets described the races as “high‑profile” for retention elections, with multiple national and state‑level groups raising funds, purchasing advertising, and organizing voter‑contact operations in connection with the yes‑or‑no votes on Donohue, Dougherty, and Wecht.[5][3][1][2]

### Support for retention

Democratic Party organizations and allied groups framed support for retaining the three justices as essential to maintaining a court majority that they described as protective of voting rights, abortion access, and consumer and worker protections.[3][1][7][2]

The Democratic National Committee announced a financial investment in the Pennsylvania Democratic Party to assist in the 2025 judicial elections, including the Supreme Court retention contests. The committee linked the investment to issues such as redistricting and state‑level protections for abortion and voting rights, stating that the court played a central role in these areas and that the outcome of the retention races could shape future decisions.[3]

Legal advocacy organizations and progressive‑leaning groups highlighted decisions issued by the current court majority in cases involving legislative redistricting and ballot access as reasons to vote “yes” on the retention questions. Bar associations and civic organizations also conducted informational campaigns that explained the retention process and summarized the justices’ backgrounds and judicial records.[5][7][2][3]

### Opposition to retention

Republican Party officials and allied organizations argued that the Democratic‑elected majority on the Supreme Court had issued decisions on topics such as redistricting and election administration that favored Democratic interests and expanded judicial power. They characterized the 2025 retention elections as an opportunity to move the court toward a different jurisprudential direction in advance of the 2028 elections and future redistricting cycles.[7][2]

Some business associations and conservative advocacy groups criticized the court’s rulings on tort law, employment classification, and regulatory authority, contending that these decisions created uncertainty for employers and insurers. These groups supported “no” votes on one or more of the justices up for retention, arguing that a change in the court’s composition would make it more receptive to limiting liability exposure and narrowing certain worker‑protection rulings.[2][7]

Republican strategists cited historical voter unfamiliarity with judicial retention contests as a potential advantage, reasoning that targeted persuasion campaigns in a low‑information environment could influence the results more than in high‑salience partisan races.[2]

### Spending and advertising

Reports in early and mid‑2025 described an escalation of spending on television, digital, and direct‑mail advertising by political action committees and interest groups focused on the Supreme Court retention elections.[5][1][3][2]

Liberal and Democratic‑aligned groups funded advertisements that emphasized the court’s role in rejecting certain congressional maps as unconstitutional gerrymanders, preserving access to mail‑in voting, and considering challenges to abortion restrictions under state constitutional provisions. These messages urged voters to support retention to maintain what these groups characterized as protections for democratic processes and individual rights.[3][7][2]

Conservative and Republican‑aligned groups, by contrast, ran advertising that criticized the court’s perceived activism in redrawing electoral maps and expanding the scope of state constitutional protections. They linked the justices to decisions that they said created instability in election rules and imposed additional burdens on businesses. These campaigns encouraged “no” votes as a means of signaling dissatisfaction with the court’s recent direction.[7][2]

Coverage from state news outlets noted that the aggregate spending on the 2025 Supreme Court retention contests, while lower than in some prior open‑seat Supreme Court races, was unusually high for Pennsylvania judicial retention elections and reflected the heightened political attention on the court.[5][1][3][2]

# Conduct of the election

The 2025 Pennsylvania judicial elections, including the Supreme Court retention questions, were held on November 4, 2025, concurrently with contests for one open seat on the Superior Court and one open seat on the Commonwealth Court, as well as various local and county offices.[8][1][5]

The statewide ballot presented separate retention questions for each of the three justices, listing their names and asking whether each should be retained for an additional ten‑year term on the Supreme Court. Voters could cast a “yes” or “no” vote for each justice independently.[5][2]

Election administration followed the procedures established in Pennsylvania law and in prior statewide elections. Counties conducted in‑person voting at designated polling places and processed mail‑in and absentee ballots under rules adopted by the General Assembly and interpreted by the courts in earlier election cycles.[5][2]

In addition to the Supreme Court retention questions, voters elected Democratic candidates Brandon Neuman and Stella Tsai to open positions on the Superior Court and Commonwealth Court, respectively. Both races were partisan contests in which the Democratic nominees defeated Republican nominees in the general election, contributing to Democratic gains across statewide judicial offices in 2025.[8][1][5]

# Results

### Statewide outcome

The three Supreme Court justices up for retention in 2025—Christine Donohue, Kevin Dougherty, and David Wecht—were each retained for additional ten‑year terms.[9][1]

According to statewide election results, each justice received a majority of “yes” votes. Media reports noted that they were retained by wide margins and that their support extended to multiple counties that had supported Republican candidates, including former president Donald Trump, in the 2024 presidential election.[9][1]

The outcome preserved the existing 5–2 Democratic‑elected majority on the Supreme Court of Pennsylvania and meant that the next opportunity for a significant change in the court’s composition would likely arise through future partisan elections or retirements rather than through the 2025 retention contests.[4][9][1][7][2]

### Impact on court composition

Because all three justices were retained, no vacancies were created on the Supreme Court as a result of the 2025 retention elections, and the appointment mechanism involving the governor and the Senate was not triggered.[9][1][3][2]

Donohue, Dougherty, and Wecht each secured new terms that will extend into the mid‑2030s, subject to Pennsylvania’s mandatory retirement age for judges. The court’s overall ideological and partisan alignment therefore remained unchanged following the election, and attention among political and legal observers shifted to possible future contests for other seats, including those held by justices whose initial terms will end later in the decade.[6][4][1][7][2]

Commentary on the results linked the justices’ retention to broader 2025 electoral outcomes in Pennsylvania, where Democratic candidates also won statewide contests for the Superior Court and Commonwealth Court. Some analysts described the aggregate results as part of a favorable environment for Democratic candidates and causes in that election year.[8][1][5]

# Aftermath and analysis

Post‑election analyses from state and national media outlets emphasized several themes arising from the 2025 Supreme Court retention elections:

- **Endurance of incumbency advantage in retention contests.** Despite unusually high spending and organized opposition efforts, the three justices were retained by large margins, reinforcing the historical pattern that Pennsylvania appellate judges almost always win retention.[9][1][2]

- **Continued prominence of state courts.** Commentators pointed to the level of national and state attention on the 2025 races as further evidence that state supreme courts had become central battlegrounds over questions previously resolved in federal courts, especially election administration and abortion rights.[1][3][7][2]

- **Implications for future elections.** Analysts observed that the retention of all three Democratic‑elected justices reduced the likelihood of an immediate partisan shift on the Supreme Court but did not eliminate the potential for high‑stakes judicial elections later in the decade, when other justices will approach the end of their terms or mandatory retirement.[6][4][7][2]

Legal scholars and practitioners also noted that the 2025 results would allow the existing court majority to continue shaping Pennsylvania constitutional doctrine in areas such as voting rights, redistricting, and the scope of protections for individual liberties and workers for many years.[4][1][7][2]
