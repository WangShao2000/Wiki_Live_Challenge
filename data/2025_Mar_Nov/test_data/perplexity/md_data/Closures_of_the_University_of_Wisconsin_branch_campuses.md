# Closures of the University of Wisconsin Branch Campuses

The **closures of the University of Wisconsin branch campuses** refer to the shutdown of multiple two-year regional campuses within the University of Wisconsin System, beginning with UW–Platteville Richland in 2023. As of December 2025, six branch campuses have fully closed, one campus operates exclusively online without in-person instruction, and one campus has relocated to a nearby technical college. These closures followed decades of declining enrollment and reduced state funding. The closures have raised concerns among educators and community leaders about the future of the Wisconsin Idea, a public philosophy that the state's universities should serve all residents throughout Wisconsin.

# Background

### Historical Development

Following World War II, the University of Wisconsin System established a network of freshman-sophomore centers across Wisconsin to serve communities distant from existing four-year universities. Between 1945 and the early 1960s, 14 campus centers opened in underserved regions, primarily designed to provide general education and transfer-oriented curriculum before students completed their degrees at four-year institutions. The centers were founded on the principle of the Wisconsin Idea, which emphasized that the university should extend its beneficial influence to every family in the state. An internal UW System committee proposed that campuses require at least 600 high school graduates within a 20-mile radius to be viable, with a target enrollment of 150–200 students per campus.[1][2]

In 1971, following a merger between the University of Wisconsin System and the Wisconsin State Universities System, the freshman-sophomore centers became formally designated as the University of Wisconsin Centers and later renamed the University of Wisconsin Colleges in 1997. These 13 campuses offered liberal arts curricula with the goal of enabling students to earn associate degrees and transfer to four-year universities.[3]

### UW–Medford Closure (1980)

UW–Medford was the first and only UW campus closure for over 40 years. In 1980, a special task force reviewed several two-year campus programs and considered closing six centers: Baraboo, Barron, Manitowoc, Marinette, Medford, and Richland. The task force ultimately recommended closing only UW–Medford, citing enrollment declines, with the campus's final enrollment at 90 students. Funding saved from the closure was redirected to support the remaining campuses.[4]

# Contributing Factors

### Funding and Demographics

Multiple structural factors contributed to the eventual wave of closures beginning in 2023. In 2013, the Wisconsin state legislature froze tuition statewide and reduced UW System funding by $66 million. This combination of frozen tuition and reduced state appropriations severely constrained the budgets of smaller campuses, which depend heavily on state funding to maintain operations. By 2023, Wisconsin's public university system ranked 44th nationally in state funding, significantly lower than neighboring states Illinois and Michigan, which ranked first and second respectively.[5][6]

Wisconsin faced demographic challenges that exacerbated enrollment declines at branch campuses. The state's relatively low birth rate directly reduced the number of high school graduates available to attend college. Projections indicated that Wisconsin high school graduates would decline by approximately 13 percent over the following decade from 2023 levels. Additionally, fewer high school graduates opted to pursue post-secondary education, as participation rates fell below those of neighboring states. These demographic and participation trends created a declining pool of potential students for the two-year campuses.[7][8]

### 2018 Restructuring

In October 2017, University of Wisconsin System President Ray Cross proposed merging the 13 UW Colleges with nearby four-year universities to create regional clusters. The UW Board of Regents approved this restructuring in November 2017, and implementation began July 1, 2018. The merger was intended to improve administrative efficiency and prevent campus closures by allowing students to access a broader range of faculty and facilities across multiple campuses.[9][3]

However, the 2018 restructuring did not achieve its intended goal of stabilizing the branch campuses. Following the merger, four-year universities reduced funding for recruitment and marketing at the two-year campuses, which had previously engaged in active outreach to high schools. Additionally, the restructuring did not result in meaningful collaboration between two-year and four-year campuses across the system, and enrollment at branch campuses continued to decline. Between 2018 and 2023, total enrollment at branch campuses fell 50 percent, from approximately 9,600 to 4,803 students.[10][1][9]

# Wave of Closures (2023–2026)

### Initial Closures and Announcements

On October 17, 2023, Universities of Wisconsin President Jay Rothman announced the closure of UW–Platteville Richland and the suspension of in-person instruction at UW–Milwaukee Washington County and UW–Oshkosh Fond du Lac by June 2024. The announcement marked the beginning of the most significant period of contraction in the system's history since the 1980 closure of UW–Medford.[11]

**UW–Platteville Richland** officially closed on July 1, 2023, after enrollment had declined to 60 students in its final year. The campus, which had served the region since 1967, ceased operations as the University of Wisconsin System and Richland County failed to negotiate a sustainable operating model for continued presence in the community.[11]

**UW–Milwaukee Washington County** suspended in-person instruction and formally closed on June 30, 2024. The campus had experienced steep enrollment declines, falling from 1,054 students in fall 2020 to 332 students in 2022. The closure prompted discussions about developing a partnership with nearby Moraine Park Technical College to continue serving the region, though direct merger proposals were not finalized.[12][13][14]

**UW–Oshkosh Fond du Lac** closed on June 30, 2024, following a steady decline in enrollment from 733 students in fall 2020 to 243 in its final year. The campus, originally opened in 1968 with a construction cost of $5 million, had undergone significant renovations in 2000 including construction of a student commons and 300-seat theater.[15]

### Subsequent Closures

**UW–Milwaukee Waukesha** closed after the spring 2025 semester, on June 30, 2025. This closure also closed the College of General Studies, which had managed both the Washington County and Waukesha campuses and offered associate degree programs. More than 100 employees, including tenured faculty, were affected by the closure. In response, UW–Milwaukee and Waukesha County Technical College (WCTC) established a collaborative University Center model to offer bachelor's and graduate degree completion programs at WCTC's facilities.[16]

**UW–Oshkosh Fox Cities** (Menasha) closed on June 30, 2025. The closure announcement in June 2024 cited declining enrollment and projections that student numbers would fall below 100 by 2032. The Fox Cities campus had been established in 1933 and was home to significant facilities including the Barlow Planetarium and the Weis Earth Science Museum, Wisconsin's official state mineralogical museum.[17]

**UW–Platteville Baraboo Sauk County** announced closure effective May 22, 2026. The campus had experienced dramatic enrollment decline from approximately 500 students in 2018 to 126 in fall 2025. Despite a $780,000 commitment from Sauk County toward increased enrollment and retention, the university determined the campus was no longer sustainable. Chancellor Tammy K. Evetovich stated the decision was not made lightly but was necessary given continued enrollment declines.[18]

### Campus Suspended In-Person Instruction

**UW–Green Bay Marinette** suspended in-person instruction in fall 2024, though some online courses continue to be offered. The decision was made proactively to maintain a presence in the community while acknowledging that the current operational model was unsustainable. The campus had enrolled approximately 242 students prior to the suspension. UW–Green Bay continues to use the Marinette campus for theater programming and cultural events, maintaining the Herb Williams Theatre as a community venue for performances and educational programming.[19]

### Campus Relocation

**UW–Stevens Point Marathon County** (Wausau) is relocating to Northcentral Technical College (NTC) beginning fall 2026. Between fall 2011 and fall 2023, enrollment at the Wausau campus declined 78.5 percent to 232 students, raising serious concerns about viability. The partnership allows UW–Stevens Point to continue offering bachelor's and master's degrees while NTC provides associate degrees and certificates, with coordinated transfer pathways and joint programming in high-demand fields such as healthcare. Faculty members will retain their positions as UW–Stevens Point employees while teaching at NTC's facilities, though some staff reductions are anticipated. The university and Marathon County are discussing future use of the current Wausau campus facilities, located approximately two miles from NTC.[20]

# Remaining Campuses

As of December 2025, five UW branch campuses remain fully operational with in-person instruction: UW–Eau Claire Barron County, UW–Green Bay Manitowoc, UW–Green Bay Sheboygan, UW–Stevens Point Marshfield (pending relocation), and UW–Whitewater Rock County. Of these, UW–Green Bay Manitowoc has maintained growing enrollment and demonstrated relative stability, while the others face declining enrollment trends though no immediate closure announcements have been made. UW–Green Bay's campuses in Manitowoc and Sheboygan have shown increasing enrollment and are described as being on "positive trajectories."[21][22]

# Impacts and Controversies

### Community Effects

The closures have created significant challenges for rural and smaller communities that relied on branch campuses for educational access and economic activity. Communities have faced mounting costs to maintain campus facilities following the departures of UW System operations. In response, Wisconsin enacted legislation allowing counties with closed campuses to apply for grants of up to $2 million for property redevelopment.[23]

Former campuses have been repurposed for various community uses. Fond du Lac County transformed its former UW–Oshkosh campus into an events center and community gathering space. Waukesha County's former UW–Milwaukee campus is planned for demolition and redevelopment as a housing development. Richland County has struggled to find uses for its campus facilities and has gradually dismantled structures due to lack of buyer interest.[24]

### Wisconsin Idea and Access Concerns

Critics argue that the closures represent a failure to uphold the Wisconsin Idea, the founding principle that Wisconsin's universities should serve the public good and extend educational opportunity throughout the state. Educators note that branch campuses served students who could not afford to relocate to four-year campus towns and who might not have pursued higher education without local access. Ken Brosky, a professor and documentary filmmaker, argued that the closure of UW–Platteville Richland was particularly damaging given that Richland County is among the poorest in Wisconsin and the campus had been a critical educational resource for the region.[25][26][10]

Some commentators blamed insufficient state funding for the closures, arguing that the Wisconsin legislature's long-term underfunding of the UW System left branch campuses vulnerable. Others, including former UW System President Ray Cross, acknowledged that demographic trends and enrollment declines made some campuses economically unviable even before the 2018 restructuring.[27][28]

### Restructuring Impact

Faculty members at branch campuses reported that the 2018 restructuring by President Ray Cross had not been implemented with adequate input from affected stakeholders. The merger was approved without consulting shared governance groups or campus administrators, and implementation across the system was uneven. Some faculty expressed concerns that the four-year universities prioritized recruiting for their main campuses at the expense of branch campus marketing and support. The inequities between two-year and four-year campus faculty regarding pay, tenure criteria, and autonomy have been noted as ongoing sources of institutional tension.[29][10]
