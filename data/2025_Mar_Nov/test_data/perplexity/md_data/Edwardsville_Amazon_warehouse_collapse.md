# Edwardsville Amazon Warehouse Collapse

On the evening of December 10, 2021, a tornado struck the DLI4 delivery facility in Edwardsville, Illinois, an Amazon warehouse that served as a distribution center for the Greater St. Louis region. An EF3 tornado with estimated wind speeds of 150 miles per hour (240 km/h) struck the facility at approximately 8:29 p.m. local time, causing catastrophic structural failure of the building. Six workers were killed and one was critically injured in the collapse. Most of the deceased were delivery contractors brought to the facility for the busy Christmas holiday season, with only seven of the 45 people on-site employed full-time at the warehouse.[1]

## Background

The DLI4 delivery facility opened in July 2020 as an Amazon delivery station. Located in the Gateway Commerce Center straddling Edwardsville and Pontoon Beach in Illinois, the facility processed packages as part of the final stage before shipment to customers. The 594,000-square-foot warehouse was built by Contegra Construction in 2018 and employed approximately 190 workers across multiple shifts. The facility was not owned by Amazon but rather leased from a San Diego-based real estate company.[2][1]

The warehouse was originally constructed according to the 2006 International Building Code standards, with concrete walls rated to withstand wind gusts of 90 mph. The building featured 11-inch-thick concrete walls approximately 40 feet tall.[3][1][2]

## Tornado and Collapse

On December 10, 2021, a severe weather outbreak developed across multiple states as a cold front approached the region. The DLI4 facility received tornado warnings at 8:06 p.m. and again at 8:16 p.m. through a web application. The facility's designated severe weather shelter was located in a bathroom on the northern side of the building, but confusion about the shelter's location led approximately 10 employees to seek refuge in a bathroom on the southern side of the building instead.[4][1]

At 8:29 p.m., the EF3 tornado struck the western face of the warehouse with winds reaching 150 mph. The tornado collapsed the west-facing wall onto the southern bathroom where the workers had sheltered, resulting in six fatalities and one critical injury. Approximately 150 yards of the building collapsed in total, including multiple structural failures involving walls and portions of the roof. The eastern wall collapsed into a parking lot, and high-tension transmission towers and overhead power lines in the area were destroyed.[1]

The tornado remained on the ground for only six minutes, lifting at 8:32 p.m. The same supercell subsequently produced another long-lived EF2 tornado that struck the communities of Bingham and Ramsey State Park, Kentucky. Across Edwardsville, damage was relatively minor and rated up to EF1 intensity, with primary damage to trees, power infrastructure, and roofs.[1]

Emergency personnel from Edwardsville and surrounding communities including Glen Carbon, Pontoon Beach, Mitchell, Long Lake, Troy, Maryville, Granite City, and Highland, Illinois, arrived within six minutes of receiving the initial call at approximately 8:35 p.m. Water and gas leaks, as well as downed electrical mains, posed hazards to rescue operations. Nevertheless, 45 employees were rescued from the facility, and one survivor called 911 from inside the collapsed southern bathroom at 8:41 p.m.[1]

## Casualties

The six individuals killed in the collapse were:[5][2]

* Deandre S. Morrow, 28, of St. Louis, Missouri[2]
* Kevin D. Dickey, 62, of Carlyle, Illinois, an Amazon dispatcher[2]
* Clayton Lynn Cope, 29, of Alton, Illinois, a U.S. Navy veteran[2]
* Etheria S. Hebb, 34, of St. Louis, Missouri, a delivery driver and new mother[2]
* Larry E. Virden, 46, of Collinsville, Illinois, an Army veteran and delivery driver[2]
* Austin J. McEwen, 26, of Edwardsville, Illinois, an Amazon delivery driver[2]

All six deceased individuals were in the southern bathroom when the tornado struck. Five of the deceased were independent delivery contractors employed by separate logistics firms, while one was a CBRE Group employee assigned to the facility. One additional person was critically injured in the collapse.[1]

## Investigations

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) launched an investigation into the collapse shortly after the incident. In April 2022, OSHA released its findings, determining that Amazon's severe weather emergency procedures met minimal federal safety guidelines for storm sheltering and did not cite the company for violations. However, OSHA issued a Hazard Alert Letter recommending that Amazon improve its severe weather safety procedures in three areas: ensuring all employees receive training and participate in emergency weather drills, including site-specific information in severe weather emergency plans, and clearly identifying the location of all audible warning devices.[3][1][2]

OSHA's investigation noted several concerns about the facility's emergency action plan, including that it was not customized with specific instructions for anticipated hazards in Edwardsville and contained elements not applicable to the location, such as hurricane preparation procedures. The agency also found that the megaphone designated in the emergency plan to activate shelter procedures was locked in a cage and inaccessible, requiring management to rely on verbal communication. Some employees did not recall the location of the designated severe weather shelter.[3][1]

Structural engineer assessments following the collapse raised concerns about potential building code violations. A structural engineer report prepared after the incident suggested that support columns were not properly anchored to the foundation, describing the failure as similar to an unanchored patio umbrella being lifted by wind. The 2006 International Building Code under which the warehouse was built required that anchorage of walls and columns to the foundation be designed to resist uplift and sliding forces. Contegra Construction disputed these allegations, stating that the company was confident that a comprehensive forensic investigation would ultimately attribute the structural failure to the tornado rather than pre-existing defects.[3][1]

## Legal Action

In January 2022, the family of Austin McEwen filed a wrongful death lawsuit against Amazon and the companies involved in constructing and designing the facility, including Contegra Construction, the general contractor. In April 2022, civil rights attorney Ben Crump announced the filing of two lawsuits on behalf of victims and survivors, alleging that Amazon prioritized profit over employee safety. The lawsuits contended that Amazon failed to evacuate the facility despite tornado warnings, did not provide adequate storm shelters, continued operating until moments before the tornado struck, and did not timely inform employees of the approaching danger.[3][2]

The defendants named in the lawsuits included Amazon, Amazon Logistics, the facility developer TriStar Properties, Contegra Construction, and various design and engineering firms including Gray Architects and KB Engineering. As of September 2024, one wrongful death suit was scheduled for mediation and could proceed to trial.[1]

## Response and Aftermath

Amazon donated $1 million to the Edwardsville Community Foundation and provided support to the families of the deceased. The company stated that it received tornado warnings and directed employees to shelter-in-place. However, Amazon faced criticism for its handling of the emergency. Additionally, founder Jeff Bezos faced criticism for appearing to prioritize attendance at a Blue Origin launch on December 11, 2021, where he greeted returning crew members, before addressing the warehouse collapse.[2]

Following the incident, Amazon hired a meteorologist and implemented enhanced extreme weather training and procedures for employees. The company also conducted expert re-evaluations at over 800 Amazon facilities nationwide to identify safer shelter-in-place areas.[4][1]

## Reconstruction

Edwardsville's public works department received a request to rebuild the facility in April 2022. The city mandated that the rebuilt warehouse comply with the 2015 International Building Code standards, which superseded the 2006 code used for the original construction. This required the rebuilt facility to have walls specified to withstand wind gusts of 114 mph, an increase from the previous 90 mph specification.[1]

DLI4 fully reopened on September 4, 2024, exactly 1,000 days after the tornado struck. However, neither the rebuilt DLI4 warehouse nor any other facility in the commerce center was constructed with storm shelters, despite discussions about adding shelters following the initial collapse. Amazon stated that decisions about additional construction on the site were largely at the discretion of the landlord, and the company has not sought to install shelters at any of its approximately 800 North American facilities.[1]

## Context

The December 10-11, 2021 tornado outbreak was one of the largest single-day tornado outbreaks in recorded history, with 120 tornadoes occurring over an eight-hour period. The outbreak resulted in at least 71 deaths across multiple states and caused over $1.8 billion in damages. The Edwardsville tornado was one of two EF3 tornadoes produced by the same supercell; the other occurred near Defiance, Missouri, and resulted in one fatality. Another notably destructive tornado from this outbreak struck Mayfield, Kentucky, and resulted in 57 deaths, making it the deadliest tornado ever recorded in the United States in the month of December and the deadliest since the 2011 Joplin, Missouri tornado.[4][1]
