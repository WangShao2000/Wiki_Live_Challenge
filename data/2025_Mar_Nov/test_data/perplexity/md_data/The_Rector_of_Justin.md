# The Rector of Justin

**The Rector of Justin** is a 1964 psychological fiction novel by Louis Auchincloss about the headmaster (or "rector") of a socially exclusive American boarding school. The narrative unfolds through multiple voices—primarily the diary of Brian Aspinwall, a young English teacher, supplemented by writings and interviews from five individuals who knew the school's founder, Francis Prescott. Through these varied perspectives, the novel gradually reveals how the protagonist's charismatic leadership masked profound contradictions between his articulated ideals and the institutional realities of the school he created. The work explores the decline of White Anglo-Saxon Protestant (WASP) society's moral authority and the erosion of the Christian values upon which the educational institution was ostensibly founded.

The novel was a major commercial success, competing for thirty-five weeks on the bestseller lists with Saul Bellow's *Herzog*. It was a finalist for the Pulitzer Prize for Fiction and the National Book Award for Fiction, ultimately losing to Shirley Ann Grau's *The Keeper of the House* and Bellow's *Herzog*, respectively. Upon its publication, *Harper's Magazine* described it as "almost certainly the finest work ever written about an American preparatory school". The novel is widely regarded as Auchincloss' greatest achievement and represents a significant moment in American literary history, as its loss at the National Book Award marked the literary establishment's shift away from realist society novels about the American upper class toward works featuring poetic prose, avant-garde styles, and culturally diverse protagonists.

# Background and Inspiration

Louis Auchincloss attended Groton School (1932–1935) and maintained lifelong interests in both the institutional forms and the individuals who shaped American boarding schools. The character of Francis Prescott, the novel's central figure, was partially inspired by Endicott Peabody, the founder and longtime headmaster of Groton School, though Auchincloss emphasized that the character also reflected federal judge Learned Hand, whom he regarded as the greatest man he had known. Auchincloss had conceived of writing a novel about a boarding school headmaster for some time but delayed the project until he felt prepared to handle the material adequately.

The author's interest in the subject matter drew from his high school English teacher Malcolm Strachan, whose wife published the *Atlantic Monthly*. Strachan had become a close confidant of Endicott Peabody and had planned to write a novel demonstrating that Peabody's "theology was subtler and more complicated than any of us supposed." After Strachan's premature death, Auchincloss resolved to write the novel himself, incorporating his own interpretation of boarding school life and the figures who led such institutions.

Auchincloss drafted an initial version of the novel in 1938, just three years after graduating from Groton. This early version centered on a headmaster who sought to raise students "with the ideals of public service and a sense of noblesse oblige," only to realize that most of his students pursued lucrative positions on Wall Street instead. Auchincloss submitted a revised draft to Houghton Mifflin in July 1963, and the novel was published in July 1964.

# Plot and Narrative Structure

The novel's structure is multilayered, employing six distinct narrators whose perspectives gradually construct a complex portrait of Francis Prescott. The framing narrator is Brian Aspinwall, a twenty-seven-year-old English teacher who arrives at Justin Martyr School (a fictionalized version of Groton, an Episcopal boarding school) in September 1939 to fill a position vacated by a master leaving to enlist in the Royal Air Force. Aspinwall is immediately captivated by Prescott, the school's aging rector, and finds his personality puzzling: Prescott presents himself as an intellectual yet presides over an institution focused on athletics; he speaks of democratic ideals while maintaining exclusionary standards; he advocates for Christian values while condoning institutional snobbery.

Aspinwall attempts to write a biography of Prescott but ultimately fails, as have others before him. The narrative incorporates five additional testimonies: an unpublished memoir by Horace Havistock, Prescott's oldest friend; an interview with Prescott's daughter; documents from David Griscam, the chairman of the school's trustees and principal architect of its financial success; a section from Jules Griscam, David's son, who was expelled by Prescott for misconduct; and writings from Charley Strong, a favored student and senior prefect from the class of 1911 who represents the idealized embodiment of Prescott's vision yet became a casualty of the social and spiritual upheavals of the twentieth century.

The story spans from September 1939 to April 1947, chronicling Prescott's final years as headmaster and the circumstances leading to his retirement. The narrative reveals that Prescott, despite his intellectual gifts and genuine commitment to educating young men according to principles of service and moral responsibility, was fundamentally unable to reconcile his idealism with the material realities of wealth, social ambition, and institutional self-interest that pervaded both his school and the broader WASP society it represented.

# Major Themes

The novel examines the tension between idealism and institutional reality, presenting Prescott as a man whose personal vision consistently collides with the practical limitations of the world he inhabits. Prescott genuinely believes in the possibility of cultivating virtue in young men, yet the very institution he founded becomes an instrument for reproducing social privilege and material advantage rather than moral transformation.

The work also explores the decline of WASP hegemony in American society. Through Prescott's reflections and his attempts to preserve the values and structures of an earlier era, the novel documents the erosion of the cultural authority once wielded by the White Anglo-Saxon Protestant establishment. This theme becomes increasingly pronounced as the narrative progresses, with World War II and its aftermath serving as a watershed moment in which the assumptions underlying Prescott's worldview are fundamentally challenged and made obsolete.

The relationship between tradition and progress constitutes another thematic strand. Prescott represents a preservationist impulse, defending the accumulated practices and structures of his institution against forces of modernization and social change. The novel suggests that this resistance to change, while rooted in genuine concern for values and community, ultimately fails to address the substantive contradictions within the system being preserved.

# Critical Reception

*The Rector of Justin* achieved significant commercial success upon publication. The novel spent thirty-five weeks competing for the top position on bestseller lists with Saul Bellow's *Herzog* and was the sixth-best-selling fiction book of 1964, though *Herzog* ultimately outsold it in hardcover copies (142,000 to 80,000). The novel had sold approximately 2 million copies by 1966, establishing itself as a major literary event.

Contemporary critical response was predominantly positive. The *New York Times* praised the work as "a passionately interesting, but a spiritually important study of the American character of, and for, our time." *Harper's Magazine* declared it "almost certainly the finest work ever written about an American preparatory school." Many critics recognized the novel as a significant achievement in the tradition of American realist fiction, with some comparing Auchincloss favorably to predecessors such as Henry James and Edith Wharton in his ability to examine the psychology and moral dilemmas of his characters.

The novel's failure to win the Pulitzer Prize (awarded to Shirley Ann Grau's *The Keeper of the House*, which addressed racial themes in the American South) and the National Book Award (won by Saul Bellow's *Herzog*) proved historically significant. Literary historians have observed that this moment marked a transition in American literary taste away from realist novels focused on the upper-class WASP society toward works incorporating poetic innovation, stylistic experimentation, and narratives centered on marginalized or culturally diverse protagonists. Auchincloss' novel, despite its critical acclaim, was viewed as the culmination of an earlier literary tradition rather than as an indication of the future direction of American fiction.

# Literary Significance and Legacy

*The Rector of Justin* is widely considered Louis Auchincloss' most significant work and ranks among the important American novels of the second half of the twentieth century. The novel is valued for its incisive examination of the psychology of leadership, the nature of institutional change, and the moral complexities of individuals navigating between personal conviction and social obligation.

Auchincloss' treatment of Prescott as a character worthy of complex representation—neither simply condemning his class prejudices nor endorsing his idealism uncritically—contributed to the novel's enduring appeal. The work exemplifies Auchincloss' broader literary project: depicting the American upper class not as figures of contempt or simple admiration but as individuals struggling with genuine moral questions within the constraints of their social position.

The novel's sophisticated narrative technique, employing multiple narrators whose partial and sometimes contradictory perspectives force readers to construct their own understanding of Prescott's character and legacy, has been recognized as a significant formal achievement. This structure mirrors the epistemological challenge at the novel's heart: determining the true nature of a person whose external success masks internal contradiction and whose influence extends beyond his ability to control or fully understand it.

# Publication History

*The Rector of Justin* was first published by Houghton Mifflin in July 1964 as a hardcover edition with 363 pages. The novel subsequently appeared in paperback and has been issued in various editions, including a 2002 Modern Library edition. It remains continuously in print, with multiple editions available from different publishers, ensuring its accessibility to readers decades after its initial publication.
