# Breton Civil War, 1341–1343

The **Breton Civil War, 1341–1343** was a succession conflict in the Duchy of Brittany that erupted following the death of Duke John III in April 1341. Two rival claimants competed for control of the duchy: Joan of Penthièvre, niece of the deceased duke, whose claim was backed by her husband Charles of Blois and the French crown; and John of Montfort, half-brother of John III, who was supported by John's wife, Joanna of Flanders, and eventually by King Edward III of England. The conflict formed an integral part of the early Hundred Years' War due to the involvement of France and England in supporting their respective claimants. The war, which would ultimately last until 1365, saw dramatic military campaigns featuring the celebrated defense of Hennebont by Joanna of Flanders and the first significant Anglo-French land battle at Morlaix in 1342.

### Background and Succession Dispute

In the early 14th century, the Duchy of Brittany occupied a unique position within the Kingdom of France. Although the dukes were nominally vassals of the French crown, they governed their territory with considerable autonomy. When Duke John III died childless on 30 April 1341, he left a contested succession dispute that would escalate into a broader European conflict.[1][2]

John III had married three times but produced no direct heirs. By his first marriage to Mary of Limoges, he had two brothers: one was his heir as John III, and another was Guy, Count of Penthièvre. By his father Arthur II's second marriage to Yolande of Dreux, John had a half-brother named John of Montfort. John III spent much of his reign attempting to exclude his half-siblings from the succession, even seeking an annulment of his father's second marriage to bastardize them.[3]

At John III's death, two principal claimants emerged. Joan of Penthièvre, the niece of the late duke and daughter of Guy of Penthièvre, asserted her claim through her marriage to Charles of Blois, a nephew of King Philip VI of France. The alternative claim came from John of Montfort, the half-brother of John III. The succession question became intertwined with the concept of semi-Salic law, which governed Brittany. Under this system, male succession was strongly preferred, but if no male heir of the previous duke existed, inheritance could pass through a female to her male relatives. John of Montfort's supporters emphasized that Brittany's status as a duchy-peerage ("duchy-peerage") of the French crown should be subject to strict male succession principles, while the Blois faction argued for a more flexible interpretation that would allow a woman to inherit if married to a capable male heir.[4]

The French king, Philip VI, naturally favored his nephew Charles of Blois and the Blois claim, viewing this as an opportunity to bring the semi-autonomous Duchy of Brittany under more direct royal control. The situation became further complicated by the fact that both claimants' patrons—Edward III of England and Philip VI of France—were locked in the Hundred Years' War, a conflict that had begun in 1337.[5]

### Initial Campaign of 1341

Upon John III's death, John of Montfort acted with remarkable speed. By August 1341, he and his wife Joanna of Flanders had secured control of most of the major fortified towns and cities in Brittany, including Rennes, Vannes, Auray, Hennebont, Quimper, Brest, and Nantes. An assembly of citizens and minor nobility in Nantes formally acknowledged John as duke, giving his claim a veneer of local legitimacy. This early maneuvering secured the Montfort faction's position and demonstrated that John of Montfort had considerable support among the Breton nobility and merchant classes.[6]

However, Philip VI of France grew concerned when reports of John's negotiations with English emissaries reached him. On 7 September 1341, the French king officially declared Charles of Blois the rightful heir to the duchy and resolved to enforce this claim militarily. Philip dispatched a substantial French army to Brittany under the command of Charles and Jean, Duke of Normandy (the future King John II of France), to compel the Montfort faction to submit.[7]

The result was swift and dramatic. Under overwhelming military pressure, the French forces besieged John of Montfort in the city of Nantes. After a siege lasting approximately three weeks, John surrendered on 21 November 1341 and was taken prisoner. He was subsequently transferred to Paris and imprisoned in the Louvre, where he would remain for several years. With the male leader of the Montfort cause captured, many observers assumed the conflict had been resolved in favor of Charles of Blois and the French crown.[8]

### The Role of Joanna of Flanders

The conflict, however, was far from settled. Joanna of Flanders, John of Montfort's wife, now took the unprecedented step of assuming military and political leadership of the Montfort faction. This decision proved transformative for the character of the war. Rather than accept her husband's captivity and her family's apparent defeat, Joanna acted with strategic vision and considerable courage. She secured the ducal treasury and transferred it westward to the stronghold of Brest, beyond the reach of the French forces. She captured the town of Redon and then established herself at Hennebont, a fortified port town in southern Brittany with strong defensive walls.[9]

Most significantly, Joanna declared her two-year-old son, John (the future John IV), as the legitimate heir and figurehead of the Montfort faction. By elevating a child to nominal leadership, she maintained the continuity of the Montfort claim and provided a rallying point for supporters who might otherwise have accepted the loss of John of Montfort's cause. She then sent emissaries to England to appeal directly to King Edward III for military assistance, leveraging the fact that Edward had strategic reasons to support any force that could weaken French authority in Brittany.[10]

Edward III, engaged in his own conflict with France and seeking to establish English influence in Brittany as a springboard for operations against France, agreed to provide military support. However, the practical difficulties of transporting troops across the English Channel—limited shipping capacity, unfavorable weather, and competing military priorities—meant that English reinforcements were delayed and limited in scale throughout 1342.[11]

### Siege of Hennebont and Joanna's Defense

In late May 1342, Charles of Blois launched a siege of Hennebont, where Joanna had positioned herself and the bulk of the Montfort faction's remaining forces. This siege became one of the most celebrated episodes of the conflict and established Joanna's reputation for military prowess and leadership.[12]

According to contemporary chronicles, Joanna personally participated in the defense of the town. During the initial assault, when French forces attacked the gates in disarray, Joanna rallied the defenders and inflicted a sharp defeat on the attackers, with many French casualties. Most dramatically, she reportedly organized a daring sortie of approximately 300 mounted men-at-arms. Under her leadership, these troops rode out of the fortified town, set fire to the French siege camp, destroyed supplies and siege equipment, and inflicted significant casualties before withdrawing. This bold action and the resulting destruction of the French encampment earned Joanna the epithet "Jeanne la Flamme," or "Fiery Joan."[13]

After this initial repulse, Charles attempted a more systematic siege, bringing up siege engines and establishing a closer blockade. By late May or early June 1342, the situation appeared dire for the defenders, and there was discussion of surrender. However, Joanna held firm, expressing confidence that English reinforcements would arrive within three days. Her faith proved justified. In June 1342, a small English relief force arrived by sea, having penetrated the harbor of Hennebont. This modest English reinforcement was sufficient to encourage the defenders and to prompt Charles to withdraw his besieging forces temporarily. Joanna and her remaining troops evacuated the town and moved further west, continuing the struggle in the extreme western portions of Brittany.[14]

### English Military Intervention and Battle of Morlaix

The arrival of English forces marked a significant escalation in the Breton conflict. In August 1342, an English fleet of approximately 260 ships commanded by William of Northampton arrived off the coast of Brittany with approximately 1,350 fighting men. This modest force caused a dramatic reversal of the military situation through a combination of shock effect and psychological impact. When Charles of Blois, who was besieging the port of Brest, saw the large English fleet crowded with what he assumed were vast numbers of troops, he dramatically overestimated the size of the landing force and withdrew his siege forces, retreating northward along the coast to Guingamp, a distance of approximately 120 kilometers.[15]

Northampton, reinforced and moving inland from Brest, soon engaged Charles's army at Morlaix on 30 September 1342 in what became the first significant land battle of the Hundred Years' War fought in France. At Morlaix, the English employed defensive tactics and superior use of longbowmen, elements that would characterize English military success throughout the Hundred Years' War. The English force, numbering approximately 1,500 men, was heavily outnumbered by Charles's army of roughly 3,000 men. However, Northampton positioned his troops on rising ground and had his men dig concealed pits and ditches to impede the French cavalry charge. When the French attacked in successive waves, the English longbowmen, armed with powerful yew bows capable of penetrating armor, inflicted devastating casualties on the charging horsemen. The French could not overcome the English defensive position, and after repeated failed assaults, Charles withdrew from the field, leaving the English victorious.[16][17]

### Edward III's Campaign

Following the English success at Morlaix, King Edward III himself arrived in Brittany in October 1342 with additional reinforcements, though not in the numbers he had hoped for due to difficulties in assembling sufficient shipping. Edward proceeded to besiege the important town of Vannes, seeking to consolidate English and Montfort control over southern Brittany. However, the siege proved inconclusive, and the English advance was soon checked.

The numerical advantage shifted dramatically against the English when Philip VI raised a much larger French army and marched it into Brittany. This large French force, commanded by royal princes and numbering in the thousands, advanced toward Edward's position near Vannes and came to a halt at Malestroit, approximately 18 miles (29 kilometers) from the English camp. With Edward's forces facing a massive French army and the supply situation becoming critical due to inability to send sufficient provisions and reinforcements from England, both monarchs agreed to negotiate.

### Truce of Malestroit

On 19 January 1343, the Truce of Malestroit was sealed between Edward III and Philip VI at the town of Malestroit. This truce was mediated by papal emissaries and was intended to pause the Hundred Years' War for approximately three and a half years, lasting until 29 September 1346. The truce provided that both monarchs would send delegations to Avignon, then the seat of the Papacy, where Pope Clement VI would mediate negotiations aimed at achieving a permanent peace settlement between England and France.[18][19]

The Truce of Malestroit formally suspended major military operations between the English and French crowns, though the underlying conflict remained fundamentally unresolved. Both Edward and Philip viewed the truce as a temporary respite rather than as a genuine peace agreement, and neither side engaged in good-faith negotiations during the subsequent peace conference in Avignon. The truce also raised questions about the status of the Breton conflict, as it suspended the active military engagement between the French and English armies that had characterized the conflict since 1342.

### Aftermath

Although the Truce of Malestroit brought large-scale military operations to an end in 1343, the Breton Civil War itself continued, albeit at reduced intensity and often at the periphery of larger Franco-English military operations. The Montfort faction retained control of western and northern Brittany, while the Blois faction controlled the east and south. The fundamental question of the duchy's succession remained unresolved through the 1340s and 1350s. The conflict would eventually continue with renewed intensity following the expiration and renewal of various truces between England and France, culminating in the definitive English-supported victory of John of Montfort (John IV) at the Battle of Auray in 1364, which resulted in the death of Charles of Blois and the end of active hostilities in 1365.[20]
