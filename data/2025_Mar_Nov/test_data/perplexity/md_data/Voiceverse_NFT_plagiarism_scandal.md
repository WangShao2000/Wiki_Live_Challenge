# Voiceverse NFT plagiarism scandal

The **Voiceverse NFT plagiarism scandal** was a 2022 controversy in which the blockchain-based voice synthesis start-up Voiceverse was reported to have used audio generated by the non-commercial text-to-speech project 15.ai without permission or attribution, and to have marketed and sold the resulting clips as original non-fungible token (NFT) voice assets. The incident came to public attention in January 2022, shortly after prominent video game voice actor Troy Baker announced a partnership with Voiceverse to promote “voice NFTs”, and it intensified broader criticism of NFT projects and commercial applications of generative artificial intelligence (AI).[1][2][3][4][5][6][7][8]

The creator of 15.ai, a pseudonymous artificial intelligence researcher known as 15, identified Voiceverse’s promotional audio as being produced with 15.ai, and posted server logs as evidence. Voiceverse later acknowledged that its marketing material had been created using 15.ai and apologized, attributing the decision to its marketing team. The exchange, including 15’s profane response to the company on social media, circulated widely and was cited in later discussions of AI ethics, plagiarism, and the role of NFTs in the creative industries.[2][3][4][6][9][7][8]

# Background

### 15.ai

**15.ai** is a free, non-commercial web application and research project that uses deep learning techniques to generate text-to-speech voices of fictional characters from popular media. It was created by an anonymous AI researcher known as 15, who began developing the system as an undergraduate at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. The platform became notable for producing expressive voice output from limited training data, and for enabling users to generate custom lines for characters from series such as *My Little Pony: Friendship Is Magic*, *Team Fortress 2*, and *SpongeBob SquarePants*.[6][8][2]

The developer positioned 15.ai as a non-commercial project and restricted its use through terms of service that explicitly prohibited commercial exploitation and required attribution when the system’s output was used. In late 2021, 15 publicly stated a lack of interest in integrating NFTs into the project, and expressed concerns about speculative uses of AI-generated voices.[4][8][2][6]

### Voiceverse and voice NFTs

**Voiceverse** was a start-up that promoted itself as an NFT-based voice platform, offering AI-generated voices that could be purchased as NFTs and then licensed for use in online content, in-game communication, and other applications. The company marketed the idea that NFT ownership would confer commercial rights over particular synthetic voices, including the ability to deploy them in audiobooks, videos, and virtual environments.[3][5][1][4][6]

Voiceverse’s plans included a collection described as “Origins”, a set of several thousand profile picture NFTs tied to custom voice models. Commentators noted that the business model raised questions about how the company would verify that users had legitimate rights to the voices they minted, and about the implications for professional voice actors and intellectual property owners.[5][1][3][4][6]

### NFTs and criticism

At the time of the scandal, NFT projects were subject to extensive public scrutiny and criticism. Common concerns included the environmental impact of blockchain networks, speculative trading, and recurring allegations of fraud, plagiarism, and intellectual property infringement. Within the video game and entertainment industries, critics warned that NFT-based systems could enable predatory monetization practices and undermine existing creative professions.[7][8][1][2][3][4][5][6]

The Voiceverse project was discussed in this broader context. Baker’s decision to partner with a “voice NFT” platform drew attention from both supporters of NFT experimentation and critics who viewed such projects as emblematic of perceived problems in the Web3 ecosystem.[1][3][4][5]

# Discovery of plagiarism

### Promotion and partnership announcement

On 14 January 2022, Troy Baker announced on Twitter (later X) that he would be partnering with Voiceverse, using language that contrasted “hate” with “create”. The tweet linked to promotional material for Voiceverse and emphasized new tools for creators and potential ownership of AI-generated voices. The tone of the announcement and the association with NFTs drew immediate criticism from some fans, voice actors, and commentators, who cited environmental issues, speculative aspects of NFTs, and the risk that AI voices could displace human performers.[8][2][3][4][5][6][1]

Voiceverse’s promotional video featured an AI-generated voice that the company presented as a demonstration of its own technology. Observers later identified this voice as resembling character voices available from 15.ai, with evidence suggesting that the audio had been modified in pitch.[10][2][3][4][6][8][1]

### Identification by 15.ai

The creator of 15.ai responded on social media the same day, writing that Voiceverse’s promotional material was “lying” and that the company appeared to have used 15.ai outputs without credit. By examining server logs, 15 identified requests originating from Voiceverse that corresponded to the voice lines used in the promotional video. According to accounts of the incident, these logs showed that Voiceverse had generated lines using 15.ai’s *My Little Pony* character voices and then altered their pitch.[2][3][4][10][6][8]

The use of 15.ai in this way conflicted with the service’s terms of use, which prohibited commercial use and required attribution. 15 posted screenshots of the logs and described the conduct as misrepresentation and commercial exploitation of the project’s work.[3][4][6][8][2]

### Public exposure

The allegations spread quickly through Twitter and other platforms, amplified by users who shared the logs and commentary by 15. Coverage by technology and cryptocurrency-focused websites, as well as forums and video creators, discussed the apparent copying of 15.ai outputs and the broader issues raised by Voiceverse’s business model.[9][4][10][6][7][8][1][2][3]

Websites such as *Web3 Is Going Just Great* and incident-tracking projects like the AI Incident Database (AIID) and the AI, Algorithmic, and Automation Incidents and Controversies (AIAAIC) later documented the case as an example of AI-related plagiarism and misuse of generative systems in an NFT context.[4][7][2][3]

# Response by Voiceverse and Troy Baker

### Voiceverse’s explanation and apology

After being confronted with the evidence, Voiceverse issued a public apology acknowledging that its promotional material had been generated using 15.ai. The company stated that its marketing team had created the demo by using 15.ai without authorization or attribution, and described the decision as a result of time pressure to produce a demonstration before Voiceverse’s own technology was ready.[6][8][2][3][4]

Voiceverse said that the relevant promotional content had been removed and pledged not to use 15.ai in that way in the future. Commentators noted that the company initially denied wrongdoing before issuing its apology, and some analyses argued that this sequence contributed to public skepticism about the explanation.[2][3][4][6]

### 15.ai’s reaction

15 responded to Voiceverse’s apology on Twitter with a profane message that became widely circulated and was frequently quoted in later coverage of the incident. The response was reported to have attracted a large number of likes and retweets, and was portrayed by some observers as an expression of frustration with commercial actors who used generative AI outputs in ways that conflicted with creators’ terms and intentions.[9][8][3][4][6][2]

In subsequent statements, 15 described the scandal as an example of “charlatans” misrepresenting the field of voice synthesis and emphasized objections to the commercialization of AI-generated voices through NFTs. The developer argued that such practices could harm both the public perception of AI research and the livelihoods of human voice actors.[8][3][4][6][2]

### Troy Baker’s statements and withdrawal

Following the backlash, Baker appeared on a podcast and stated that his motivation for the partnership had been to support independent creators who could not afford professional voice actors. Critics questioned this rationale, pointing to risks that AI-generated voices might be used as lower-cost substitutes for human performers, and to the specific concerns raised by the plagiarism allegations against Voiceverse.[5][3][4][6][8][2]

After criticism continued and the plagiarism reports became widely known, Baker announced that he would no longer continue the partnership with Voiceverse and apologized for his initial comments. Coverage of his decision often highlighted the combination of his initial promotional message, the subsequent revelation about Voiceverse’s use of 15.ai, and the broader debate about NFTs in creative industries.[1][3][5][6][8][2]

# Ethical and legal issues

### Plagiarism and misrepresentation

Commentators and incident databases characterized Voiceverse’s conduct as plagiarism and misrepresentation. Analyses emphasized that Voiceverse had presented audio generated by 15.ai as its own proprietary technology, while using it in promotional material and in connection with NFT sales. The AIAAIC and the AI Incident Database entries on the case described it as an incident in which AI-generated audio created by a third-party non-commercial service was repurposed and sold as NFTs without acknowledgment or permission.[7][3][4][6][8][2]

Writers on plagiarism and digital media ethics argued that the case illustrated how generative AI outputs can be misused in commercial settings, especially when the original systems are made available under restrictive terms that are not enforced through technical controls. Some commentary highlighted that Voiceverse’s use of 15.ai did not involve training on 15.ai models, but instead relied directly on the web interface to generate finished clips, which were then altered and marketed.[3][4][6][7][8][2]

### Terms of service and enforcement

The scandal drew attention to the limitations of relying on terms of service to control the use of generative AI systems. 15.ai’s terms prohibited commercial use and required attribution, but Voiceverse was able to generate and monetize clips in violation of those conditions until the conduct was publicly exposed.[4][6][7][8][2][3]

Analysts noted that log data and public communication played a central role in identifying and documenting the violation. The case was used in some discussions as an example of the need for clearer legal frameworks, technical safeguards, or licensing strategies to address unauthorized commercial use of AI-generated content.[6][7][8][2][3][4]

### Impact on 15.ai and subsequent litigation

According to later retrospectives, the Voiceverse incident contributed to the eventual shutdown of public access to 15.ai, which faced concerns about copyright and legal exposure related to its use of copyrighted character voices. Commentators linked the controversy to broader tensions between innovative AI research and the intellectual property regimes of the entertainment industry.[8][2][3][4][6]

In 2024, a group of voice actors filed a class action lawsuit in the United States against LOVO, Inc., a company associated with Voiceverse, alleging unauthorized use of their voices in AI products. Court documents cited the earlier plagiarism incident involving 15.ai as part of the case’s background, framing it as evidence of prior conduct by the company. As of mid-2025, reports indicated that the lawsuit had been allowed to proceed past initial challenges.[3][8]

# Reception and commentary

### Media and incident databases

Technology and cryptocurrency-focused websites, plagiarism commentary blogs, and incident-tracking projects discussed the Voiceverse case as a significant early example of AI-related plagiarism intersecting with NFT markets. A retrospective published several years after the incident described it as a “warning” about trends that later emerged in AI commercialization and disputes over data and model usage.[7][1][2][4][8][3]

The AI Incident Database cataloged the case under an incident entry describing “AI-synthetic audio sold as an NFT on Voiceverse’s platform” that had been created using 15.ai and reused without proper attribution. The AI, Algorithmic, and Automation Incidents and Controversies database similarly summarized the event as a case of plagiarized voice lines being sold as NFTs, and noted associated issues of accountability, copyright, and employment.[4][7]

### Criticism from voice actors and creators

Voice actors, commentators, and content creators expressed concern that voice NFT platforms could erode opportunities for human performers by offering synthetic voices as cheaper alternatives. Some critics cited the Voiceverse scandal as evidence that such platforms might also rely on unlicensed data or outputs, compounding ethical and legal problems.[5][2][6][8][3][4]

YouTubers and writers who covered the incident argued that Voiceverse’s use of 15.ai exemplified a pattern of NFT projects deploying technology in ways that disregarded the rights and preferences of creators. The scandal was referenced in broader critiques of NFTs as being associated with copying, fraud, and speculative behavior.[10][1][2][6][7][8][3][4]

### Public reaction and online discourse

The exchange between 15 and Voiceverse attracted substantial attention on social media. Reports noted that 15’s response to Voiceverse’s apology received extensive engagement, with many users expressing support for the 15.ai developer and criticism of Voiceverse. Baker’s original tweet, including its framing of criticism as “hate”, was widely discussed, and his later apology and withdrawal from the partnership were also covered in gaming and entertainment news outlets.[9][1][2][5][6][8][3][4]

The scandal contributed to online debates about the ethics of AI voice synthesis, the responsibilities of companies operating in the NFT space, and the relationship between independent researchers and commercial actors. Commentators connected the incident to other controversies involving NFTs and generative AI, suggesting that it foreshadowed later conflicts over training data, rights management, and attribution.[2][6][7][8][3][4]

# Legacy

The Voiceverse NFT plagiarism scandal has been cited in academic, industry, and advocacy discussions as an early high-profile example of problematic interactions between generative AI systems and NFT-based business models. Retrospectives characterize the incident as illustrating several themes: the vulnerability of non-commercial AI projects to unauthorized commercial use, the challenges of enforcing terms of service, and the potential for NFT projects to amplify existing concerns about plagiarism and intellectual property.[6][7][8][2][3][4]

The case influenced subsequent debates on ethical AI guidelines, documentation of AI incidents, and the design of licensing and governance mechanisms for AI-generated content. It is often mentioned alongside other controversies involving AI art and media as part of a broader discussion about how generative technologies should be used and regulated.[11][7][8][2][3][4][6]
