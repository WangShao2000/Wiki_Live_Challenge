# Operation Forager Logistics

**Operation Forager Logistics** encompassed the supply and support operations for Operation Forager, the United States invasion of the Mariana Islands in June and July 1944 during World War II. This logistical undertaking represented one of the largest amphibious supply operations in the Pacific theater, sustaining the Fifth Fleet and amphibious forces over extremely long distances. The operation involved coordination of over 600 vessels—including specialized supply ships, ammunition carriers, fleet oilers, and transport vessels—operating from multiple forward bases in the Marshall Islands. Planners faced unprecedented challenges in sustaining fleet operations at distances never before attempted, managing the supply of ammunition, fuel, provisions, and equipment for a campaign that lasted significantly longer than initially planned. The logistical strategy of forward fleet bases, underway replenishment at sea, and coordinated shore party organizations proved essential to success in the assault on Saipan and subsequent operations against Tinian and Guam.

## Planning and Organizational Structure

Operation Forager was planned under the direction of Admiral Chester W. Nimitz, Commander in Chief Pacific Ocean Areas, with Admiral Raymond A. Spruance commanding the United States Fifth Fleet. On 12 May 1944, Spruance issued his operation plan for the capture of Saipan, Tinian, and Guam, with assault landings scheduled for 15 June 1944 on Saipan. Vice Admiral Richmond Kelly Turner commanded the Joint Amphibious Forces, designated Task Force 51, which encompassed the Northern Attack Force (Task Force 52) and Southern Attack Force (Task Force 53). Lieutenant General Holland M. Smith, commanding the V Amphibious Corps, directed all ground combat forces.

The logistical framework established by Fifth Fleet headquarters, designated Task Force 50, coordinated the movement of supplies from continental United States ports and forward supply bases in Hawaii and the Marshall Islands. Vice Admiral William L. Calhoun, Commander Service Force Pacific, oversaw all fleet support operations. Commodore W.R. Carter commanded Service Squadron Ten, which provided the mobile logistics support infrastructure, ultimately based at Eniwetok Atoll. This organizational structure allowed naval planners to manage an unprecedented volume of supplies and support vessels across vast ocean distances.

## Scale and Composition

Operation Forager involved over 600 vessels in direct support of combat operations, assembling approximately 800 ships in total for the campaign. The expeditionary force included an estimated 2,000 aircraft and approximately 300,000 Navy, Marine, and Army personnel. For the assault on Saipan, ground forces comprised the 2nd Marine Division, 4th Marine Division, and 27th Infantry Division, with each division numbering approximately 22,000 combat personnel when fully reinforced. The 77th Infantry Division and 3rd Marine Division were held in reserve for operations against Guam and other objectives.

The assault transport vessels available for the Saipan landings totaled 110 ships, including 37 attack transports and troop transports, 11 attack cargo ships and cargo ships, 5 dock landing ships, 47 tank landing ships, and 10 high-speed transports. These vessels carried a total of 74,986.6 measurement tons of cargo. Staging areas were geographically dispersed to utilize available port facilities: the 2nd Marine Division loaded at Hilo, Hawaii; the 4th Marine Division at Kahului, Maui; and the 27th Infantry Division on Oahu.

## Supply Constraints and Loading

Insufficient cargo space in assigned vessels necessitated substantial reductions in unit equipment. The V Amphibious Corps was forced to leave behind significant quantities of vehicles and equipment, with only 94 percent of ambulances, 83 percent of trucks, 71 percent of trailers, and 75 percent of tractors transported to Saipan. The 27th Infantry Division made extensive use of wooden pallets for cargo loading, placing between 25 and 50 percent of embarked supplies on pallets. The 2nd Marine Division placed approximately 25 percent of bulk cargo on pallets, while the 4th Marine Division managed only about 15 percent due to shortages of palletizing materials and vessel space constraints requiring accommodation of troops and equipment from multiple organizations.

Assault forces carried quantities of supplies proportionate to anticipated combat duration. The operation plan called for forces to embark with a 32-day ration supply, fuel and lubricants for 20 days, medical supplies for 30 days, ammunition for ground weapons for 7 days, and antiaircraft ammunition for 10 days. This represented 1.22 short tons per soldier, substantially more than the 0.84 short tons per soldier employed in Operation Husky, the Allied invasion of Sicily in 1943, reflecting the anticipated disruption risks posed by Japanese air and naval forces and the longer supply lines.

## Forward Supply Bases

Logistical support depended upon a system of forward supply bases at Majuro, Kwajalein, and Eniwetok Atolls in the Marshall Islands. These bases provided staging areas for assault forces and served as resupply points for returning vessels. Service Squadron Ten, under Commodore Carter, operated a fleet of 120 floating units from these locations, including three destroyer tenders, three repair ships, internal-combustion engine repair ships, movable floating drydocks ranging from 1,000 to 3,000 tons capacity, ocean tugs, and specialized cargo and ammunition barges.

The provisions stores ship Azimech exemplified the resupply operations at forward bases. Operating at Eniwetok from 6 June through early July 1944, Azimech issued 2,223 tons of dry provisions to 142 ships and 174 tons of canteen stores to 171 ships and units during June alone. The ship operated its own fleet of landing boats, which handled approximately 70 percent of cargo deliveries to vessels lacking direct mooring facilities.

Fresh and frozen provisions were supplied by dedicated stores ships including the Bridge, which provisioned Mitscher's Fast Carrier Task Force 58 in May and resumed operations at Eniwetok in early June. The Service Force managed to supply fresh produce to all combatant ships on five days out of six during the operation, and to forces ashore on approximately one day in three, reflecting ongoing supply constraints.

## Ammunition and Ordnance Supply

Sustaining the intensity of naval bombardment and ground combat required unprecedented quantities of ammunition. Six ammunition carriers—Mauna Loa, Lassen, Rainier, Sangay, Shasta, and Mazama—were positioned at Eniwetok to provide resupply throughout the campaign. Loaded ammunition barges containing all sizes of ordnance and depth charges were available at assault shipping assigned to the objectives.

The most critical resupply occurred during the Battle of the Philippine Sea in June 1944, when the ammunition ship Mazama was called forward to replenish the Fifth Fleet while Japanese air attacks occurred almost daily. This represented a high-risk operation given the concentration of Japanese aircraft in the area. Between 21 June and 7 July, when Mazama returned to Eniwetok, she discharged 3,448 short tons of ammunition to fleet units.

Most resupply vessels were not vertically loaded, which created difficulties in retrieving specific ammunition grades most urgently needed by task forces. The practice of "block loading," where successive increments of different cargo types were stowed in sequence, required complete unloading of earlier layers to access later cargo. This inefficiency contributed to longer turnaround times for ammunition vessels and delays in receiving critical ordnance.

## Fleet Fueling Operations

Underway replenishment of fuel at sea was essential for sustained operations at distances of over 1,000 miles from forward bases. Fleet oiler task groups operated under the command of Commander Task Group 50.17, with tactical direction provided by the oiler task group commander. Eight task units (16.7.1 through 16.7.8) were organized, each comprising three fleet oilers escorted by at least two destroyer escorts and sometimes a destroyer.

Fueling areas, each measuring approximately 75 miles long and 25 miles wide, were designated with names derived from major oil companies and assigned to task forces through D-plus-6 (21 June). After this date, fleet commanders requested additional fueling support as operational requirements dictated. Large fleet oilers operated continuously throughout the campaign, transferring fuel to capital ships, cruisers, and destroyers via hose connections while ships operated in close formation.

The fleet oiler Guadalupe provided an example of typical support operations. From 17 May through 13 July, Guadalupe fueled numerous combatant vessels at Majuro and Eniwetok, operated with Task Group 50.17 in fueling areas west of Saipan, and maintained a cycle of operational deployments interspersed with replenishment from commercial tankers. During this period, she issued over 200,000 barrels of fuel oil and hundreds of thousands of gallons of aviation gasoline.

Vice Admiral Spruance emphasized the critical importance of fuel supply in his operation plan, recognizing that the operation would be conducted at distances unprecedented in naval history. Fleet tankers were directed to load fuel oil to maximum draft, Diesel oil to standard stock levels, and maintain reserves of lubricants and compressed gases to support extended operations.

## Aircraft Replacement at Sea

A logistical innovation involved replacement of carrier aircraft at sea, allowing combat-damaged carriers to receive replacement aircraft without withdrawing from the combat zone. Escort carriers, operating with oiler task units, delivered replacement fighters, torpedo bombers, and dive bombers to fleet carriers throughout the campaign. The escort carrier Copahee, assigned as Task Unit 16.7.10, conducted continuous operations from April through July 1944. Departing Pearl Harbor on 17 April with 86 aircraft, Copahee delivered replacement planes at Majuro in May, returned to Pearl for loading, and subsequently launched replacement aircraft to fleet carriers during critical phases of the campaign. On 14 June, the eve of the assault, Copahee launched fighters and torpedo bombers to multiple carriers of Task Force 58.

This replenishment-at-sea system proved entirely new to Pacific naval operations. By allowing fighting carriers to remain in the combat zone without withdrawing to rear bases for aircrew and aircraft replacement, the system contributed substantially to maintaining air superiority throughout the extended campaign.

## Beach Unloading Operations

Amphibious operations at Saipan faced significant obstacles from hydrographic conditions and equipment constraints. A coral reef extending the length of the invasion beaches required all landing craft to navigate a narrow channel marked by Underwater Demolition Team #7 on the day before the assault. This passage was pre-sighted by Japanese defensive positions, creating vulnerability during periods of maximum traffic.

During high tide, amphibious tractors and amphibious trucks (DUKWs) shuttled cargo across the reef in a steady flow, utilizing a narrow window of 4 to 5 hours during each tidal cycle for direct landing craft access to beach positions. Low tide exposed coral outcroppings, necessitating establishment of offshore transfer points where supplies were moved between landing craft and amphibious vehicles by hand or with available cranes. Barge cranes required for cargo transshipment were not available in significant numbers until L plus 2 (17 June), slowing the flow of supplies ashore.

Supplies accumulated on beaches as divisions advanced inland from the beachhead. The 2nd Marine Division's supplies were frequently deposited on 4th Marine Division beaches due to the complexity of sorting cargo in the high-tempo unloading environment. When the 27th Infantry Division was called forward from floating reserve on 21 June, supplies could not be consolidated on a single beach as originally planned, straining beachmaster operations.

As ground units outdistanced supporting elements, divisions withdrew trucks from beach operations to resupply forward units, reducing shore party capability to clear beaches. Ships' captains, motivated to unload rapidly and clear the vulnerable anchorage, created scheduling disruptions. The combination of inadequate truck transport for inland hauling and changing unloading priorities resulted in vessels standing offshore with partially loaded holds while awaiting return of landing craft.

## Logistics at Guam

The amphibious assault on Guam, commencing 21 July 1944, presented logistical challenges distinct from the Saipan operation. Hydrographic conditions prevented landing craft from approaching closer than several hundred yards from the assault beaches, requiring transfer of cargo to amphibious vehicles on the coral reef itself. The loss of twenty-four LVTs during initial assault operations depleted available transport capacity.

Although 25 additional mobile cranes were provided to support supply operations, shortages persisted throughout the campaign. Unlike northern beaches where cranes could be established on the reef, all cranes on southern beaches required mounting on barges, with cargo transferred in deep water. This arrangement increased transfer complexity and reduced efficiency.

The rapid advance inland created mounting difficulties in resupply. Heavy rains damaged road networks, and shortages of bulldozers and graders impeded maintenance of supply routes. Insufficient trucks prevented maintenance of the target of five days' supply in forward dumps. An unforeseen requirement to provide food for thousands of displaced civilians strained ration reserves below desired levels, though the 5th Field Depot managed to maintain twenty days' supply of most items without complete exhaustion.

## Water Supply

Water supply at forward positions required immediate development of local sources. Each shore party engineer battalion was equipped with two distillation units capable of producing fresh water from seawater through evaporation. The 77th Infantry Division transported six distillation units to Pago Bay on Guam to supply forward units advancing in northern areas. The division managed to capture the well at Barrigada village intact, which supplied 30,000 United States gallons per day and became critical for sustained operations.

## Logistics for Tinian

Operations against Tinian, beginning 24 July 1944, benefited from supply infrastructure established through Saipan and Guam. Fuel supply presented particular challenges given the requirements for sustained aviation operations. Initially, fuel was supplied in drums, with aviation gasoline drawn from a barge moored in Tinian Harbor. Development of permanent tank farm facilities commenced in September 1944, becoming the responsibility of the 29th Naval Construction Regiment with the 18th Naval Construction Battalion as lead unit.

Harbor development including dredging, breakwater construction, and quay development for Liberty ships proceeded slowly, with completion not achieved until March 1945. Until harbor facilities became operational, landing craft mechanized and landing craft tank brought cargo ashore. Subsequently, fuel was pumped via a submarine pipeline from an oil tanker moored north of Tinian Harbor through 86,000 feet of pipeline to forward distribution points.

## Impact of Extended Campaign

The Battle of the Philippine Sea from 19 to 20 June 1944, coupled with unexpectedly fierce Japanese resistance on Saipan, extended the campaign far beyond initial estimates. The assault forces' original 32-day ration supply proved inadequate as the capture of Saipan required nearly one month of continuous combat. Extended fighting on Guam further increased supply requirements. Significantly increased ammunition consumption for naval gunfire support of ground operations required multiple resupply missions by ammunition vessels operating in dangerous proximity to the islands.

The extended campaign revealed logistical limitations that would inform subsequent operations. Vertical loading of cargo vessels proved inadequate for rapidly retrieving specialized ammunition grades. Supplies accumulated on beaches reflected the mismatch between unloading rates and forward distribution capacity. Road construction and maintenance became critical constraints on Guam, where terrain and weather limited vehicle transport.

## Medical and Personnel Support

Four hospital ships—Relief, Solace, Bountiful, and Samaritan—provided medical support throughout the operation. Two additional transport vessels (Rixey and Tryon) were available for evacuation of wounded personnel. Hospital LSTs equipped with permanent medical staffs provided initial casualty treatment at beaches, with wounded personnel subsequently transferred to transport vessels for more comprehensive care and evacuation.

No replacement drafts accompanied assault forces, as planners assumed personnel losses could be compensated through inter-unit transfers. This arrangement proved impractical as losses in the 2nd Marine Division could not be restored through transfers from the 4th Marine Division without degrading the latter's combat effectiveness. Replacement drafts were dispatched to Saipan during June and July to restore combat strength.

## Ship Repair and Maintenance

The repair ship Ajax exemplified intensive vessel maintenance required to maintain fleet ships in operational status. In June 1944, Ajax serviced 157 different fleet units, including fast battleships, light carriers, cruisers, destroyers, and support vessels. By July, as the fleet underwent repairs while preparing for subsequent operations, workload at Eniwetok increased to 173 units.

Repair ships and destroyer tenders operated at forward bases to conduct emergency and battle-damage repairs without requiring vessels to withdraw to Pearl Harbor. The availability of floating drydocks allowed repair of damaged hulls, and specialized repair ships maintained engines and internal-combustion equipment essential to amphibious landing craft and small boats. Throughout the campaign, this forward repair capability proved essential for maintaining the throughput of combat and support vessels.

## Operational Significance

Operation Forager logistics demonstrated the maturation of American naval logistics in the Pacific theater. The operation successfully sustained extended fleet operations at unprecedented distances from United States bases, supported the largest amphibious assault in Pacific theater history to that date, and maintained combat operations over several months despite unexpected intensity of Japanese resistance. The effectiveness of underway replenishment at sea, the mobile forward base system, specialized support vessel operations, and coordinated shore party organizations established the model for subsequent major operations including Iwo Jima and Okinawa.

The operation also revealed limitations informing future planning. Inadequacy of cargo space in assault shipping, insufficient landing craft and transport vehicles, limitations of ammunition vessel loading practices, and mismatches between unloading and distribution capacity influenced improvements in subsequent operations. Experience with extended combat on larger land masses demonstrated the necessity for more comprehensive beachhead logistics planning, greater allocation of materials-handling equipment, and improved coordination between assault and garrison supply operations.
