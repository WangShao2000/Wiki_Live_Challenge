# Roman invasion of Africa (204–201 BC)

The **Roman invasion of Africa** was a military campaign conducted by the Roman Republic against the Carthaginian Empire in North Africa towards the end of the Second Punic War. Lasting from 204 to 201 BC, the invasion was led by the Roman consul Publius Cornelius Scipio (later known as Scipio Africanus). The campaign successfully forced Carthage to recall its general Hannibal from Italy and concluded with the decisive Roman victory at the Battle of Zama in 202 BC. The subsequent peace treaty in 201 BC stripped Carthage of its overseas territories and established Rome as the dominant power in the western Mediterranean.[1][2][3]

# Background
By 205 BC, the Second Punic War had continued for thirteen years. While Rome had gradually regained control over Italy and successfully expelled Carthaginian forces from Iberia (modern Spain) under Scipio's leadership in 206 BC, Hannibal remained on the Italian peninsula with a veteran army.[4][2]

Upon his return to Rome, Scipio was elected consul for 205 BC. He proposed a bold strategy to end the war: instead of engaging Hannibal in Italy, Rome should invade the Carthaginian homeland in North Africa to force a recall of Carthaginian forces. This plan met stiff resistance in the Roman Senate, particularly from Quintus Fabius Maximus, who argued that dispatching a major army to Africa would leave Rome vulnerable to Hannibal.[5][6][7][4]

A compromise was reached wherein Scipio was granted the province of Sicily and permission to cross into Africa, but he was not allowed to levy conscripts. Instead, he relied on volunteers and the two "Cannae legions"—troops who had been exiled to Sicily as punishment for their defeat at the Battle of Cannae in 216 BC. Scipio spent the year training this force and gathering supplies.[1][5]

# Invasion and Early Campaign (204 BC)
In 204 BC, Scipio sailed from Lilybaeum in Sicily with an army estimated between 12,000 and 35,000 men, landing near the Carthaginian port of Utica. The Romans immediately began pillaging the countryside and laid siege to Utica to secure a port for supply lines.[8][1]

Carthage responded by mobilizing a large army under Hasdrubal Gisco, who was soon joined by Syphax, the king of the Numidian Kingdom of Western Numidia. The combined Carthaginian and Numidian force significantly outnumbered the Romans. Facing this superior force, Scipio abandoned the siege of Utica after forty days and withdrew to a fortified promontory nearby, which became known as the *Castra Cornelia*. The armies entered winter quarters in a stalemate.[9][8][1]

# Campaign of 203 BC

### Burning of the Camps
During the winter truce, Scipio entered into peace negotiations with Syphax, using the envoys to gather intelligence on the enemy camp layout. He learned that the Numidian huts were made of flammable reeds and mats. In the spring of 203 BC, Scipio abruptly broke off negotiations and launched a surprise night attack.[10][9]

Roman forces set fire to the Numidian camp, and when the Carthaginians in the adjacent camp opened their gates to assist what they believed was an accidental fire, they were ambushed by waiting Roman troops. The ensuing panic and massacre resulted in the destruction of both the Carthaginian and Numidian armies, with ancient sources claiming heavy casualties. Hasdrubal and Syphax escaped with only a fraction of their forces.[11][9][1]

### Battle of the Great Plains
Following the disaster at the camps, Carthage raised a new army reinforced by Celtiberian mercenaries. Within thirty days, Hasdrubal and Syphax confronted Scipio again at the Battle of the Great Plains (*Campi Magni*), approximately 120 kilometers (75 miles) from Utica.[9][1]

The Roman army executed a pincer maneuver, encircling and destroying the opposing forces. While Hasdrubal fled to Carthage, Syphax retreated to his kingdom but was pursued by the Roman ally Gaius Laelius and the Numidian prince Massinissa. Syphax was subsequently defeated and captured at the Battle of Cirta, allowing Massinissa to unify Numidia as a Roman ally.[2][9]

### Peace Negotiations
The twin defeats of 203 BC caused panic in Carthage. The Carthaginian Council of Elders sued for peace, agreeing to preliminary terms that included the withdrawal of all Carthaginian forces from Italy. Consequently, Hannibal and his brother Mago were recalled to Africa to defend the capital. An armistice was declared while a final treaty was debated in Rome.[12][2]

# Battle of Zama (202 BC)
The armistice collapsed in 202 BC after Carthaginians seized a stranded Roman supply fleet in the Gulf of Tunis. Hostilities resumed with the arrival of Hannibal, who landed at Hadrumetum. Scipio and Hannibal met inland near Zama Regia to attempt a final diplomatic resolution, but talks failed, leading to the decisive battle of the war.[13][4][11]

At the Battle of Zama, Hannibal commanded an army of approximately 40,000 men, including 80 war elephants and his veteran infantry from Italy. Scipio commanded roughly 35,000 men, but held a distinct advantage in cavalry due to the arrival of Massinissa's Numidian horsemen.[14][4]

Scipio countered Hannibal's elephants by arranging his infantry maniples in columns rather than the traditional checkerboard formation, creating open lanes through which the elephants were channeled harmlessly. During the infantry engagement, the superior Roman and Numidian cavalry drove the Carthaginian horsemen from the field and returned to strike Hannibal's infantry in the rear. The Carthaginian army was effectively destroyed, ending significant resistance.[15][14]

# Aftermath and Treaty (201 BC)
Following the defeat at Zama, Carthage surrendered. The peace treaty ratified in 201 BC imposed severe penalties on the Carthaginian state. The terms included:[12]
*   **Territorial Losses**: Carthage surrendered all territories outside Africa, effectively losing its empire in Iberia and the Mediterranean islands.[16][3]
*   **Military Restrictions**: The Carthaginian navy was limited to 10 triremes, and the state was forbidden from possessing war elephants.[3][16]
*   **War Indemnity**: Carthage was required to pay 10,000 silver talents to Rome over a period of 50 years.[3]
*   **Sovereignty Restrictions**: Carthage was prohibited from waging war outside Africa, and could only wage war within Africa with Rome's express permission.[12][3]

This final clause severely compromised Carthaginian sovereignty and frequently led to border disputes with Massinissa's Numidia, which Rome often arbitrated in Numidia's favor. The victory marked the rise of Rome as the hegemon of the western Mediterranean. For his success, Scipio was awarded the agnomen *Africanus*.[4]
