# "The Hand That Signed the Paper" – Wikipedia Article

**"The Hand That Signed the Paper"** is a poem by Welsh poet Dylan Thomas, written in 1933 and published in 1936 as part of his collection *Twenty-Five Poems*. The work stands as Thomas's only explicitly political poem and represents a powerful critique of political power and its devastating consequences during an era marked by rising totalitarianism in Europe.[1][2][3][4]

### Background and Publication

Dylan Thomas composed the poem in 1933 when he was only 19 years old, during a period of remarkable productivity for the young poet. At this time in Swansea, Wales, Thomas was rapidly filling notebooks with poems at an exceptional rate—nearly two-thirds of his complete poetic output would eventually come from his teenage years. The poem later appeared in *Twenty-Five Poems*, Thomas's second collection of poetry, published by J. M. Dent and Sons in September 1936. Many of the poems included in this collection had been written before those in his 1934 debut collection, *18 Poems*, as Thomas drew extensively from his extensive personal notebooks compiled between 1930 and 1935.[2][5][6][7][8]

### Historical Context

The composition and publication of "The Hand That Signed the Paper" occurred during a politically turbulent period in Europe. In 1933, the year Thomas wrote the poem, Nazi Germany was consolidating power following the Treaty of Versailles and the economic devastation of the Great Depression. Totalitarian regimes were rising across the continent, while democratic governments struggled with economic instability and social unrest. Thomas, living in this politically charged atmosphere, held decidedly left-wing sympathies and maintained anti-fascist and pacifist views, though he typically avoided explicit political engagement in his poetry. This makes "The Hand That Signed the Paper" a notable exception to his general literary practice, representing his direct engagement with contemporary political anxiety.[3][9][10][11]

### Structure and Form

The poem consists of four quatrains—stanzas of four lines each—organized with a free verse structure that avoids traditional meter and rhyme scheme. The first stanza introduces the central image; subsequent stanzas develop and elaborate upon the poem's core metaphor. The work contains 16 lines total, with the final line standing as the most concise and devastating statement of the poem's theme: "Hands have no tears to flow."[12][13][14]

### Content and Themes

The poem explores the devastating power of political authority through the central image of a hand that signs a peace treaty or similar document. The "hand" serves as a synecdoche representing the political leader as a whole, reduced to the instrument of their power. Thomas employs the metaphor of "five sovereign fingers" representing the five kings—suggesting that each finger of the leader's hand possesses regal power equivalent to that of a sovereign ruler.[15][16][1]

The poem catalogs the catastrophic consequences of the hand's signature. The first stanza declares that this hand "felled a city," "taxed the breath," "doubled the globe of dead," and caused the death of a foreign king through what appears to be a disastrous treaty. Rather than representing the leader as a complete human figure, Thomas's deliberate reduction of the political agent to merely a hand emphasizes alienation from human consequence and emotion.[14][17]

The second stanza further develops this imagery through description of physical decline: the mighty hand "leads to a sloping shoulder," and "finger joints are cramped with chalk." Thomas introduces the symbol of a "goose's quill"—the traditional instrument of royal authority—which "has put an end to murder / That put an end to talk." This paradoxical phrasing suggests that the penned agreement supposedly ending conflict through peace actually enabled greater violence.[18][14]

The third stanza shifts to the aftermath of the treaty, where consequences unfold in the form of famine, disease, and plague: "The hand that signed the treaty bred a fever, / And famine grew, and locusts came." These images evoke biblical devastation, suggesting the apocalyptic scale of suffering resulting from the leader's decision. The stanza emphasizes the profound disconnect between the act of signing—"a scribbled name"—and its massive human toll.[19][14]

The final stanza presents the hand's ultimate moral failing: though the leader's hand counts the dead, it neither softens wounds nor comforts the afflicted. The speaker declares that "A hand rules pity as a hand rules heaven," raising the political hand to divine status while simultaneously condemning it for failing to exercise mercy. The poem concludes with the devastating image that "Hands have no tears to flow," reinforcing that those wielding absolute political power have been stripped of human compassion.[14]

### Literary Devices

Thomas employs multiple literary devices to advance his critique. **Synecdoche** is fundamental to the poem's structure: the hand represents the whole political leader, while the five fingers represent the five kings or complete sovereignty. **Metonymy** operates through the "goose's quill," which substitutes for royal authority and the act of writing legislation or treaties. **Personification** grants human agency to the hand itself, allowing it to act, rule, tax, and feel—or fail to feel—independently of the full human being.[16][20][1][18]

**Metaphor** pervades the work, with the signing of a document representing the exercise of absolute political power, and the consequences of that power—famine, disease, plague—rendered through apocalyptic imagery drawn from biblical and historical sources. The repeated image of the hand throughout all four stanzas creates **structural repetition** that emphasizes the singularity and persistence of the power being critiqued. The poem's **diction** tends toward the abstract and cosmic, elevating what might be a specific historical event into a timeless statement about the nature of political power itself.[17][2][19]

### Reception and Legacy

Since its publication, "The Hand That Signed the Paper" has been recognized as a landmark of wartime poetry and political literature despite—or perhaps because of—the ambiguity surrounding which specific historical event it addresses. The poem circulated during World War II and continued to resonate with subsequent generations of readers confronting questions of political responsibility and the human cost of state decisions. Thomas himself read the poem aloud in an October 1938 broadcast for the BBC, lending it authority and reach beyond the printed page.[21][22][14]

Scholars have noted that the poem's power derives in part from its refusal to name the specific historical moment or political figure it describes, making it applicable to multiple contexts of political violence and authoritarian decision-making. This universality—combined with Thomas's distinctive lyrical language and the poem's mastery of compression and imagery—has ensured its continued study in academic and literary contexts.[23]

### Significance in Thomas's Work

"The Hand That Signed the Paper" remains significant as Thomas's sole explicitly political poem, distinguishing it from the vast majority of his work which focused on personal themes of mortality, birth, decay, and Welsh landscape. While contemporary poets of the 1930s such as W. H. Auden and Stephen Spender embraced overtly political and committed verse, Thomas's individualistic approach meant that direct political engagement was rare in his canon. The poem thus represents a unique moment in his literary career when the political urgency of the era overcame his typical resistance to ideological explicitness.[11][3]

The work has become a touchstone for understanding the intellectual and emotional landscape of the 1930s, particularly regarding left-wing intellectuals' responses to the rise of fascism in Europe and anxieties about another imminent world war.[10]
