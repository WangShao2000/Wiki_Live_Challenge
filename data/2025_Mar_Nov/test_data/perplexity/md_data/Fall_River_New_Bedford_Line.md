# Fall River/New Bedford Line

The **Fall River/New Bedford Line** (formerly the **Middleborough/Lakeville Line**) is a commuter rail line of the MBTA Commuter Rail system in southeastern Massachusetts, United States.[1] It runs south from Boston's South Station to Taunton, where it splits into branches serving Fall River and New Bedford.[1] There are ten intermediate stations on the combined section and one on each branch.[1] With a distance of 60 miles (97 km) from Boston to New Bedford and 56.6 miles (91.1 km) to Fall River, it is the second-longest line in the MBTA system.[1]

The line originated as the Old Colony Railroad, which opened between Boston and Plymouth in 1845 and expanded to serve Fall River and New Bedford through a network of branch lines built in the 19th century.[2] The line became part of the New York, New Haven and Hartford Railroad in 1893 and remained the principal passenger rail connection for southeastern Massachusetts for much of the 20th century.[2] Commuter service peaked in the early 20th century and began declining in the 1910s.[1] The New Haven discontinued all passenger service on the line in 1959, effectively ending regular rail access to the South Coast region for over six decades.[1]

The MBTA began planning to restore service to the Middleborough line in the 1970s, with the Plymouth and Kingston lines reopening in 1997.[1] The South Coast Rail project, intended to extend commuter rail service to Fall River and New Bedford, underwent decades of planning and multiple route proposals before being implemented in phases beginning in March 2025.[1] The Fall River/New Bedford Line service opened on March 24, 2025, as the first phase of the South Coast Rail project, providing the first regular passenger rail service to Fall River and New Bedford since 1958.[1]

# History

### Early origins and development

The Fall River/New Bedford Line originated from multiple railroad companies built during the early railroad era of southeastern Massachusetts. The Taunton Branch Railroad, one of the earliest railroads in Massachusetts, opened between Mansfield and Taunton in 1836 as a branch of the Boston and Providence Railroad.[3] In 1840, the New Bedford and Taunton Railroad extended the Taunton Branch Railroad southward to New Bedford, establishing a 20-mile connection between Taunton and New Bedford.[4]

The Fall River Branch Railroad, incorporated in March 1844, opened between Myricks Junction and Fall River on June 9, 1845, providing a route north from Fall River to the New Bedford and Taunton Railroad at Myricks.[5] The same year, the Fall River Branch Railroad merged with the Middleborough Railroad and the Randolph and Bridgewater Railroad to form the Fall River Railroad.[5] This consolidated railroad extended throughout 1846, with the final segment between North Bridgewater and Middleborough opening on December 21, 1846.[5]

The Middleborough and Taunton Railroad opened between southeastern Taunton and Middleborough in July 1856, completing the connection between Taunton and Middleborough.[6] This network of lines was gradually consolidated under the Old Colony Railroad, which dominated southeastern Massachusetts rail service by the 1870s and 1880s.[7]

### Old Colony Railroad era

In 1854, the Fall River Railroad merged with the Old Colony Railroad to form the Old Colony and Fall River Railroad.[5] Through a series of consolidations and acquisitions, the Old Colony Railroad expanded to control most rail lines in southeastern Massachusetts and parts of Rhode Island, operating routes to Plymouth, Fall River, New Bedford, Newport, Providence, and Cape Cod.[7]

The Old Colony Railroad was leased by the New York, New Haven and Hartford Railroad on March 1, 1893, beginning more than 65 years of New Haven Railroad ownership and operation.[7] All Old Colony lines became known as the "Old Colony Division" of the New Haven system.[7] During the early 20th century, commuter service on the Old Colony Division peaked between approximately 1898 and 1914.[1] In 1898, daily inbound service to Boston from the Middleborough line included six trains from Cape Cod, three from Fall River, six from Middleborough, seven from Campello, and one from Stoughton via the West Bridgewater Branch.[1]

### Twentieth-century decline

Service on the Fall River–Middleborough segment ended in 1931.[1] During the 1930s, the New Haven Railroad faced severe financial difficulties following its 1935 bankruptcy. In May 1939, the Old Colony Division proposed to abandon all freight and passenger service on its lines. The Supreme Court of the United States ruled in *Palmer v. Massachusetts* (1939) that a district court could not order discontinuance of intrastate passenger service, preventing complete abandonment.[8]

After this court decision, a compromise schedule took effect on March 31, 1940, cutting service by nearly half while preserving operations on the remaining lines.[1] The second track between Bridgewater and Buzzards Bay was removed over the following two years.[1]

On February 18, 1941, the Interstate Commerce Commission refused the New Haven's application to abandon the Boston Group lines, which included the Middleborough line, forcing continuation of operations on the Old Colony Division.[1] However, World War II provided only temporary relief from mounting deficits. Sharp cuts in March 1949 removed most off-peak service; the Middleborough line was left with two inbound trains from Cape Cod and three from Middleborough.[1]

Under the 1951–1954 presidency of Frederic C. Dumaine Jr., the New Haven increased passenger service using new Budd Rail Diesel Cars to reduce operational costs.[1] By April 1954, there were three daily inbound trains from Cape Cod, four from Middleborough, and six from Brockton.[1]

However, Patrick B. McGinnis's assumption of the New Haven presidency in 1954 resulted in deferred maintenance and cancellation of service expansion plans.[1] The New Haven again proposed to abandon all Old Colony Division service. A temporary state subsidy was introduced in 1958 to continue service for an additional year while the Southeast Expressway and other highways were under construction.[1] The final day of passenger service was June 30, 1959, as the subsidy expired, effectively ending regular rail access to Fall River and New Bedford for over six decades.[1]

### MBTA era and service restoration

In November 1965, the MBTA purchased the Old Colony main line between Boston and South Braintree from the New Haven.[1] By 1984, a state-directed MBTA study determined that restoration of commuter rail service on the former Old Colony lines would be feasible.[9] A Draft Environmental Impact Statement released in May 1990 examined restoration options for the Middleborough, Plymouth, and Greenbush lines.[9]

Limited weekday service with four daily round trips on the Middleborough/Lakeville Line began on September 29, 1997, with full service, including weekend service, commencing on November 30, 1997.[9] In 2001, weekend and some weekday trains began stopping at JFK/UMass station.[9]

### South Coast Rail project

The South Coast Rail project aimed to extend MBTA commuter rail service from Boston to the South Coast communities of Taunton, Fall River, and New Bedford. Feasibility studies were released in 1990 and 1995, with the MBTA filing initial plans in 1995.[1] Planning was suspended in 2002 due to increasing costs but resumed several years later.[1] In 2008, the state announced an agreement with CSX Transportation for the purchase and upgrade of freight lines in the region.[10]

The state and CSX completed the first phase of the agreement on June 11, 2010, transferring the South Coast Rail lines to MassDOT for $21.5 million.[11] Environmental impact statements in 2011 and 2013 recommended a route via Stoughton that would require reactivation of an abandoned section of rail line and full electrification.[1]

In March 2017, the project was split into two phases due to high costs and long construction timelines.[1] Phase 1 would provide interim diesel service to Fall River and New Bedford via Middleborough as an extension of the existing Middleborough/Lakeville Line, with completion targeted for 2024 at a cost of approximately $1.1 billion.[1] Phase 2, called the Full Build, would complete the northern section between Boston and the South Coast via the Stoughton route with full electrification, intended to open in 2029 at a total project cost of $3.42 billion.[1]

The MBTA issued construction contracts for the project beginning in August 2020.[10] Construction proceeded throughout the early 2020s, with the Freetown and Fall River stations completed substantially by December 2022.[12] However, multiple delays pushed back the planned opening, with the MBTA announcing in June 2024 that the opening would be delayed to May 2025.[1]

# Operations

### Route and stations

The Fall River/New Bedford Line runs approximately 60 miles from Boston's South Station to New Bedford and 56.6 miles to Fall River.[1] The northern portion of the line follows the Middleborough Main Line, running approximately north–south from Boston to Middleborough.[1] The northernmost section from South Station through Boston, Quincy, and Braintree is shared with the Kingston Line and Greenbush Line.[1] The line continues south through Randolph, Holbrook, Avon, Brockton, West Bridgewater, East Bridgewater, and Bridgewater, with intermediate stations at JFK/UMass, Quincy Center, Braintree, Holbrook/Randolph, Montello, Brockton, Campello, and Bridgewater.[1]

In downtown Middleborough, the line turns west at Pilgrim Junction just north of Middleborough station.[1] It follows the Middleboro Secondary west to Cotley Junction in southern Taunton, then the New Bedford Main Line southeast to Myricks via East Taunton station.[1] From Myricks, the New Bedford Branch follows the New Bedford Main Line to New Bedford station via Church Street station, while the Fall River Branch follows the Fall River Secondary to Fall River station via Freetown station.[1] On the Fall River Branch, the line passes through Berkley and Freetown before reaching Fall River.[1]

Modern stations on the South Coast portion of the line include East Taunton, Freetown, Church Street (in New Bedford), and New Bedford on the New Bedford Branch, as well as Freetown and Fall River Depot on the Fall River Branch.[13] East Taunton serves as a transfer point between shuttle trains and through trains.[14] Layover yards for the line are located in Fall River and New Bedford, plus an older yard in Middleborough.[1] All stations feature full-length high-level platforms.[1]

### Current service

Service on the Fall River/New Bedford Line began on March 24, 2025, initially with diesel multiple units that were later to be replaced with electric trains.[1] Weekday service on the main portion of the line operates approximately every 70 minutes with 15 weekday trips on the Fall River Branch and 17 trips on the New Bedford Branch, totaling 32 trips between South Station and East Taunton on weekdays.[15] Weekend service increased from 10 round trips to 13 round trips on the main line in 2025.[1] The earliest weekday inbound trains depart Fall River and New Bedford at 4:27 a.m. and 5:05 a.m., respectively, with the last trains for both cities departing South Station at 11:59 p.m.[15] Late-night service is available, with the last train departing Boston around midnight.[15]

Direct trips from New Bedford to Boston's South Station generally take 94 to 98 minutes, with some transfer-required trips taking approximately two hours.[15] The MBTA fare structure categorizes all stations into Zone 8, with a one-way ticket from Fall River or New Bedford to Boston's South Station priced at $12.25, or $6 for those eligible for reduced fares.[1] Free parking was initially provided at new stations through the end of April 2025, subject to availability.[16]

Phase 2 of the South Coast Rail project, called the Full Build, would provide a more direct route via the Stoughton Line with electrification, but it currently lacks funding and has no established timeline for construction.[15][16] In 2022, a South Coast Rail spokesperson projected that the train service would accommodate around 1,610 passengers daily from Fall River and New Bedford, alleviating congestion on highways such as Route 24.[1]

# Infrastructure

### Track segments and subdivisions

The Fall River/New Bedford Line uses several distinct track segments built in the 19th and 20th centuries. The Middleborough Main Line, which forms the northern section, runs north–south from Boston to Middleborough.[1] The Middleboro Secondary runs from Middleborough southwest to Cotley Junction (near Weir Village) in Taunton, incorporating portions of the Middleboro and Taunton Railroad opened in July 1856.[6]

The New Bedford Main Line, also known as the New Bedford Subdivision, runs from Cotley Junction south to New Bedford along a former New York, New Haven and Hartford Railroad line.[10] The Fall River Secondary runs from Myricks Junction south to Fall River along another former NYNH&H line.[10] The Fall River Branch Railroad completed the original line from Myricks to Fall River on June 9, 1845.[10] The Old Colony and Newport Railway extended this line to Newport, Rhode Island, in 1864.[10]

In October 2008, the state announced an agreement with CSX Transportation to purchase the Fall River Secondary and New Bedford Main Line for the South Coast Rail project.[10] The purchase was completed on June 11, 2010, with the Massachusetts Coastal Railroad assuming freight rights on both lines.[10] The MBTA issued a $403.5 million contract for reconstruction of the Middleborough Secondary and New Bedford Main Line portions in August 2020.[10]

### Freight operations

Freight operations on the line are handled by the Massachusetts Coastal Railroad, which operates on both the Fall River Secondary and New Bedford Main Line.[10] The Massachusetts Coastal Railroad interchanges with CSX Transportation for through freight service.[10] Freight service continues on both branches alongside passenger operations, with freight passing sidings located at various stations.
