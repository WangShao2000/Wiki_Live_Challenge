# Chapel of St. Cornelius the Centurion

The **Chapel of St. Cornelius the Centurion** is an Episcopal chapel located on Governors Island in New York City, United States. Situated within New York Harbor, the chapel is part of Trinity Church's parish and dedicated to Cornelius the Centurion, a biblical figure honored as the first Gentile convert to Christianity. The chapel's history spans nearly two centuries, beginning with a wooden structure erected in 1847 and replaced by a substantial stone building designed in the English Gothic style and completed in 1906. Following the closure of military operations on Governors Island in 1996, the chapel has been under the sole ownership of Trinity Church and has served as a venue for performing arts and cultural installations.

## History

### Early establishment

During the first seven decades following American military occupation of Governors Island, no dedicated chapel existed at the garrison. Religious services were conducted in temporary shelters or open air. In 1844, the Reverend John McVickar, a professor of moral philosophy at Columbia College and an Episcopalian priest, was appointed as chaplain to the United States Army forces at Fort Columbus on Governors Island. McVickar began advocating for a proper chapel building to serve the spiritual needs of soldiers and their families stationed at the fort.[1][2]

With support from Major General Winfield Scott, the chief of the United States Army, who allocated 150 square feet of land on the south side of the island for a church site, McVickar formulated plans for a dedicated chapel. The federal government declined to provide funding for the construction, but McVickar successfully raised donations from wealthy members of Trinity Church in Manhattan to support the project, and he contributed a substantial sum of his own money. McVickar personally designed the wooden structure, which was described at the time as "a neat and tasteful church-like building of wood."[3][1]

The original chapel was dedicated on April 19, 1847, by Bishop William H. DeLancey of the Episcopal Diocese of Western New York. Following its establishment, Trinity Church provided annual financial appropriations to support the chapel from 1846 to 1855, and in 1868 the chapel formally became part of Trinity Parish. McVickar continued to serve as the chapel's chaplain until 1862, when he retired at the age of 75 after eighteen years of service.[2][1]

### Replacement and reconstruction

By the beginning of the twentieth century, the original wooden structure had deteriorated and was condemned. In response, Trinity Church commissioned architect Charles C. Haight to design a new chapel building. Haight, a Civil War veteran and accomplished architect known for his ecclesiastical designs, created a plan for a substantial stone edifice in the English Gothic style. The cornerstone was laid on October 27, 1905, and the building was completed the following year.[2]

The new structure, dedicated on October 17, 1906, was constructed from buff Indiana limestone and designed in the manner of fourteenth-century English Gothic architecture. It is a cruciform building featuring a massive tower, a nave, transepts, a chancel, and a side chapel known as St. Albans Chapel. The building measures 106 feet in total length and 70 feet at its greatest width. The interior incorporates carved oak clergy and choir seats, with the nave and St. Albans Chapel furnished with chairs for worshippers. A mortuary chapel is located beneath the chancel and is vaulted with flat Italian tile. The clergy and choir sacristies and the organ are situated on the south side of the chancel. Electronic carillon chimes were installed in 1949 as a gift from the garrison.[2]

### Military period and chaplaincy

Throughout its operational history, the chapel was served by chaplains from various sources, including Trinity Church, the United States Army's chaplain services, and the United States Navy Chaplain Corps. Reverend Edmund Banks Smith, an Episcopal priest and Army chaplain, was among those who served at the chapel and later authored an early history of Governors Island published in 1913. Morgan Dix, Rector of Trinity Church, wrote a detailed account of the chapel's history and significance at the time of its dedication in 1906.

In 1966, when the United States Coast Guard assumed control of Governors Island from the Army, the Coast Guard took possession of the chapel and added additional chaplain positions to the island's complement. The chapel remained an active place of worship for military personnel and their families stationed on the island throughout this period.[3]

### Contemporary period

Following the closure of military operations on Governors Island in 1996, Trinity Church became the sole owner of the chapel. The building was no longer regularly used for religious services; as of 2014, it held no scheduled worship activities. However, the building retained its architectural integrity, including original stained-glass windows and a collection of historically significant military artifacts, including battle flags and other relics associated with units that had been stationed on the island.[1]

In the twenty-first century, the chapel has served as a venue for cultural programming and contemporary art. The building has hosted performing arts events and art installations, welcoming public audiences to experience the historic structure in new contexts. This adaptation reflects the chapel's transition from active military-religious service to a site of public cultural engagement while maintaining its historic significance as a landmark within the Governors Island Historic District.

## Namesake

The chapel is dedicated to Cornelius the Centurion, a figure from New Testament history. According to the Acts of the Apostles (10:1–11:18), Cornelius was a Roman centurion stationed in Caesarea who was sympathetic to Jewish religious practice and believed in the one God of Israel, making him what was known as a "God-fearer." His conversion and baptism, facilitated by the Apostle Peter, marked a significant moment in early Christian history as he and his household are regarded as the first known Gentiles to convert to the Christian faith. The conversion of Cornelius set a precedent for the inclusion of non-Jewish believers in the early Church and is documented in the Acts as a pivotal event in Christian missionary expansion.[4]

## Architecture

The 1906 chapel designed by Charles C. Haight represents a refined interpretation of English Gothic Revival architecture adapted for late nineteenth-century American ecclesiastical practice. The building's cruciform plan, with its substantial central tower rising from the crossing, creates a prominent silhouette visible from the harbor. The exterior is constructed of buff Indiana limestone, a material chosen for its durability, elegant appearance, and resistance to weathering. The architectural details, including pointed arch windows, buttresses, and castellated elements, reflect Gothic Revival principles while the interior spatial arrangement—with its nave, transepts, and chancel—follows traditional cruciform church planning that emphasizes the sanctuary space.
