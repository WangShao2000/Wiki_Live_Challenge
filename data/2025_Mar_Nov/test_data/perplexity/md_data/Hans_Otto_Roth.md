# Hans Otto Roth

Hans Otto Roth (29 April 1890 – 1 April 1953) was a Transylvanian Saxon lawyer and politician who represented the German ethnic minority in Romania during the interwar period and World War II. Born in Sighișoara (German: Schäßburg), Roth studied law at several European universities before earning his doctorate from the Eötvös Loránd University Faculty of Law in Budapest in 1913. He was elected to the Chamber of Deputies in 1919 and served as Parliamentary Chairman of the German Party from 1922 to 1938, focusing on minority rights and education. During World War II, Roth took a public anti-Nazi stance that put him at odds with the radical Nazi-oriented leadership within the German ethnic group, particularly after the rise of the "renewal movement." Though offered ministerial positions, he declined them due to his opposition to the Iron Guard's influence. In 1940, he briefly served as Minister of State for Minorities in the Gigurtu cabinet before transitioning to work as a church official. After the 1944 Romanian coup d'état, he attempted to protect the German minority from Soviet deportations before being arrested by the communist regime. He died in Ghencea prison in Bucharest in 1953.[1][2]

# Early Life and Education

Hans Otto Roth was born on 29 April 1890 in Sighișoara, a medieval town in Transylvania that had been a center of Transylvanian Saxon culture since the 12th century. He was the son of Karl Roth (1846–1901), a lawyer, and Louise Roth, née Hausenblaß (1855–1915). His grandfather, also named Karl Roth, had served as a leading municipal administrator in Sighișoara. Roth attended elementary school in Sighișoara from 1896 to 1900, followed by the Evangelical Gymnasium in the same town from 1900 to 1908.[3][1]

Between 1908 and 1912, Roth pursued legal studies at the universities of Budapest, Vienna, Berlin, and Zurich. He completed his doctoral degree in law from the Eötvös Loránd University Faculty of Law in Budapest in April 1913. During his university years, Roth was politically active. In the 1907–1908 academic year, he served as Rex Chlamydatorum (leader) in the Coetus, a student organization of the Schäßburg Gymnasium. Between 1910 and 1912, he chaired the "Bund Sächsischer Hochschüler" (Union of Saxon University Students).[2][1]

# Early Career

Following his graduation, Roth worked as a lawyer in Budapest from 1913 to 1915. He then served in the Austro-Hungarian Army during World War I from 1915 to 1917. After the war, from 1917 to 1918, he worked as an editor at the Siebenbürgisch-Deutsches Tageblatt (Transylvanian German Daily), an important German-language newspaper based in Hermannstadt (Sibiu).[1]

# Political Career

### Early Postwar Period and Entry to Parliament

Roth's political career began shortly after the unification of Transylvania with Romania in 1918. He served as a lawyer and secretary of the "Zentralausschuss" (Central Committee) and the "Deutscher Volksrat für Siebenbürgen" (German People's Council for Transylvania), working toward the political integration of ethnic Germans into the new Romanian state. In January 1919, Roth was one of the principal speakers at the Saxon assembly in Mediaș, where Saxon delegates voted to support Transylvania's union with Romania. He was a member of the delegation that presented the Saxon decision to the Romanian government and King Ferdinand I.[4][1]

As a member of the leadership committee of the "Deutsch-Sächsischer Nationalrat für Siebenbürgen" (German-Saxon National Council for Transylvania), Roth helped prepare the program for the Fourth Sachsentag (Saxon Day) held in Schäßburg in November 1919. In 1919, Roth was elected to the Chamber of Deputies representing the German Party (Deutsche Partei in Rumänien).[2][1]

### Parliamentary Leadership

Roth was elected ten times during the interwar period, serving continuously in parliament from 1919 to 1938. His long parliamentary tenure led to his automatic elevation to the Senate upon reaching the necessary service requirements, where he held a lifetime seat beginning in 1938. By 1922, Roth had become the Parliamentary Chairman of the German Party, a position he held until his retirement from parliament in 1938. During his time in parliament, Roth prioritized issues related to denominational schools and cooperation between Germans and other ethnic minorities.[1][2]

Unlike his rival Rudolf Brandsch, who advocated for the German minority to align with the National Party of the Transylvanian Romanians, Roth believed that the German minority should remain independent and cooperate pragmatically with whatever government was in power. This strategy proved effective, resulting in the official recognition of German-language schools, churches, and associations, which provided German communities with opportunities for cultural development.[2][1]

### World War II and Anti-Nazi Activities

In July 1940, Prime Minister Ion Gigurtu appointed Roth as Minister of State for Minorities. Roth served in this position from 4 July to 4 September 1940. When Ion Antonescu came to power in September 1940, he approached Roth several times with offers to join the cabinet as Justice Minister, and later as Education Minister, eventually offering him his choice of ministry. Roth declined all these offers, citing his opposition to the presence of the Iron Guard in government cabinets.[5][1][2]

As Germany's influence over Romania grew and the "renewal movement" (Erneuerungsbewegung), a radical Nazi-oriented organization, gained strength within the German ethnic group, Roth increasingly distanced himself from Nazi ideology. On 15 June 1933, while serving as chairman of the Association of German Minorities in Europe (a position he held from 1931 to 1934), Roth met with Adolf Hitler and used the opportunity to protest the persecution of Jews in Germany, warning Hitler of the potential damage such policies could cause to German minorities in Europe.[1][2]

By the late 1930s and 1940s, Roth had become openly opposed to National Socialism within the German community. In September 1939, at the outbreak of World War II, he published an article expressing his concerns about the conflict. The ideological conflict between Roth's Christian liberal views and the Nazi ideology promoted by the renewal movement came to a climax in 1943 when Andreas Schmidt, the Volksgruppenführer (ethnic group leader) of the German minority, publicly criticized Roth. Following this confrontation, Roth was expelled from the German Party.[6][2][1]

### Church Leadership and Later Activities

Despite his expulsion from the German Party, Roth continued to serve the German community through various positions. From 1921 to 1923, he was chairman of the German Theater Association. From 1928 to 1932, he served as president of the Hermannstädter Allgemeine Sparkasse (Hermannstadt General Savings Bank), and from 1938 to 1942 as second president of the Siebenbürger Elektrizitätsgesellschaft (Transylvanian Electrical Company). From 1939 to 1941, he was president of the board of directors of the Hermannstadt Electric Works, and from 1939 to 1942, he chaired the board of the Siebenbürgisch-Deutschen Verlag (Transylvanian German Publishing House), which published the Siebenbürgisch-Deutsches Tageblatt.[1]

From 1935 to 1948, Roth also maintained a law practice in Bucharest. His most significant ecclesiastical position came in 1932, when he became Landeskirchenkurator (regional church curator) of the Evangelical Church of the Augsburg Confession in Romania, serving as the highest lay representative of this Protestant denomination. In this capacity, he held the title of chief attorney from 1927 and served as a member of the Land Consistory from 1926 to 1932. He worked to defend the church against Nazi ideology and attempted to prevent its Nazification, though his efforts largely failed.[1]

# Later Life and Death

After the 1944 Romanian coup d'état on 23 August, which saw King Michael I overthrow Ion Antonescu and realign Romania with the Allies, Roth attempted to represent the leaderless German minority. He called upon Saxons to remain loyal to the Romanian state and worked with other former parliamentary colleagues to draft protective measures for the German minority. Together with Rudolf Brandsch, he sought to prevent the deportations of ethnic Germans to the Soviet Union that began in early 1945, but these efforts were unsuccessful.[6][2][1]

Following the Soviet occupation and subsequent communist takeover of Romania, all German organizations were disbanded and the old elites faced arrest, deportation, or execution. In spring 1948, Roth was arrested for the first time and held in the Bucharest Interior Ministry for approximately eight weeks. In April 1952, he was arrested again by the Securitate (Romanian communist secret police). He received an administrative sentence of 24 months, with the official grounds listed as his previous status as a "former deputy and senator."[2][1]

Roth died on 1 April 1953 in Ghencea prison camp in Bucharest at age 62. His death occurred less than a week before his sentence would have been completed.[2][1]
