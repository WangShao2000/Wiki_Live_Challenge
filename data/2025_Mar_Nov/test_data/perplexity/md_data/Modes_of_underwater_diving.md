# Modes of Underwater Diving

Underwater diving, the practice of descending below the water's surface to interact with the environment, encompasses a variety of modes, each with its own set of techniques, equipment, and procedures. These modes are broadly categorized by the diver's exposure to the surrounding water pressure and the type of breathing apparatus used. The primary modes include freediving, scuba diving, surface-supplied diving, and atmospheric diving. Each method carries its own set of risks and benefits, influencing its application in recreational, scientific, and commercial diving operations.[1][2][3][4]

The choice of diving mode depends on factors such as the depth and duration of the dive, the tasks to be performed, and the level of risk deemed acceptable. While recreational diving is largely self-regulated, professional diving activities are typically governed by occupational health and safety legislation to mitigate the inherent dangers of the underwater environment. Technological advancements throughout history have continuously expanded the capabilities of divers, allowing them to explore greater depths and remain underwater for longer periods.[2][5]

# Ambient Pressure Diving

Ambient pressure diving involves the diver being directly exposed to the pressure of the surrounding water. This category includes freediving, scuba diving, and surface-supplied diving.[1][2]

### Freediving

Freediving, also known as breath-hold diving or skin diving, is the simplest and oldest form of diving, relying on the diver's ability to hold their breath until resurfacing. This mode of diving requires minimal equipment, typically including a mask, fins, and a wetsuit for thermal protection and buoyancy.[5][6][1]

#### Techniques and Safety

Proper technique is crucial for safe and effective freediving. This includes efficient finning, proper body positioning to minimize drag, and mastering equalization techniques to prevent barotrauma to the ears and sinuses. Relaxation and breath-hold techniques are fundamental to maximizing time underwater and preventing loss of consciousness.[6][7]

Safety is a primary concern in freediving, and the most critical rule is to never dive alone. A trained buddy system is essential for monitoring the diver and providing immediate assistance in case of a shallow water blackout or other emergencies. Other safety measures include creating a thorough dive plan, being aware of environmental conditions such as depth, visibility, and currents, and carrying emergency equipment like a rescue buoy.[7][8][6]

### Scuba Diving

Scuba diving utilizes a self-contained underwater breathing apparatus (SCUBA), allowing the diver to carry their own supply of breathing gas. This independence from the surface grants the diver greater mobility and a wider range of movement compared to surface-supplied diving.[9][10][1]

#### Equipment

Scuba diving equipment, or scuba gear, consists of several key components. The core of the system is the breathing apparatus, which includes a diving cylinder containing compressed gas, a regulator to reduce the pressure of the gas for breathing, and a demand valve. Buoyancy is controlled by a buoyancy control device (BCD), and weighting systems are used to counteract the diver's natural buoyancy. Other essential pieces of equipment include a diving mask for clear vision, fins for propulsion, and a diving suit for thermal protection.[11][12]

#### Open-Circuit and Closed-Circuit Systems

Scuba systems can be either open-circuit or closed-circuit. In an open-circuit system, the diver exhales directly into the water, and the entire volume of each breath is replaced with fresh gas from the cylinder. Closed-circuit rebreathers, on the other hand, recycle the diver's exhaled gas by scrubbing it of carbon dioxide and replenishing the oxygen consumed by the diver. Rebreathers are more gas-efficient, allowing for longer dive times, but are also more complex and expensive than open-circuit systems.[13]

### Surface-Supplied Diving

In surface-supplied diving, the diver is connected to the surface via an umbilical that supplies breathing gas, communications, and sometimes hot water for heating. This mode of diving is predominantly used in professional applications such as commercial and scientific diving due to its enhanced safety and the ability to provide a virtually unlimited gas supply.[4][10][1]

#### Equipment and Procedures

Surface-supplied diving equipment typically includes a diving helmet or a full-face mask, a diver's umbilical, and a surface-based gas panel and control system. The umbilical is a bundle of hoses and cables that connects the diver to the surface support station. A bailout cylinder with a separate gas supply is often carried by the diver as a safety precaution in case of a primary gas supply failure.[10][12]

The advantages of surface-supplied diving include a lower risk of drowning, a continuous and reliable gas supply, and direct communication with the surface team. However, the diver's mobility is limited by the length of the umbilical, and the logistical and equipment costs are significantly higher than those for scuba diving.[10]

# Atmospheric Pressure Diving

Atmospheric pressure diving isolates the diver from the ambient pressure of the water by enclosing them in an atmospheric diving suit (ADS). This allows the diver to work at great depths for extended periods without the need for decompression.[14][2][1]

### Atmospheric Diving Suits (ADS)

An ADS is a single-person, articulated submersible that resembles a suit of armor, with its own life support system. The suit's internal pressure is maintained at or near surface atmospheric pressure, protecting the diver from the physiological effects of deep diving, such as nitrogen narcosis and decompression sickness.[15][16][17][14]

#### History and Design

The concept of an armored diving suit dates back to the 18th century, but the first truly functional ADS was the *Tritonia*, invented by Joseph Salim Peress in 1932. Early designs struggled with joint flexibility and watertightness under pressure, problems that were eventually solved with the development of fluid-filled and articulated joints. Modern atmospheric diving suits, such as the *Newtsuit* and the *Exosuit*, are constructed from lightweight materials like aluminum and feature advanced joint technology that allows for a high degree of mobility.[17][15][14]

# Saturation Diving

Saturation diving is a technique used for deep commercial and scientific diving that allows divers to live and work at great depths for extended periods. The principle behind saturation diving is that once a diver's tissues have absorbed the maximum amount of inert gas at a given pressure, the decompression time required remains the same regardless of how much longer they stay at that depth.[16][18]

### Physiology and Procedures

Saturation divers live in a pressurized habitat on the surface, which is maintained at a pressure equivalent to their working depth. They are transported to and from the worksite in a diving bell, which is also pressurized. This eliminates the need for repeated decompressions after each dive, which would be impractical and increase the risk of decompression sickness.[18][16]

The decompression process at the end of a saturation diving project is a slow and carefully controlled procedure that can take several days. Divers are monitored closely for any signs of decompression sickness during this time. The use of helium-based breathing gases is common in saturation diving to mitigate the narcotic effects of nitrogen at depth.[19][16][18]

# Breathing Gases in Diving

The composition of the breathing gas used by a diver is a critical factor in safe diving, particularly at greater depths. While compressed air is the most common breathing gas for recreational diving, specialized gas mixtures are used in technical and commercial diving to manage the risks of oxygen toxicity, nitrogen narcosis, and decompression sickness.[20][21]

### Nitrox

Nitrox, also known as enriched air nitrox, is a breathing gas mixture with a higher percentage of oxygen than air, typically between 22% and 40%. The reduced nitrogen content in nitrox allows divers to extend their no-decompression limits and reduce the risk of decompression sickness. However, the increased partial pressure of oxygen in nitrox also increases the risk of oxygen toxicity, which limits the maximum depth at which it can be safely used.[19][20]

### Trimix and Heliox

For deep diving, trimix and heliox are commonly used breathing gases. Trimix is a mixture of oxygen, helium, and nitrogen, while heliox is a mixture of just oxygen and helium. The addition of helium, a less narcotic and less dense gas than nitrogen, helps to reduce the effects of nitrogen narcosis and the work of breathing at great depths.[21][19]

Hypoxic trimix, which has an oxygen content lower than that of air, is used for very deep dives to avoid oxygen toxicity. Divers using hypoxic trimix must use a different "travel gas" at shallower depths, as the low oxygen content of the bottom gas is not sufficient to support consciousness at or near the surface.[20][21][19]
