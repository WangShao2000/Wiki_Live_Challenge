# Kingston Line

The **Kingston Line** is a commuter rail line of the MBTA Commuter Rail system in southeastern Massachusetts, United States. It runs 35.1 miles (56.5 km) southeast from Boston's South Station to Kingston, with eight intermediate stations serving passengers in communities along the South Shore. The line historically extended to Plymouth, which served as a second outer terminal, but Plymouth station has been indefinitely closed since April 2021. In October 2022, the line recorded 4,171 boardings per weekday.[1]

The Kingston Line's origins trace to the Old Colony Railroad, which opened between Boston and Plymouth on November 10, 1845, making it the first rail connection to southeastern Massachusetts. The Old Colony expanded through mergers and acquisitions over subsequent decades, eventually becoming the dominant railroad system in the region before being leased by the New York, New Haven and Hartford Railroad in 1893. Passenger service declined after World War I, and the New Haven terminated all commuter service in 1959. The line remained inactive for nearly four decades until the Massachusetts Bay Transportation Authority began restoring service in the 1990s as part of a federally-funded multi-modal transportation initiative. Service resumed in 1997 as the Plymouth/Kingston Line, becoming one of the MBTA's most significant capital projects of that decade. The line was renamed the Kingston Line following the permanent closure of Plymouth station.[2][3][4][1]

### History

#### Old Colony Railroad Era (1845–1959)

The Old Colony Railroad Company was chartered by the Massachusetts Legislature on March 16, 1844, to provide rail connectivity between Boston and the southeastern portion of the state. John Sever of Kingston was elected as the company's first president. Construction commenced in South Boston in 1844, and the inaugural ceremonial train departed on November 8, 1845, carrying company officials and dignitaries. The first regular service began on November 10, 1845, when two trains operated the 37-mile (60 km) route between South Boston and Plymouth, marking the beginning of rail service to the region.[3][4]

The Old Colony Railroad's success led to its expansion throughout eastern Massachusetts. Between 1845 and 1893, the company constructed branch lines and acquired existing railroads, building what became one of the region's most extensive rail networks. By 1845, the Old Colony had acquired the Fall River Railroad, and in 1854, it formally consolidated with the Fall River Railroad to form the Old Colony and Fall River Railroad. The company continued expanding through additional mergers: in 1863, it became the Old Colony and Newport Railway, and in 1872, it reverted to the name Old Colony Railroad after absorbing the Cape Cod Railroad. By the 1890s, the Old Colony system covered more than 600 route miles and served numerous destinations including Providence, Newport, Fall River, New Bedford, and Cape Cod. The Boston-Plymouth mainline featured seven inbound trains each weekday in 1898 at the height of operations.[5][6][7][3]

To accommodate growing traffic, the Old Colony made substantial infrastructure improvements. The mainline was relocated between South Boston and Crescent Avenue in 1897–1898 to eliminate grade crossings in preparation for the opening of South Station in 1899. The line was quadruple-tracked from South Boston to Savin Hill in 1901–1902, extended to Neponset in 1911–1912, and further to Atlantic in 1913. Double-track sections were added between Kingston and Plymouth in 1900 and between South Braintree and Whitman in 1907.[8]

The New York, New Haven and Hartford Railroad, the dominant northeastern carrier, leased the Old Colony Railroad in 1893, integrating it into its broader system. Under New Haven management, the company sought to consolidate its many local stations into a larger facility, ultimately constructing Boston's South Station and rerouting Old Colony lines to that new terminal.[9][1][3]

Passenger service declined significantly after World War I due to increasing automobile competition and changing travel patterns. Despite decades of local opposition to service termination, the New Haven discontinued all commuter rail service on the Old Colony Division in 1959, ending more than a century of passenger rail operations to southeastern Massachusetts.[1][8][5]

#### Service Interruption and Line Closure (1959–1997)

Following the termination of service in 1959, the Boston-Plymouth line remained inactive for nearly four decades. Freight service continued sporadically; the Bay Colony Railroad operated occasional freight trains on the line until 1994, just before the MBTA's restoration project commenced. The railroad corridor itself remained largely intact, preserving the right-of-way for potential future restoration.[10]

#### Restoration and Modern Service (1997–Present)

In the 1980s, the Massachusetts Bay Transportation Authority began planning to restore commuter rail service to the South Shore region. The restoration project was part of a larger federal agreement that provided funding for multi-modal transportation improvements in the Greater Boston area, which also led to the restoration of service to Middleborough/Lakeville and the construction of the Greenbush Line. Construction commenced in 1993, and the MBTA completed the restoration by 1997.[8][2][1]

Service resumed on September 29, 1997, as the Plymouth/Kingston Line, with rush-hour trains operating initially. Full off-peak and weekend service began on November 29, 1997. The project included significant infrastructure modifications: a completely new right-of-way, described as the first new section of mainline rail in Massachusetts since the Needham Cutoff in 1906, was constructed along Massachusetts Route 3 and through previously undeveloped land. This new construction allowed for the creation of Kingston station as a purpose-built park-and-ride facility away from downtown Kingston to minimize traffic through residential areas. The Kingston Branch, a short spur from the Plymouth Branch to Kingston station and the Kingston Layover yard, was constructed during this restoration effort.[11][12][1]

In October 2011, the MBTA began construction of a $2.5 million wind turbine at Kingston station to generate electricity for the station and layover facility. The 120-foot (37 m) turbine was completed in January 2012, though it produced only approximately 60 percent of its expected energy output.[11]

#### Plymouth Station Closure (2021–Present)

Plymouth station, which had served as a secondary terminus with limited service, was indefinitely closed in April 2021 due to budget cuts and operational constraints. The closure was initially characterized by the MBTA as temporary in response to fiscal pressures and pandemic-related ridership declines. However, service has not been restored as of December 2025.[13][12][1]

Operational challenges contributed to the permanent nature of the closure. The configuration of serving both Kingston and Plymouth required trains to reverse direction, creating inefficiencies that complicated scheduling. Single trains could not efficiently serve both terminals simultaneously; instead, three daily trips formerly operated sequentially to both stations, effectively doubling operational requirements. Following the Plymouth closure, the line was renamed the Kingston Line, with Kingston station becoming the sole outer terminus.[10][1][11]

Despite efforts by local business leaders and elected officials to restore service, the MBTA cited operational challenges as the basis for maintaining the closure. An alternative shuttle bus service operated by the Greater Attleboro and Taunton Regional Transit Authority (GATRA) was established to connect Plymouth residents to Kingston station. As of late 2024, the MBTA indicated no plans to restore Plymouth service in the near term.[13]

### Operations

The Kingston Line follows two primary segments: the Middleborough Main Line and the Plymouth Branch. The northern section from Boston to Braintree operates on the shared Middleborough Main Line, which is also used by the Fall River/New Bedford Line and the Greenbush Line. At Braintree, service transitions to the dedicated Plymouth Branch, which continues southeast through South Weymouth, Abington, Whitman, Hanson, Halifax, and Kingston.[1]

Eight intermediate stations serve the line between South Station and Kingston:

1. JFK/UMass
2. Quincy Center
3. Braintree
4. South Weymouth
5. Abington
6. Whitman
7. Halifax

At Kingston, the Plymouth Branch splits into the Kingston Branch, a short spur running south to Kingston station and its associated layover facility. The Plymouth Branch itself continues southeast from this junction point toward Plymouth, where service has been suspended since 2021.[1]

All stations on the Kingston Line are fully accessible to passengers with disabilities, including level boarding platforms and elevator access where required. The line provides park-and-ride facilities at Kingston and other major stations. The Kingston station parking area is accessed via Marion Drive, which crosses the railroad line at grade just east of the platform.[11]
