# 2001 Biggin Hill Airshow Disasters

The **2001 Biggin Hill Airshow disasters** refers to a series of aviation accidents occurring during June 2–3, 2001, at London Biggin Hill Airport in Bromley, London, England. Three separate incidents occurred during the weekend Biggin Hill Airshow: a landing mishap involving a de Havilland Venom that resulted in minor damage, followed by two fatal crashes of vintage military aircraft. On June 2, a de Havilland Vampire crashed during a paired display, killing both pilots. The following day, a Bell P-63 Kingcobra crashed during its display sequence, killing the pilot. Three people died across the two fatal accidents. The Air Accidents Investigation Branch (AAIB) conducted separate investigations into each incident and identified wake turbulence, pilot decision-making, and communication failures as contributing factors. Despite the tragic events, the airshow resumed operations the following year and continued annually until 2011.

# Incidents

### June 2: Venom Landing Incident

On the morning of June 2, 2001, three aircraft operated by a Bournemouth-based company—a de Havilland Sea Vixen, a de Havilland Venom, and a de Havilland Vampire—departed from Bournemouth Airport in formation for the Biggin Hill Airshow.[1] The Venom, a single-seat aircraft, was piloted by 69-year-old Clive Rustin, the company's chief pilot.[1] Rustin had over 5,500 hours of flying experience, including approximately 242 hours on the Venom.[1]

As the formation began its landing approach at Biggin Hill, the three aircraft executed a "run in and break" manoeuvre from line astern formation to initiate individual landings on Runway 21.[1] During the pre-landing checks, Rustin performed the undercarriage lowering procedure but subsequently checked the flap position indicator and noticed the flaps were beyond the intended one-third setting.[1] He corrected the flap position but made a critical error: he did not verify that the undercarriage had actually been lowered.[1] The undercarriage position indicator lights were dim and difficult to discern from their position in the lower left of the instrument panel, and Rustin's concentration was divided between maintaining proper spacing with the other aircraft and avoiding their slipstreams.[1]

As he entered the final approach, Rustin transmitted to air traffic control (ATC) "Finals three greens," indicating he believed the undercarriage was fully down.[1] However, the aircraft touched down on its belly. Nose vibrations during the landing run should have alerted the pilot, but Rustin did not realize until ATC informed him that the landing gear had not been lowered.[1] The Venom sustained damage to its drop tanks and fuselage underside, with crushed components preventing the aircraft from participating in the airshow displays.[1] The runway was subsequently closed for approximately three hours while recovery crews removed the damaged aircraft.[1] Rustin evacuated the aircraft uninjured, making the ejection seat safe before exiting the cockpit.

The AAIB investigation identified three contributing factors: a confusion between the similar-looking undercarriage and flap levers located in close proximity; poor visibility of the undercarriage position indicator due to its location and light intensity; and the pilot's divided attention while executing a stream landing in close proximity to other aircraft.[1]

### June 2: Vampire Crash

Following the runway closure necessitated by the Venom incident, the Biggin Hill Airshow organizers scheduled the de Havilland Vampire and de Havilland Sea Vixen to perform at the end of the afternoon's display program. The pilots, Air Marshal Sir Kenneth Hayr (pilot-in-command) and Jonathan Kerr (safety pilot), conducted a briefing and developed a display sequence involving paired and individual manoeuvres.[2] Sir Kenneth, aged 66, was a retired senior Royal Air Force commander who had served as Deputy Air Officer Commanding-in-Chief Strike Command and Chief of the Defence Staff (Commitments) from 1988 to 1990.[3] He held a Private Pilot's Licence with display authorization and had accumulated 4,398 hours of flying experience, including 185 hours on the Vampire.[2] Jonathan Kerr, the safety pilot seated in the right cockpit, was positioned to assist with lookout as the left-seat view was restricted.

At approximately 1702 hours (local time), the two aircraft took off separately and joined in formation behind the crowd line.[2] The display sequence began with a run-in along Runway 03, followed by a coordinated 405-degree level turn to the left.[2] As the Sea Vixen initiated its right wing-over manoeuvre, reducing speed to approximately 220 knots, the Vampire—which was to execute the same manoeuvre but with delayed timing to maintain separation—began its roll to the right.[2]

As the Vampire reached approximately 80 degrees of bank to enter its wing-over, the aircraft continued its right roll beyond the planned angle, becoming fully inverted with its nose dropping into a steep descent.[2] The aircraft then rolled to the erect position but continued its descent angle of approximately 56 degrees below the horizon.[2] Within seconds, the Vampire impacted the ground in a field of tall grass beyond the airfield boundary, approximately 850 metres north of Runway 03/21.[2] The impact occurred at approximately 1702 hours local time, with both pilots fatally injured.[2] The aircraft disintegrated upon impact, with wreckage scattered more than 130 metres from the principal point of impact.[2]

The impact was witnessed by approximately 35,000 airshow spectators. Video recordings of the final moments of the accident were limited; one digital recording captured the aircraft's descent, providing critical evidence for the investigation.[2]

#### Investigation and Cause

The AAIB investigation ruled out mechanical failure and pilot incapacitation as primary causes. The aircraft's structure and flying controls remained intact until impact, and no evidence suggested either pilot was physically incapacitated or unable to control the aircraft.[2] The investigation commissioned an aerodynamic study examining wake turbulence effects. Modeling showed that the de Havilland Sea Vixen, significantly heavier (14,784 kg at operating weight versus the Vampire's 4,400 kg), generated trailing vortices three to four times stronger than those of the Vampire.[2] These vortices, when encountered at the Vampire's speed and bank angle, could generate roll accelerations exceeding 70 degrees per second squared—probably greater than the Vampire's available aileron roll authority at that speed.[2] The AAIB concluded that a rapid 90-degree roll to the right, as observed in the accident, was the likely result of the Vampire encountering the Sea Vixen's wake vortices during the wing-over manoeuvre.[2]

### June 3: Kingcobra Crash

On June 3, the second day of the airshow, a Bell P-63A-7BE Kingcobra (registration G-BTWR) performed an aerobatic display.[4] The aircraft was piloted by Guy Bancroft-Wilson, a 43-year-old experienced warbird display pilot holding an Airline Transport Pilot's Licence with display authorization for Category C aircraft.[4] Bancroft-Wilson had 7,730 hours of total flying experience and had flown the Kingcobra on at least 10 previous occasions since first flying the type in April 1998.[4] He had completed 25 minutes of Kingcobra display practice on June 1, 2001, the day before the crash.[4]

The Kingcobra displayed with two other American World War II fighter aircraft, initially flying jointly and performing manoeuvres including loops and half Cuban eights.[4] After approximately one minute of solo display following separation from the other aircraft, the Kingcobra executed a rolling, climbing manoeuvre during which the pilot appeared to lose control at the top of the manoeuvre, resulting in an incipient spin.[4] The aircraft recovered, and Bancroft-Wilson continued the display. The aircraft then performed a wing-over to the left and returned to fly past the crowd at a speed of 190 to 195 knots.[4]

The aircraft subsequently turned out of view for approximately 20 seconds, executing a right-hand turn of approximately 220 degrees, before running directly toward the crowd line.[4] The pilot pulled up into the first half of a loop manoeuvre.[4] At the top of the manoeuvre, with the aircraft inverted, full nose-up elevator input was maintained while substantial right rudder was applied.[4] The aircraft yawed to the right and entered an incipient spin.[4] The nose dropped steeply, the rudder returned to neutral, but full nose-up elevator continued to be applied.[4] The aircraft did not recover from the ensuing dive and impacted the ground at approximately 160 knots in a steep nose-down attitude.[4]

The crash occurred at 1444 hours local time on June 3, 2001, within the airfield boundary west of the runway, approximately 100 metres beyond the high-speed display line.[4] An immediate fire was extinguished by airport fire service personnel, but Bancroft-Wilson was fatally injured in the impact.[4]


Video evidence analyzed by AAIB investigators revealed that the Kingcobra was flown throughout the display at progressively decreasing speeds.[4] Entry speeds for the first loop were estimated at 250 to 270 knots, but speeds declined to below 200 knots by the time of the crowd fly-past preceding the final manoeuvre.[4] Video analysis of the final manoeuvre indicated that the aircraft entered with insufficient airspeed for the vertical loop to be completed successfully.[4] This speed deficiency was attributed to a continuing loss of energy throughout the display, exacerbated by an earlier failed manoeuvre during which the aircraft had already suffered a loss of altitude.[4]

During the final manoeuvre, the control inputs—particularly the substantial rudder application when inverted at the top of the loop—were inconsistent with a normal display manoeuvre recovery.[4] Instead, they were similar to control positions used to deliberately initiate a spin entry.[4] The AAIB investigation concluded that the primary cause of the loss of control was insufficient aircraft speed when attempting the loop manoeuvre.[4] The investigation noted that an experienced warbird pilot of Bancroft-Wilson's caliber would be expected to recognize performance issues and discontinue the display; the absence of a pre-planned and practiced sequence of manoeuvres may have contributed to decision-making difficulties.[4] The investigation suggested that an unknown factor affecting the pilot's physical and/or mental performance during the display could not be excluded, though no evidence of mechanical control jamming or pre-impact mechanical failure was discovered.[4]

A Flying Display Committee member expressed concern about the loss of control during the first rolling climbing manoeuvre and attempted to contact air traffic control to stop the display, but communication difficulties across the airfield (the ATC tower was located on the opposite runway side from the crowd line) prevented the system from functioning as intended.[4]

# Aftermath and Response

Following the conclusion of the airshow weekend, the Air Accidents Investigation Branch opened three separate formal investigations into the Venom, Vampire, and Kingcobra incidents.[5] The AAIB's findings were published in three individual accident investigation bulletins, with particular focus on the mechanisms of wake turbulence affecting aircraft during air displays and communication issues between display coordinators and air traffic control.[5]

As a direct response to the communication difficulties that prevented intervention during the Kingcobra display, the airshow organizers installed a direct telephone line between the Flying Display Committee (positioned on the crowd side of the runway) and the Display Director in the air traffic control tower.[4] This upgrade was intended to facilitate more effective communication and rapid display termination if safety concerns arose.

The resident community of Biggin Hill experienced conflicting emotions regarding the weekend's events. While acknowledging the tragic nature of the accidents, there was significant public division over whether the airshow should continue.[5] Despite these concerns, the Biggin Hill Airshow resumed operations in 2002 under the direction of Air Displays International, which had organized the show since 1982.[5] The airshow continued to be held annually at Biggin Hill until 2011, when organizers replaced it with an open house air day format.[5] In 2014, a scaled-down version of the classic airshow was resurrected as "The Biggin Hill Festival of Flight."
