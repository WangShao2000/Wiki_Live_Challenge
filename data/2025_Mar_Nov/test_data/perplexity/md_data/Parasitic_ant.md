# **Parasitic ant**

A **parasitic ant** is a species of ant that relies on the labor and resources of another ant species (the host) to survive and reproduce. Unlike most ants, which establish independent colonies with their own queens and workers, parasitic ants exploit the social structure of host colonies. This phenomenon, known as social parasitism, has evolved independently at least 60 times within the family Formicidae. The dependence on the host varies from temporary association during colony founding to complete, lifelong reliance, often accompanied by the loss of the worker caste.

Parasitic ants are classified into three primary categories based on their life history strategies: temporary social parasites, slave-making ants (dulotic species), and inquilines (permanent social parasites). The relationship between parasite and host is often governed by Emery's rule, which posits that social parasites are usually closely related to their host species.

# Types of parasitism

Social parasitism in ants manifests in distinct forms, ranging from facultative associations to obligate dependence.

### Temporary social parasitism
In temporary social parasitism, the parasitic queen depends on a host colony only during the initial founding of her own colony. A newly mated parasitic queen invades a host nest, often killing or driving away the resident queen. The host workers subsequently accept the intruder and rear her brood. As the parasitic queen's offspring emerge, they gradually replace the host workers, who eventually die of natural causes. Once the host workforce is depleted, the colony consists entirely of the parasitic species and functions independently for the remainder of its existence. This strategy is common in the genera *Formica*, *Lasius*, and *Polyrhachis*.[1][2][3][4][5]

### Dulosis (Slave-making)
Dulosis, or slave-making, involves parasitic ants raiding the nests of other species to capture developing brood (larvae and pupae). These captured individuals hatch in the parasite's nest and function as workers, performing tasks such as foraging, brood care, and nest defense. Slave-making species can be facultative or obligate. Facultative slave-makers, such as *Formica sanguinea*, are capable of surviving without slaves but often conduct raids to augment their workforce. Obligate slave-makers, such as the genus *Polyergus*, possess specialized morphological adaptations—such as sickle-shaped mandibles for combat—that render them incapable of feeding themselves or caring for their brood without the assistance of enslaved workers.[6][7][8][9]

### Inquilinism
Inquilinism represents the most extreme form of social parasitism, where the parasite spends its entire life cycle within the host colony. Inquiline species, such as *Acromyrmex insinuator* and *Pseudoatta*, typically lack a worker caste and produce only reproductive males and females. The parasitic queen may coexist with the host queen or eliminate her. Because inquilines contribute no labor to the colony and rely entirely on the host for resources, they often exhibit "social parasitic syndrome," a set of morphological reductions including smaller body size and simplified anatomical structures.[10][1]

### Xenobiosis
Xenobiosis is a relationship in which one ant species lives within the nest of another but keeps its brood separate. Xenobiotic ants, such as *Formicoxenus*, move freely among the host ants and may obtain food from them through solicitation (trophallaxis) or theft, but they generally rear their own larvae.[4]

# Evolutionary origins

The evolution of social parasitism in ants is a subject of significant study in evolutionary biology, particularly regarding speciation mechanisms.

### Emery's rule
The evolutionary relationship between parasitic ants and their hosts is frequently described by **Emery's rule**, named after Italian entomologist Carlo Emery who first noted the pattern in 1909. The rule states that social parasites tend to be closely related to their host species.[11]
*   **Strict Emery's rule** asserts that the parasite is a sister species to the host, suggesting the parasite evolved directly from the host lineage, possibly through sympatric speciation.[12][11]
*   **Loose Emery's rule** posits that the parasite and host are close relatives (e.g., within the same genus) but not necessarily sister species, which supports models of allopatric speciation followed by secondary contact.[11][12]

Research indicates that the genus *Formica* is a "hotspot" for the evolution of social parasitism, containing a high density of parasitic species and exhibiting all major parasitic life history traits.[3]

# Mechanisms of parasitism

Successful parasitism requires overcoming the host colony's defense systems, which rely heavily on chemical recognition.

### Chemical disguise
To infiltrate a host colony, parasitic queens employ chemical strategies to mask their identity.
*   **Chemical camouflage** involves the parasite acquiring the colony odor (cuticular hydrocarbons) of the host through physical contact. For example, the queen of the temporary social parasite *Polyrhachis lamellidens* performs a specific "rubbing behavior" against host workers to transfer their scent onto herself.[5]
*   **Chemical mimicry** occurs when the parasite actively biosynthesizes cuticular hydrocarbons that match the host's profile. This allows the parasite to be recognized as a nestmate without needing constant physical contact with host individuals.[13][5]

### Behavioral adaptations
Parasitic ants also utilize behavioral tactics to facilitate integration. "Appeasement" behaviors, such as playing dead or adopting submissive postures, can reduce aggression from host workers. Conversely, some species use "propaganda substances"—pheromones that cause panic or confusion among host workers—allowing the parasites to invade during the chaos.[7]

# Host defenses and co-evolution

The relationship between parasitic ants and their hosts is often characterized as a co-evolutionary arms race. Hosts evolve defense mechanisms to detect and repel parasites, while parasites evolve more sophisticated methods of infiltration.[14]

Populations of host ants, such as *Temnothorax*, exhibit varying defense strategies based on the prevalence of parasites in their environment. In areas with high parasite pressure, hosts may resort to "fight" strategies (collective aggression) or "flight" strategies (nest evacuation) when an intruder is detected. In some cases, enslaved workers in dulotic colonies have been observed engaging in rebellion, selectively killing the pupae of the slave-making queens, thereby reducing the parasite's reproductive success.[9][15]
