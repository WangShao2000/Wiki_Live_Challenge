# Vorlesungen über die Entwicklung der Mathematik im 19. Jahrhundert

*Vorlesungen über die Entwicklung der Mathematik im 19. Jahrhundert* (*Lectures on the Development of Mathematics in the 19th Century*) is a two-volume work authored by Felix Klein and published posthumously in 1926 and 1927 by Springer in their Grundlehren der mathematischen Wissenschaften series. Klein had delivered lectures on mathematical history and relativity during World War I, which were later edited and released after his death by Richard Courant with Otto Neugebauer for Volume I and with Stefan Cohn-Vossen for Volume II. These volumes synthesize Klein’s personal insights from interactions with leading mathematicians of his era and serve as an extensive overview of 19th-century mathematics.


# Background and Publication

Felix Klein was deeply interested in the history of mathematics and amassed relevant texts for the Göttingen library. He attempted to lecture on the development of mathematics in the 19th century in the winter semesters of 1910–1911 and 1912–1913 but encountered obstacles, ultimately resuming in private sessions during the war years. The notes for these classes underwent considerable editing: Volume I was prepared by Courant and Neugebauer, while Volume II—postulating additional topics including relativity and new mathematics—was completed by Courant and Cohn-Vossen. Both editions retain much of Klein’s original style and perspective, though they are considered preliminary drafts rather than fully polished surveys.

The books' release marked a significant event in mathematical historiography. Despite incomplete content on certain subjects and somewhat subjective judgements, both volumes were regarded as substantial contributions due to Klein’s unique vantage point as both a practicing mathematician and historian.


# Content Overview

### Volume I

Volume I consists of eight chapters covering pivotal developments in 19th-century mathematics, each offering a blend of biographical and technical detail:

- **Chapter One: Gauss** focuses on Carl Friedrich Gauss’s life and work, divided into sections on applied and pure mathematics.
- **Chapter Two: France and the École Polytechnique** examines mechanical physics, geometry, analysis, and algebra, referencing Poisson, Fourier, Cauchy, Monge, and Galois.
- **Chapter Three: The Founding of Crelle’s Journal** traces the rise of pure mathematics in Germany.
- **Chapter Four: Algebraic Geometry After Möbius, Plücker, and Steiner** discusses projective geometry, invariant theory, n-dimensional spaces, and complex numbers, with attention to Grassmann and Hamilton.
- **Chapter Five: Mechanics and Mathematical Physics in Germany and England Until About 1880** contrasts Hamilton’s and Jacobi’s approaches to mechanics with advances by Green, Stokes, and Maxwell.
- **Chapter Six: General Theory of Functions of Complex Variables According to Riemann and Weierstrass** presents competing philosophies and techniques.
- **Chapter Seven: Deeper Insight into the Nature of Algebraic Varieties and Structures** explores algebraic geometry, number theory, and the works of Clebsch and Noether.
- **Chapter Eight: Group Theory and Function Theory; Automorphic Functions** covers Lagrange, Galois, Jordan, hypergeometric functions, conformal mappings, and Poincaré.

Klein initially planned full chapters on Henri Poincaré and Sophus Lie but these were never included. Set theory, Hilbert’s problems, and other elements were also deferred or left out.

### Volume II

Volume II is organized into three principal sections:

- **Chapter One: Elementary Content Regarding the Fundamentals of Linear Invariant Theory** divides into general linear invariant theory and commentaries on Erlangen Program, vector/tensor analysis.
- **Chapter Two: The Special Theory of Relativity in Mechanics and Mathematical Physics** analyzes classical celestial mechanics, electrodynamics, Lorentz group adaptation, and its consequences.
- **Chapter Three: Transformation Groups on the Basis of a Quadratic Differential Form** includes Lagrangian mechanics, intrinsic surface differential geometry following Gauss, and progresses to Riemannian manifolds and advanced geometric developments.

A further chapter on general relativity and Hamiltonian mechanics was drafted but could not be completed without external editorial intervention.

Both volumes emphasize content over narrative depth, with less emphasis on personal biography in the second volume, reflecting a systematic approach to mathematical progress.


# Reception and Legacy

Upon publication, the first volume of *Vorlesungen* was received enthusiastically and celebrated as a landmark work by contemporary reviewers. Its author, Klein, was lauded for his extensive familiarity with major mathematicians of his time, and its lack of national bias was noted. Subsequent reviews echoed this praise, although some critiqued aspects of its historiographic method.

Virtually every prominent mathematician in the late 20th century engaged with Klein’s study. Dirk Jan Struik called it “the best history of nineteenth century mathematics,” while Vladimir Arnold, renowned for his breadth of knowledge, stated that “one-half of the mathematics I know” originated from studying this text. French and Soviet historians also recognized its value, noting the deep moods and intellectual climate of 19th-century mathematics. Nonetheless, gaps in coverage—such as missing chapters on Poincaré and Lie, and the absence of an up-to-date bibliographical index—were regularly pointed out as limitations.

The impact of *Vorlesungen* extends beyond academia, influencing textbooks and subsequent historical scholarship. It remains referenced in modern discussions on mathematical culture and the evolution of foundational theories.

## Conclusion

*Vorlesungen über die Entwicklung der Mathematik im 19. Jahrhundert* occupies a central position in the historiography of mathematics. Authored by Felix Klein and subsequently refined by distinguished editors, it offers a uniquely insightful synthesis of 19th-century mathematical developments, informed by personal encounters and editorial pragmatism. The two volumes combine broad thematic exploration with selective depth, forming a touchstone for historians and practitioners alike.

The enduring enthusiasm surrounding *Vorlesungen*—its critical reception, frequent reprints, and adaptation into multiple languages—attests to its persistent relevance. By maintaining a balance between narrative clarity and academic rigor, Klein’s work continues to shape understanding of mathematics’ historical trajectories and remains integral to the scholarly record.
