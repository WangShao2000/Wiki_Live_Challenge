# 2025 Pennsylvania Supreme Court election

The 2025 Pennsylvania Supreme Court election was a statewide judicial retention contest held on November 4, 2025, to determine whether three incumbent justices—Christine Donohue, Kevin M. Dougherty, and David N. Wecht—would be given additional ten-year terms on the Commonwealth’s highest court. All three justices were retained by the electorate, ensuring the continuation of a five-to-two Democratic majority on the bench. The contest attracted significant campaign spending, exceeded $15 million, and drew intervention and debate from national figures and major interest groups. The Pennsylvania Supreme Court plays a pivotal role in shaping policy on voting rights, redistricting, abortion, and other contentious issues, making the outcome of the election consequential for the state’s legal landscape and future elections.


### Background

The Pennsylvania Supreme Court serves as the state’s highest appellate body, comprising seven justices who serve staggered ten-year terms. Justices are initially elected via partisan contests, but after completing their first term, they stand for retention via a simple yes-or-no ballot, rather than facing oppositional candidates. If retained, they earn another ten-year term. This process is designed to be nonpartisan, although in practice, the political affiliations and judicial philosophies of incumbents often become central issues during retention races.

In the years leading up to the 2025 election, the court had consistently maintained a Democratic-controlled majority. The three justices standing for retention—Donohue, Dougherty, and Wecht—were all first elected in 2015. Under the Democratic majority, the court issued several landmark rulings on matters including the validity of congressional district maps (struck down as unconstitutionally gerrymandered in 2018), protection of voting rights, and defense of abortion rights.


### Candidates

#### Christine Donohue

Justice Christine Donohue began her term in January 2016, having previously served eight years as a Superior Court judge. Raised in rural Pennsylvania, she holds a B.A. from East Stroudsburg University and a J.D. from Duquesne University School of Law. Before joining the bench, she practiced civil litigation for over two decades, holding leadership roles in numerous legal organizations and earning recognition as an accomplished trial attorney and advocate for young lawyers’ courtroom experience. She is known for her commitment to access to justice and has authored legal ethics materials and participated in civic education initiatives.

#### Kevin M. Dougherty

Kevin M. Dougherty, also beginning his tenure on the Supreme Court in January 2016, holds degrees from Temple University and Antioch School of Law. He started his legal career as an assistant district attorney in Philadelphia before entering private practice. Dougherty rose through the Philadelphia judiciary, serving in both the Court of Common Pleas and as Administrative Judge of the Family Division—where he implemented several programs aimed at supporting vulnerable youth and reforming juvenile justice. He has led efforts around access to justice and behavioral health within the courts, and represents the Supreme Court in various statewide judicial improvement initiatives.

#### David N. Wecht

Justice David N. Wecht, whose term also began in January 2016, received undergraduate and law degrees from Yale University and clerked for a U.S. Circuit Judge before practicing in New York and Pennsylvania. Before joining the Supreme Court, he served on the Superior Court and as Administrative Judge of the Fifth Judicial District Family Division. A prolific author, lecturer, and advocate, Justice Wecht has shaped family law reforms and continues to contribute to legal scholarship and inter-judicial collaboration across state and international venues.


### Campaign and Financing

Unlike traditional partisan elections, retention elections typically do not pit opponents against one another, but the 2025 contest broke new ground in its visibility, fundraising, and political mobilization. The campaign attracted record-setting spending—more than $15 million—with Democratic-aligned groups outpacing Republican efforts by as much as four-to-one. Major players included Planned Parenthood, labor unions, trial lawyers, and advocacy organizations pushing for judicial continuity.

Support for the incumbents featured television advertisements featuring Governor Josh Shapiro and appearances by leaders of national progressive coalitions. Meanwhile, Republican-aligned messaging sought to cast doubt on judicial independence, urging “term limit” arguments and calling for voter skepticism of entrenched justices. Former President Donald Trump made social media appeals urging support for “no” votes against all three justices, emphasizing partisan divides.

Despite the rhetoric about nonpartisanship, these strategies and spending patterns revealed the increasingly polarized character of judicial retention contests—a trend widely observed in swing states where courts have central roles in disputes over rights and electoral boundaries.


### Election Results

Each justice faced a yes-or-no vote regarding retention to the Supreme Court. The returns reflected strong support for the incumbents, with no justice falling below the 60% threshold for victory.

All three judges won retentions handily, enabling them to continue their work on the court and maintain the existing party balance.


### Aftermath and Implications

The successful retention of Donohue, Dougherty, and Wecht ensured that the Pennsylvania Supreme Court would retain its five-to-two Democratic majority through the next presidential election cycle. This continuity was crucial as the court remains deeply involved in ongoing state debates about voting procedures, the constitutionality of congressional maps, abortion access, and public school funding—all issues that have repeatedly led to confrontations between competing legislative interests.

If all three justices had lost, the court could have experienced a prolonged partisan deadlock, with a temporary 2-2 split and delayed appointments—an outcome feared by advocates on both sides for its potential to paralyze decision-making on vital policy questions for years. The current majority thus preserves the opportunity for stable governance in areas such as redistricting and election administration, which are especially critical in a battleground state ahead of midterm and presidential contests.

The increased involvement and spending in the 2025 retention election signaled a broader transformation in how state Supreme Court races are conducted nationwide, moving from obscurity to prominence in public consciousness. As the court continues to play an outsized role in setting precedents affecting civil liberties and democratic institutions, future retention campaigns in Pennsylvania and similar jurisdictions can expect similarly intense scrutiny and mobilization.


### Conclusion

The 2025 Pennsylvania Supreme Court retention election was marked by record spending, national-level attention, and outcomes with broad implications for state politics and jurisprudence. By retaining its Democratic-majority bench, Pennsylvania’s highest court assured continued stability and alignment with prior policies on voting rights, abortion, and government accountability. The evolution of judicial elections—from low-profile routine reviews to high-stakes contests—is likely to persist as the stakes for courts grow amid intensifying polarization and public scrutiny.
