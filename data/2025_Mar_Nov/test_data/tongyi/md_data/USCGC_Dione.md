# USCGC Dione

USCGC Dione (WPC-107) was a Thetis-class patrol boat operated by the United States Coast Guard from 1934 until 1963, designated as a cutter throughout its active service life. Named for Dione, the mother of Aphrodite in Greek mythology, Dione was constructed by the Manitowoc Shipbuilding Corporation in Manitowoc, Wisconsin, representing an advancement in the fleet intended to enforce Prohibition laws and pursue law enforcement missions along American coasts. The vessel was laid down on November 10, 1933; launched on June 30, 1934; delivered on September 28, 1934; and commissioned into service on October 5, 1934. As one of the early large patrol boats, she measured 165 feet in length, carried a crew complement up to 7 officers and 68 men during wartime, and featured robust machinery for coastal duties.

# Construction and Design

Dione was built to serve as a versatile, steel-hulled vessel, powered by two Winton Model 158 six-cylinder diesel engines generating 1,340 bhp, enabling her to reach a maximum speed of 16 knots and maintain sustained operations across significant distances. Her displacement was 337 tons full load at the time of completion, expanding to 350 tons by 1945 due to upgrades. Armament evolved over time; initially, Dione featured a single 3"/23 gun and a pair of 1-pounder guns. By 1945, she was equipped with two 3"/50 single mounts, two 20mm machine guns, sonar, radar, depth charge tracks, Y-guns, and Mousetraps for anti-submarine warfare. The cost of construction was $258,000, reflecting her status as a modern, multi-purpose cutter of her era.

# Early Service and Prohibition Era

After commissioning, Dione was assigned to Norfolk, Virginia, where she participated in enforcing Prohibition laws by intercepting rumrunners off the East Coast. In addition to her law enforcement work, she conducted training exercises and participated in local search and rescue activities, contributing to public safety efforts well before the outbreak of World War II.

# World War II Operations

With the onset of the Second Happy Time in January 1942, Dione’s role expanded dramatically. Assigned to the Fifth Naval District, she became the only sizable ship capable of opposing German U-boats operating off the North Carolina coast—a region infamously dubbed “Torpedo Alley.” During this period, Dione frequently escorted convoys traveling through dangerous waters, rescued survivors of torpedoed ships, and hunted suspected U-boat contacts using sonar and depth charges. While she never sank an enemy submarine, Dione made several impactful interventions.

Key events included:
- On March 20, 1942, an aircraft from Elizabeth City Air Station dropped depth charges following Dione’s detection of a suspected submarine contact.
- On April 5, 1942, she rescued a lone survivor from the torpedoed tanker Byron D. Benson.
- On April 18, 1942, another air-sea operation saw Dione deploying depth charges after an observed submarine attack, though no visual confirmation resulted.
- Throughout June 1942, Dione engaged multiple underwater contacts, expended depth charges, and aided in convoy defense during attacks on SS Nordland and SS Manuela.

In 1945, after undergoing major refit, Dione joined the Navy’s “Surrender Group” for the First Naval District. Notably, on May 17, 1945, she escorted German submarine U-1228 into Portsmouth, New Hampshire, marking one of the final naval interactions of WWII in American waters.

## Postwar Service and Storage

Following the war, Dione was used for law enforcement and search and rescue duties from 1945 to 1947. In 1952, after spending several years in storage due to personnel shortages, she was transferred to Freeport, Texas—becoming the first Coast Guard cutter stationed there. In this role, Dione continued to serve Gulf Coast communities, assisting in regional search and rescue, towing disabled vessels such as the cutter Iris in January 1957, and providing support during emergencies in the Gulf of Mexico.

## Decommission and Disposal

Dione served actively in the Gulf until February 8, 1963, when she was decommissioned and subsequently sold on February 24, 1964. Her final years marked the end of an era for a cutter that had played a vital part in both peace and wartime naval operations.

## Commercial Career

After sale to merchant service, Dione was renamed and reconfigured as a supply and cargo vessel. Under merchant registry, she was listed with official number 297193 and call sign WD4627. The Palmer Decker Boat Company was her initial commercial owner, though the company went bankrupt, leading to auction by a U.S. Marshal in December 1967. Subsequent names included:
- Big Trouble
- Delta I
- Al Rashid

She operated under the Sabik brand into the 1990s, but her final service ended around 1992, with no further reliable records after that date.

During her merchant phase, Dione experienced several incidents, including a major fire aboard in the Caribbean Sea in April 1969, which necessitated hull salvage. She was rebuilt but continued to change hands and identities until her operational retirement.

## Legacy and Impact

USCGC Dione remains notable for her distinguished military career, enduring presence through both prohibition-era law enforcement and the dangerous challenges of WWII convoy escort and anti-submarine operations. Her service record reflects adaptability and resilience, paralleling the broader evolution of the United States Coast Guard from a small domestic agency into a significant naval component during national crises.

Despite numerous changes in ownership and identity, the original cutter’s historical significance persists in maritime records and the collective memory of U.S. naval history. However, Dione did not receive any formal awards during her service.

## Summary

USCGC Dione stands out as a testament to adaptive naval operations over three decades, serving the United States Coast Guard in prohibition enforcement, WWII convoy and anti-submarine operations, and postwar Gulf Coast missions. Her story highlights the resourcefulness of a vessel that bridged eras and roles, ultimately transitioning to civilian use. The legacy of Dione continues to inform the history and development of America’s maritime defense and law enforcement infrastructure.
