# Breton Civil War, 1341–1343

# Introduction

The Breton Civil War (1341–1343) was a significant dynastic struggle for control over the Duchy of Brittany, emerging amid the larger context of the Hundred Years' War between France and England. This conflict began shortly after the death of Duke John III of Brittany, who died without direct heirs, leading to a dispute between two rival claimants: Charles of Blois and John of Montfort. The war quickly became intertwined with Anglo-French hostilities and involved key leaders from both sides, major sieges, and early demonstrations of English military tactics.

# Background and Cause

On 30 April 1341, Duke John III died childless. His death set off a succession crisis, as no clear successor could be determined under Breton law or local precedent. Two main claimants emerged:

- **John of Montfort**, the half-brother of the late duke and supported by much of the lower nobility and the bourgeoisie in western Brittany.
- **Charles of Blois**, nephew of King Philip VI of France and husband to Joan of Penthièvre, nephew through his mother Margaret of Valois, who had strong backing from the French crown and much of the Breton aristocracy.

This dynastic struggle was further complicated by broader international politics. While England saw an opportunity to weaken its French rival by supporting John of Montfort, France aimed to secure Brittany’s allegiance as a buffer against English influence, making the outcome politically vital.

## Sequence of Events

### Initial Campaign (Early 1341)

Shortly after the duke’s death, John of Montfort seized the capital city, Nantes, along with the ducal treasury and most fortified towns in Brittany. He quickly established himself as the new duke, though many Breton notables preferred Charles of Blois. In September 1341, the Parlement of Paris, under pressure from Philip VI, formally declared Charles of Blois the legitimate heir to the duchy—a decision largely ignored by the Montfortists.

### Capture of John of Montfort

A French royal army, led by Charles of Blois and commanded personally by John, Duke of Normandy, soon invaded eastern Brittany. Facing superior numbers and dwindling support, John of Montfort was besieged at Nantes. The city capitulated on 21 November 1341 after three weeks of siege. John was taken prisoner and held in the Louvre in Paris. His capture left the Montfortist faction leaderless.

### Leadership Transition to Joanna of Flanders

After John’s capture, his wife, Joanna of Flanders, assumed command of the Montfortist resistance. She moved the ducal treasury to Brest, recalled forces in western Brittany, and decisively set up their two-year-old son, also named John, as figurehead and heir. From this base, she rallied remaining Breton loyalists and appealed to England for assistance.

### Siege of Hennebont (Late May – June 1342)

Joanna and her small force fortified the town of Hennebont, which became the last stronghold of the Montfortist cause in western Brittany. In late May 1342, Charles of Blois advanced against the town with his army. The Montfortists repulsed repeated assaults, including a disorderly retreat by the French after a rare initial attack. Despite the English delay in reinforcements—due to storms and logistical difficulties—Joanna orchestrated a dramatic nighttime sortie with several hundred men-at-arms. Her actions successfully drove off the besiegers and lifted hope among her supporters.

However, as summer progressed and more French troops arrived, it became apparent Joanna would need direct English intervention. In late June 1342, the besieging force withdrew after limited supplies were delivered by sea.

### Relief of Brest and Battle of Morlaix (August–September 1342)

An English fleet of nearly 260 ships, carrying about 1,350 fighting men under William, Earl of Northampton, arrived at Brest in August 1342. The French galleys blockading the port were caught by surprise and many destroyed. Although vastly outnumbered, the English reinforced Brest and pushed back Charles of Blois’s forces, forcing them to abandon western Brittany.

Following the relief, the English laid siege to Morlaix. There, Northampton met Charles of Blois’s army in the first major land battle of the Hundred Years’ War (30 September 1342). Northampton deployed a standard English formation: dismounted men-at-arms flanked by massed longbowmen. Utilizing trench traps and superior archery, the English inflicted heavy casualties on the French, winning a decisive victory despite being outmatched numerically. The battle demonstrated the effectiveness of English defensive tactics and foreshadowed their future successes.

### Truce of Malestroit and Failed Peace Negotiations (January 1343)

In January 1343, two cardinals from Avignon brokered the Truce of Malestroit between England and France. It aimed to pause hostilities until September 1346 and included terms such as:

- Both sides would retain territories captured at truce’s onset.
- Release of John of Montfort from captivity and general exchange of prisoners.
- A peace conference was arranged at Avignon, mediated by Pope Clement VI, intended to draft a permanent treaty.

While the truce succeeded in temporarily halting large-scale operations in Brittany, negotiations in Avignon broke down due to mutual distrust and unwillingness to compromise. Hostilities resumed in June 1345, with the war continuing sporadically across western France.

## Outcome and Significance

### Political and Military Developments

- Despite the truce, the Montfortists maintained control over much of western Brittany.
- The war showcased the crucial role of naval power, local leadership (especially Joanna’s), and tactical innovation (notably, the use of longbowmen and defensive trenches).
- The truce served more as a tactical respite than a genuine diplomatic settlement, and the Breton succession dispute remained unresolved beyond 1343.

### Long-term Impact

- The Breton Civil War continued until 1365; the wider Hundred Years’ War ended only in 1453.
- The events between 1341 and 1343 helped solidify English military superiority in subsequent decades, particularly through the adoption of longbow tactics learned at Morlaix.
- The resilience and resourcefulness of leaders like Joanna of Flanders made her an iconic figure in medieval history, and the episode remains frequently cited in discussions about female leadership and wartime strategy.
- The partitioning of Brittany and eventual French victory at Auray (1364) eventually ended the conflict, but the period 1341–1343 defined the contours of later hostilities.

## Conclusion

The Breton Civil War (1341–1343) was a pivotal chapter in the dynastic struggles that marked 14th-century Europe. Triggered by the death of a duke without heirs, the war quickly expanded to involve regional rulers, monarchs, and international alliances. Major battles such as Morlaix and the defense of Hennebont illustrated the importance of logistics, innovation, and steadfast leadership. Although a truce was negotiated and briefly upheld, the underlying issues remained unresolved, setting the stage for renewed conflict as the Hundred Years’ War unfolded across Western Europe. The legacy of these years includes lasting changes in Breton governance and enduring lessons in military strategy.
