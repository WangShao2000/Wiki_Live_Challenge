# Belmont Glass Works
Belmont Glass Works, also known as Belmont Glass Company, was among Ohio’s early glassmaking enterprises, located in Bellaire, Ohio. The firm began production in 1866 and remained operational until approximately 1890, playing an important role in the growth of the local glass industry in Belmont County. 

# Location and Early History

The company was founded at the corner of 34th Street and Hamilton Street in Bellaire, Ohio, positioning it within a region that would become internationally recognized for its glass manufacturing. Initially established as Barnes, Faupel & Co., the enterprise began operations in 1866 and was officially incorporated under the name Belmont Glass Works in 1868, reflecting its roots in the area named after Belmont County. Over the course of its existence, it was also referred to as Belmont Glass Company.

# Founding Structure and Operations

From its inception, Belmont Glass Works functioned as a cooperative, meaning the laborers were also the owners of the business. At its peak, the factory operated with numerous coal-fired furnaces—dozens in total—which enabled the mass production of glass objects. The early 1870s and 1880s saw the rapid expansion of glass manufacture in the Bellaire area, contributing to the region’s reputation as “the Glass City” during the late nineteenth century.

# Products and Patterns

Belmont Glass Works manufactured a diverse array of items for both household and commercial use. The core products included tableware such as plates, bowls, tumblers, and goblets; lamp bases; bar goods; and stationery glassware. A significant aspect of the company’s output was pressed glass, which was less costly than cut glass and thus accessible to a broad segment of American households. Among its signature designs, the “Daisy and Button” pattern stands out as a widely recognized form of tableware associated with the company. 

The company also produced specialty sets and functional glass items such as water sets, shakers, syrup containers, berry sets, and individual casters. Detailed catalogs and collector resources indicate that numerous patterns were introduced over the decades, numbering at least fifty distinct types by some counts, reflecting both technical innovation and evolving consumer tastes in domestic decoration.

## Notable Personnel

At a crucial moment in the company’s history, William Zimmer was appointed factory manager in 1882. Zimmer’s expertise contributed to the design and manufacture of distinctive items, including patented glass sets that further demonstrated the company’s technical capabilities. His leadership coincided with a period of heightened creativity and output, including new patterns and utilitarian objects.

## Industry Significance

Belmont Glass Works was instrumental in establishing Bellaire as a center for glass production. The city attracted other manufacturers who joined it in forming a concentration of glass houses unmatched in the Upper Ohio Valley—more than any other city outside Wheeling, West Virginia. The legacy of these factories continues through museums, private collections, and the enduring presence of classic glass designs in antique markets and collector circles.

## Closure and Legacy

By the 1890s, the company ceased operations. Factors leading to closure included increasing costs for fuel and labor, economic pressures faced by small-scale glass producers, and widespread market oversupply. Although many business records were lost when the plant burned in 1952, surviving examples of Belmont Glass Works’ products remain valuable artifacts for historians and enthusiasts alike. Pieces are preserved in notable collections such as the National Imperial Glass Museum, and the company’s impact is still recognized in museum exhibits and publications focused on American pressed glass.

## Conclusion

Throughout its roughly 24-year existence, Belmont Glass Works represented both industrial innovation and the entrepreneurial spirit of late nineteenth-century America. As a cornerstone of the local economy and a major contributor to regional identity, its story illustrates the challenges and achievements of American glass manufacturing during a period of rapid technological change and social transformation. Collectors continue to seek out its distinctive patterns and forms, affirming its lasting influence on American decorative arts.
