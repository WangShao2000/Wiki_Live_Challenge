# Kingston Line

The Kingston Line is a commuter rail line operated by the Massachusetts Bay Transportation Authority (MBTA) as part of its extensive regional rail network in southeastern Massachusetts. Originating from historic beginnings dating back to the mid-19th century, the Kingston Line serves as a vital transportation corridor linking Boston’s South Station with communities southeast of the city, including Kingston and former terminus Plymouth, which was closed indefinitely in April 2021. The line covers approximately 35.1 miles and plays a crucial role in connecting residents to employment centers, universities, medical facilities, and other destinations in the Greater Boston area.


# History

### Early Years and Expansion

The roots of the Kingston Line trace back to the Old Colony Railroad, which began construction in 1844 and opened its Boston-to-Plymouth route on November 10, 1845. This line quickly became the backbone of passenger transport along the South Shore, serving towns such as Quincy, Braintree, and Plymouth. Over the ensuing decades, the Old Colony expanded its network through mergers and acquisitions, ultimately encompassing routes to Fall River, New Bedford, Newport, Providence, Cape Cod, and points north.

In 1893, the Old Colony Railroad was leased to the New York, New Haven and Hartford Railroad, becoming the “Old Colony Division.” During this period, the line continued to support both passenger and freight service until the mid-20th century, when declining ridership and economic shifts led to reduced service. Passenger operations on the main Old Colony lines ceased in June 1959, leaving communities without direct rail access for nearly four decades.

### Restoration and Modernization

Recognizing the value of restoring commuter rail service, the MBTA undertook a series of studies in the late 20th and early 21st centuries. Construction began in 1993 to rebuild tracks, stations, and infrastructure, culminating in the re-opening of Old Colony commuter rail service in September 1997. The initial limited service ran only during peak hours, but by November 1997, both the Middleborough/Lakeville and Plymouth/Kingston Lines were fully restored with expanded schedules. The Plymouth/Kingston Line split at its outer end, serving both Kingston and Plymouth, making these the two primary termini.


# Route and Stations

The Kingston Line extends 35.1 miles from Boston’s South Station southeastward through Quincy, Braintree, and various intermediate towns. After splitting from the main trunk at Braintree, the line follows the historic Plymouth Branch to reach Kingston, while also providing access to neighboring communities such as Halifax, Hanson, Whitman, Abington, and South Weymouth.

# Operations

### Service Patterns

The Kingston Line operates as a typical commuter rail, offering frequent service during peak hours with reduced or suspended off-peak and weekend trains at certain locations. Trains usually run between South Station and Kingston, but historical service included stops at Plymouth before its closure. As of 2023, the line averages over 5,900 weekday boardings. Service scheduling aims to serve essential and shift workers, with regular on-the-hour departures on weekdays. Weekend service was restored in July 2021, providing greater connectivity throughout the region.

### Rolling Stock and Technology

All MBTA commuter rail operations, including the Kingston Line, utilize push-pull trainsets powered by diesel locomotives. These consist of one cab car at each end of a string of coaches, enabling efficient turnaround without the need to switch engines. The fleet adheres to strict safety standards, equipped with advanced automatic train control systems.

### Speeds and Track Conditions

Operating speeds vary depending on track configuration. Generally, trains can travel up to 60–80 mph south of Braintree and around 40–60 mph farther east toward Kingston. Throughout most of the line, the maximum permitted speed is 60 mph, though upgrades in the early 2010s enabled segments to operate safely at up to 79 mph. In exceptional circumstances, such as during track repairs or due to aging infrastructure, temporary speed restrictions—sometimes as low as 30 mph—are imposed for safety.


## Ridership

Ridership figures reflect the importance of the Kingston Line to the local community and broader regional mobility. Preliminary counts in 2012 showed nearly 5,500 daily boardings across both directions, with particular concentrations at Kingston and nearby stations. Recent data from April 2023 indicate that weekday boardings have rebounded to about 5,981, signaling strong recovery post-pandemic and the continuing relevance of the line to area residents.

## Notable Events

Significant milestones include a major tie replacement project in the late 2000s, which led to extensive track improvements and some short-term service disruptions. Temporary speed restrictions were imposed during this project to ensure passenger safety as thousands of defective concrete ties were replaced or reinforced.

Another noteworthy development has been the gradual transition to high-platform accessibility and the integration of bicycles aboard trains—a feature advertised in MBTA communications and schedules, allowing commuters flexibility in multimodal journeys.


## Service Changes and Community Impact

In April 2021, MBTA announced the indefinite closure of Plymouth station—one of six stations shuttered that month, cited as a response to pandemic-era budget constraints and shifting ridership patterns. This move generated local controversy, particularly from state representatives and developers who argued that closing Plymouth compromised access to new housing and commercial developments and increased reliance on automobiles. Current planning and advocacy continue to focus on potential reopening or better service restoration to support future growth.

Service adjustments—such as increased midday trains, periodic weekend service expansions, and accessibility enhancements—have helped maintain the Kingston Line’s critical function in metropolitan transportation, especially given its role in mitigating automobile congestion, supporting regional employment centers, and enabling affordable commutes.


## Conclusion

The Kingston Line represents more than just a railroad—it is a living testament to Boston’s evolving transit landscape, capturing centuries of innovation, adaptation, and resilience. From its birth as the Old Colony Railroad in 1845, through near-total decline in the mid-20th century, to robust revival and ongoing modernization in the 21st, the line embodies the ambitions and challenges faced by urban transit systems nationwide.

With reliable daily service, accessibility improvements, and strategic investment, the Kingston Line continues to connect communities, support economic vitality, and offer sustainable alternatives to automobile dependence in southeastern Massachusetts. As plans advance to further enhance regional rail connectivity—including potential future extensions and capacity upgrades—the line remains poised to play a pivotal role in shaping the future of mobility for the Greater Boston region.
