# Trichy assault rifle

The Trichy assault rifle (TAR), sometimes referred to as the Tiruchi assault rifle, is a 7.62×39 mm assault rifle developed and manufactured by the Ordnance Factory Tiruchirappalli (OFT) in Tamil Nadu, India. Introduced in the late 2010s, the Trichy assault rifle represents one of India’s most significant efforts to indigenously design and produce small arms for domestic security forces, especially in the face of ongoing counterinsurgency challenges in regions such as the Red Corridor.


### Design and Development

The origins of the Trichy assault rifle date back to approximately 2011, when an initial version was presented at OFT. The prototype reportedly involved a modified FN FAL receiver chambered for 7.62×39 mm, but this version did not proceed into large-scale production. In subsequent years, OFT undertook a major redesign to align the rifle with international standards and operational needs. By 2017, the rifle was officially unveiled and declared to match the performance and reliability of advanced AK-47 rifles.

Crucially, the current TAR model draws heavily from the Bulgarian Arsenal AR-M1 rifle, a well-regarded AK-pattern firearm featuring a milled receiver and enhanced ergonomics compared to earlier stamped-metal AK designs. OFT incorporated minor modifications, primarily to the buttstock and pistol grip, resulting in three main variants: fixed butt, side foldable butt, and under folding butt. This flexibility allowed the rifle to be tailored for different operational roles, including counterterrorism, patrolling, and airborne operations. The project, termed the ‘7.62 × 39 mm Assault Rifle Project,’ was undertaken by OFT to address urgent requirements from central and state police forces confronting Maoist insurgency in the Red Corridor—a region of intense internal conflict in eastern and central India.


### Main Variants

| Standard TAR       | Fixed/butt, foldable, telescoping butt; mono-block receiver; 7.62×39 mm; up to 350–500 m     | ~3.4 kg       | 350–500 m         | Police, CAPF, anti-Naxal ops              |

| Baby TAR (TAR-3)   | Compact, fully automatic; 8.3" barrel; 2.7 kg unloaded; 150 m effective; 700 rpm cyclic      | ~2.7 kg       | ~150 m            | Special forces, vehicle/aircraft crews    |

Besides the standard variant, OFT developed several specialized models:
- **TriCa**: A miniaturized carbine intended for vehicle crews, paratroopers, helicopter teams, and special operations. At only 3.17 kg loaded, it features a muzzle booster to reduce visible flash and sound signature and shares magazines and parts compatibility with the standard assault rifle and classic AK-47 platforms.
- **Baby TAR (TAR-3)**: Even smaller and lighter, optimized for concealment and close-quarters combat by special forces. Despite its compact size, it fires the same ammunition as the AK-47, uses a curved 30-round magazine, and incorporates advanced heat and noise-reduction technology for operation from short barrels.
- **Police Custom Variant**: A customized version that supports single-shot firing for law enforcement scenarios, equipped with tritium night sights and a Picatinny rail for accessories.


### Service Entry and Adoption

After extensive trials and evaluations—including a series of 15,000 rounds fired during functional tests with no stoppages—the Trichy assault rifle received official approval from India’s Central Reserve Police Force (CRPF) in 2017. Within a year, over 6,000 rifles had been ordered by the CRPF and other central armed police forces such as the Border Security Force (BSF), Indo-Tibetan Border Police (ITBP), and Central Industrial Security Force (CISF). Numerous state police departments, notably in Chhattisgarh, Madhya Pradesh, Jharkhand, Rajasthan, and Kerala, also procured tens of thousands of rifles for their security operations.

In 2024, the BSF announced plans to acquire an additional 125 rifles along with accessories and bayonets. Major urban police agencies, like Kolkata Police, began replacing aging .303-inch firearms with the Trichy assault rifle in the mid-2020s. Officers highlighted key advantages such as significantly reduced weight (up to 2 kg lighter than older models), modular design, quick assembly/disassembly, and effective engagement out to 500 meters.

### Production and Procurement

OFT’s annual production capacity soon reached hundreds of thousands of units, with factory output shifting towards modernization and expansion. Initial public reports indicated yearly deliveries of over 10,000 rifles to police and paramilitary forces. As of the mid-2020s, orders and deployments exceeded the original targets, with companies citing high reliability and ease of logistics due to commonality of parts and ammunition.

Recent state-level procurement programs included custom variants for local needs, some emphasizing reduced firepower or night-operation capabilities. Throughout the process, government bodies repeatedly cited the rifle as a solution that balanced low cost (about ₹55,000 per unit, less than comparable Russian imports), logistical efficiency, and tactical effectiveness.


### Criticisms and Controversies

Despite its growing deployment, the Trichy assault rifle has attracted mixed reactions within India’s defense community and among civil society. Key points of critique include:

- **Design Originality**: Many analysts and technical commentators regard the TAR as derivative of foreign designs, particularly the Bulgarian AR-M1 and classic AK-47 pattern. While acknowledged as an improvement over some locally produced alternatives (notably the problematic INSAS rifle), detractors argue there is insufficient innovation to justify massive investment and production.
- **Quality Control**: Historical experience with domestic weapon systems—such as the earlier Excalibur and MCIWS projects, which failed despite substantial effort—has fostered skepticism regarding long-term reliability and suitability for frontline army service. Some observers caution against dependence on unproven or modification-based designs when importing established patterns (like the AK-203) remains a viable alternative.
- **Workforce and Union Concerns**: Employee unions representing ordnance factory workers emphasized that steady demand for indigenous rifles was essential for retaining jobs at manufacturing facilities facing labor shortages and restructuring. These perspectives have at times framed adoption of the rifle as a matter of economic survival for state-run defense industries, rather than purely tactical necessity.
- **Industrial Policy Implications**: Debates continue regarding the wisdom of prioritizing indigenous production over foreign technology transfers, especially amid ongoing delays in signing agreements for AK-203 joint-manufacturing. Critics cite these debates as examples of broader challenges facing India’s defense modernization goals.

Nevertheless, supporters highlight the rifle’s success as part of national self-reliance initiatives, reducing dependence on imported weapons and strengthening local supply chains.


### Modernization and Future Outlook

Ongoing research and field feedback have spurred continual upgrades to the TRICHY assault rifle system, including modular grip enhancements, improved magazine feeds, and accessory integration. Newer variants such as the Baby TAR and TriCa carbine reflect evolving needs for compactness and specialization in asymmetric warfare environments.

Plans for further exports and customization suggest continued relevance for the platform beyond domestic requirements. Recent announcements indicate phased distribution among major metropolitan police forces, with associated upgrades to supporting equipment such as automatic pistols and non-lethal munitions.


### Conclusion

The Trichy assault rifle stands as one of India’s leading indigenous small arms successes, designed to meet the pressing demands of modern policing and internal security operations. While rooted in internationally proven designs and facing scrutiny over originality and production history, it has nonetheless become widely adopted by central and state security forces across the country. Its modular features, compatibility, and robust reliability make it a pragmatic choice in contemporary operational settings, even as larger questions persist about the future of indigenous small arms development in India.
