# Walther Dobbertin

**Walther Alexander Dobbertin** (28 August 1882 – 12 January 1961) was a German photographer and publisher who played a significant role in documenting German East Africa during the colonial era and World War I. Born in Berlin, Dobbertin built his career in Tanzania, where he became regarded as the most important photographer of the region and left behind a substantial visual archive critical to both historical and contemporary scholarship.


### Early Life

Dobbertin was born on 28 August 1882 in Berlin, Germany. While detailed records of his childhood and formal education remain scarce, he would go on to become one of the leading figures in German colonial photography. His formative years in Germany provided the foundation for his subsequent professional journey abroad.


### Career in German East Africa

In 1903, Dobbertin emigrated to the colony of German East Africa (today’s Tanzania, Rwanda, Burundi, and parts of Kenya and Mozambique). Upon arrival in Dar es Salaam, he established himself as a commercial photographer and entrepreneur. In addition to his photographic pursuits, he operated several book and art shops across major urban centers of the colony, notably in Dar es Salaam, Tanga, and Moshi. Through these outlets, Dobbertin disseminated postcards and books featuring scenes of local life, architecture, and industry—documents that offered both colonists and Germans back home a pictorial window into East Africa.

His photographic output was diverse and ambitious. Albums and portfolios chronicled everything from big game hunting expeditions (featuring lions, elephants, rhinos, and other animals) to everyday scenes of village life, river labor, and market activity. Numerous subjects covered in his images include German and African communities, rural landscapes, natural habitats, and urban settings. His focus on both natural and built environments made him unique among contemporaries.


### Documentation of World War I Activities

When World War I erupted in 1914, German East Africa became a theater of conflict involving forces led by General Paul von Lettow-Vorbeck. Unlike many other European colonies, Dobbertin remained in the territory throughout the war and became the sole documented photographer capturing German activities—including military campaigns, fortifications, and the lived reality of colonial troops. His photographs offer rare visual evidence of the campaign’s context and human dimension.

This extensive body of work provided not just propaganda value but also an enduring record of the war’s impact on people, places, and the environment. Images taken during this period depict both battlefields and moments of civilian resilience, reflecting the complex realities of colonial warfare.


### Publications and Photographic Series

Dobbertin’s written and visual works contributed significantly to the historical memory of German East Africa. Among his major publications:

- **Die Soldaten Lettow-Vorbecks** (1932): A collection of photogravures portraying German soldiers under General von Lettow-Vorbeck. This volume stands out for its frontispiece portrait and over one hundred plates illustrating the campaign and military honor.
- **Schwarz-Weiss-Rot über Ostafrika** (1933): This publication further showcased Dobbertin’s vision through ninety-six plates in photogravure, displaying landscapes, colonial scenes, and wildlife.
- Various postcards and photo albums circulated during his active years in the colony, providing wide public access to his images.

These works were sometimes reprinted or republished in later decades, attesting to their continued interest and importance.


### Political Affiliations

Throughout his lifetime, Dobbertin maintained close ties to the political apparatus of the German state. He was a member of the Nazi paramilitary wing (SA) and held leadership positions within the Reichskolonialbund, serving as a Kreisleiter up until the end of World War II. This membership provides important context for understanding both his career trajectory and the ideological frameworks shaping much of his colonial-era documentation.


### Later Life and Death

Following the dissolution of the German colonial empire and the end of WWII, Dobbertin returned to Germany. He spent his final years in Wiedenhof, a locality in Jesteburg near Hamburg, and passed away on 12 January 1961 at age 78. Despite his return to Europe, his legacy remained anchored in the visual record he created in East Africa.


### Legacy and Archival Holdings

Dobbertin’s visual archive is considered vital to research on German colonialism and the history of East Africa. His photographs and albums are preserved in leading institutional repositories, such as:

- The Melville J. Herskovits Library of African Studies at Northwestern University (notable holdings include the “Deutschostafrikanische Bilder” album, group portraits of railroad workers, images of Lake Tanganyika, and Zanzibar documentation).
- The Yale University Library, which maintains collections including a 1910 big-game hunting album, portraits, village scenes, and the Tanga city album.

Recent scholarship has leveraged Dobbertin’s high-resolution scanned negatives to analyze ecological changes, land management practices, and patterns of colonial power. Researchers have used his photographs to trace transformations in both natural and built environments from the prewar era to modern times.

### Conclusion

Walther Dobbertin stands as a central figure in the documentation of German colonialism in East Africa. His prolific career spanned commercial activity, artistic pursuit, and political engagement, culminating in an archive whose importance extends far beyond his own lifetime. Through his images and publications, Dobbertin captured transformative moments—social, environmental, and political—that remain valuable resources for researchers, educators, and anyone interested in the history of Africa and its connections to Europe.
