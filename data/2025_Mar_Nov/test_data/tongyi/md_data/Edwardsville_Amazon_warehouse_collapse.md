# Edwardsville Amazon Warehouse Collapse

### Introduction

On December 10, 2021, at approximately 8:27 p.m. CST, an EF3 tornado with winds estimated at 150 miles per hour struck the DLI4 delivery facility, an Amazon warehouse located in Edwardsville, Illinois, United States. The tornado caused significant structural damage to the massive distribution center—including the inward collapse of long sections of walls and roof—killing six workers and injuring four others. The victims were all contractors or delivery drivers performing end-of-shift tasks at the time. This event triggered widespread concern over workplace safety and emergency preparedness at large logistics facilities, leading to extensive investigations, legislative action, and renewed calls for improved standards in industrial construction.

### Background

The DLI4 facility served as a central logistics hub for Amazon’s operations throughout the Greater St. Louis region. Covering about 1.1 million square feet, it handled a high volume of packages ahead of the holiday shopping season, employing both full-time staff and numerous temporary contractors.

### Weather Forecasts and Warnings

Forecasts issued during the afternoon and evening of December 10 indicated the potential for severe storms, including tornadoes, across southern Illinois and parts of Missouri. By 5:20 p.m., the National Weather Service had issued a tornado watch for much of the region, including Madison County where Edwardsville is situated. At 8:06 p.m., web-based emergency alerts were sent to Amazon managers and staff at the facility, warning of imminent severe weather.

Despite these warnings, confusion arose among staff regarding proper shelter locations within the sprawling warehouse. Some managers attempted to notify workers verbally, while the designated emergency plan referenced a northern restroom shelter, although instructions were ambiguous and a critical megaphone used for mass communication was found to be locked away and inaccessible.

### The Incident

At around 8:27 p.m., the tornado—touching down just east of Interstate 270—struck the southern side of the DLI4 building. The impact resulted in the collapse of approximately 150 yards of exterior wall onto a bathroom area where several workers had sought shelter, including five delivery contractors and one maintenance mechanic. In total, six people died instantly due to crushing injuries, one suffered critical injuries and was hospitalized, and three others sustained minor injuries.

All fatalities occurred in the south-side restroom, which was not structurally reinforced for tornado-level winds. The majority of the fatalities involved employees working on delivery routes or assigned to contractors supporting Amazon’s logistics chain.

### Emergency Response

Edwardsville Fire Chief James Whiteford reported making or receiving over 130 mobile phone calls within the first hours of the incident. Emergency teams deployed rapidly, coordinating assistance from at least 52 rescue agencies and mobilizing nearly 200 vehicles and over 375 personnel. Local, county, and specialized technical rescue units provided logistical and medical support. Two women trapped inside a restroom area were freed unharmed, while crews worked through night shifts to rescue additional individuals from under debris and concrete rubble.

As storm conditions worsened—with secondary thunderstorms producing 40–50 mph winds—commanders temporarily pulled rescue teams from active operations to address new hazards. A lone survivor, conscious and able to communicate, was extracted by firefighters despite ongoing risk of further collapse.

### Investigations and Legal Actions

#### OSHA Investigation

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration conducted a thorough review of Amazon’s severe weather protocols and emergency plans following the disaster. Investigators found that while Amazon’s procedures met minimal federal guidelines, there were serious deficiencies, notably the lack of clear site-specific plans, absence of accessible audible warning devices, and failure to ensure regular emergency drills. Training records were inconsistent, and the facility’s evacuation maps did not adequately specify safe shelter locations.

OSHA issued a Hazard Alert Letter detailing several key recommendations for Amazon and its delivery contractor partners:
- Provide comprehensive training and implement routine emergency weather drills.
- Develop site-specific emergency action plans, customizing them for actual hazards present at each location.
- Ensure all audible warning devices and their precise locations are clearly identified and immediately available.

Notably, no formal violations were cited, reflecting the narrow scope of OSHA’s regulatory authority, but the agency emphasized the urgent need to prevent future tragedies.

#### Legislative Oversight and Lawsuits

In April 2022, the U.S. House Oversight Committee launched a major investigation into Amazon’s labor practices and emergency response policies. Committee members sought detailed documentation of attendance/leave policies, records of past emergency drills, and any directives that could have prevented workers from seeking shelter. This inquiry intensified after reports emerged that Amazon had threatened contractors and drivers with termination if they left their posts during the tornado warning.

Multiple wrongful-death and emotional-distress lawsuits were subsequently filed by families of the deceased and injured delivery drivers. Plaintiffs argued that Amazon failed to provide adequate warning systems, protect workers from foreseeable risks, and allow timely escape or relocation during the crisis. As of late 2024, one suit was scheduled for mediation and possible trial.

#### Congressional Criticism

U.S. Senator Elizabeth Warren and Representatives Cori Bush and Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez questioned Amazon’s leadership over plans to rebuild the Edwardsville facility to pre-collapse specifications, arguing that new investments should include tornado shelters or significantly more robust construction. They highlighted that the warehouse was located in FEMA Wind Zone IV (highest tornado risk) and criticized Amazon’s refusal to fund basic protective features such as internal safe rooms.

### Aftermath and Building Code Updates

The DLI4 warehouse remained closed for over two years following the collapse. Reconstruction began soon after, with the owner engaging engineers to ensure compliance with enhanced building codes. While the rebuilt structure featured walls rated to withstand 114 mph wind gusts for three seconds—significantly above previous standards—no dedicated tornado shelters were installed within the commercial district or the facility itself.

City officials also updated Edwardsville’s municipal code to require all new buildings to be capable of resisting 114 mph wind loads, a direct response to the lessons learned from the tragedy. Legislative efforts led to the formation of the Illinois Warehouse Safety Standards Task Force, which released a series of recommendations:
- Amending statewide building codes to mandate tornado shelters in warehouses.
- Ensuring employers provide tailored site-specific emergency plans shared with local responders.
- Funding mutual aid agreements (e.g., MABAS) to bolster regional disaster response capabilities.

Amazon announced the hiring of an in-house meteorologist and instituted advanced extreme weather training and emergency procedures for its workforce. The company also donated $1 million to the Edwardsville Community Foundation to support recovery efforts and assist affected families.

### Significance and Broader Impacts

The Edwardsville Amazon warehouse collapse became a focal point in debates over worker safety, industrial design, and corporate accountability. It demonstrated how inadequate infrastructure planning and ambiguous emergency protocols could prove fatal—even at companies touted for technological innovation and operational excellence. The case spurred broader regulatory attention, with lawmakers examining building codes, emergency preparedness, and injury reporting standards for warehousing and logistics sectors.

Survivors and families continued to advocate for reforms, emphasizing that ordinary policies on staffing, leave, and shelter access played a decisive role in the outcome. Their testimonies, legal actions, and media coverage generated lasting pressure for change in both private industry and public policy.

### Conclusion

The Edwardsville Amazon warehouse collapse stands as a stark reminder of the importance of integrating physical safety infrastructure with clear emergency management procedures. Despite the reopening of the facility and updated local building standards, the absence of dedicated tornado shelters leaves ongoing questions about adequacy of protection for thousands of warehouse workers nationwide. The incident has driven unprecedented legislative and regulatory scrutiny of Amazon and its industry peers, highlighting the persistent need to align corporate priorities with worker welfare amidst increasing climate-related disasters.
