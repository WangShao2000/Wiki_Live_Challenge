# HMS Hyperion (1807)

**HMS Hyperion** was a 32-gun fifth-rate frigate of the Royal Navy, launched in 1807. Ordered in June 1805 and laid down in early 1806, Hyperion was constructed by contract at Kingston upon Hull by shipbuilder William Gibson, based on a design by Sir John Henslow. Her commissioning followed her completion in November 1807, after which she saw active service throughout the Napoleonic Wars and into the post-war period before being broken up in June 1833. Below is a comprehensive overview of her design, service history, notable engagements, and ultimate fate.


# Construction and Design

Ordered by the British Admiralty, HMS Hyperion was conceived as part of Britain’s ongoing program of naval expansion during the Napoleonic era. She was designed by renowned naval architect Sir John Henslow, and her build at Kingston upon Hull reflected advanced late-Georgian shipbuilding practices. When launched, Hyperion measured 143 feet 9 inches along the gundeck, with an overall length of 188 feet 8 1/8 inches and a breadth of 39 feet 4 ½ inches. Her depth in hold was 12 feet 4 inches, with draughts ranging from 10 feet forward to 13 feet 6 inches aft. Her burthen was approximately 978 tons by Builder’s Old Measurement.

Armament:  
Hyperion was nominally rated at 32 guns, a complement common to fifth-rates of her class. In 1808, this consisted of 26 long 18-pounder guns mounted on the upper gun deck, supplemented by 10 24-pounder carronades and 2 nine-pounders on the quarterdeck, plus additional carronades and nine-pounders on the forecastle. The crew complement in early 1808 stood at around 254 officers and men.

# Service History and Notable Engagements

### Mediterranean and West Indies (1808–1810)

Commissioned in January 1808 under Captain Thomas Charles Brodie, Hyperion immediately began service in the Mediterranean, a theater critical to British strategy against Napoleon’s France. During this posting, she contributed to blockading French and allied fleets and engaged in patrol and escort operations. Early successes included capturing the vessel Dix Sept Decembre on 3 October 1808 and, in conjunction with HMS Horatio and gun-brig Fervent, recapturing the merchant ship Tom on 17 January 1810.

After two years in the Mediterranean, Hyperion shifted to the Caribbean and West Indian stations, taking up duty on the Jamaica Station from January 1810 onwards. In this hotly contested region—subject to frequent French and American raids, smuggling, and commerce raiding—Hyperion played a vital role in protecting British interests and supporting colonial defenses.

#### Gonaives Incident

One episode in particular highlights Hyperion’s challenges in the Caribbean. While resupplying at Gonaives, Haiti, the frigate became embroiled in diplomatic and military crisis when local batteries unexpectedly opened fire on British boats near her waters, resulting in three fatalities among crewmen. Captain Brodie, along with several officers, was taken prisoner before being released following direct intervention by First Lieutenant James Morgan, who maneuvered Hyperion close enough to threaten destruction of the town if release did not occur swiftly. The incident underscored both the unpredictability of colonial diplomacy and the importance of naval deterrence.

### North Atlantic and War of 1812 (1811–1815)

By late 1811, Captain William Pryce Cumby assumed command of Hyperion. Under Cumby, the frigate was assigned to more distant and challenging theaters. She sailed first to Vera Cruz and Havana before returning to England for refitting. After refit, she began active participation in the War of 1812, sailing in the waters off Newfoundland and the Gulf of St. Lawrence. Her roles were primarily protective and logistical: safeguarding the whaling fleet in the Davis Strait, escorting merchant convoys between Newfoundland and Barbados, and providing general security for Allied shipping lanes in the North Atlantic.

In June 1812, Hyperion made a notable contribution to British economic warfare by escorting merchant vessels safely to port, helping maintain supply lines and trade routes despite ongoing hostilities and threats from American privateers. Cumby’s leadership was credited with maintaining discipline and readiness among crew members during these difficult months, even in harsh winter conditions.

### Anti-privateering and Final Years (1814–1833)

A key highlight of Cumby’s tenure was the capture of the American privateer brig Rattlesnake on 14 June 1814. The Rattlesnake, noted for her speed and effectiveness, had already captured 28 British merchant ships—a threat to Allied commerce. Hyperion’s pursuit and seizure of the vessel demonstrated the Royal Navy’s continued maritime dominance and its ability to disrupt enemy privateering operations.

Following the end of the War of 1812, Hyperion spent periods in refit and reactivation. In January 1815, Captain James Lillicrap took temporary command, escorting large merchant convoys between Europe and South America. He later became Commander-in-Chief of the Cape of Good Hope Station (1821–1822), a post reflecting his proven capabilities in both war and peacetime.

### South America and the Pacific (1818–1821)

From 1818 to 1821, Captain Thomas Searle commanded Hyperion. During his tenure, the frigate served in British waters (attending George IV on ceremonial cruises) and undertook a lengthy voyage to South America, specifically Chile and Peru. Here, Hyperion was present at the capture of the Spanish frigate Esmeralda by Chilean forces at Callao in November 1820. This marked a significant moment in the struggle for independence across Latin America, with British naval presence lending prestige and practical support to emerging new nations.

Searle’s journey resulted in Hyperion returning to Britain with nearly half a million sterling in specie—a valuable asset at the time—reflecting both the strategic significance and commercial value of such expeditions.

### Later Service and Decommissioning (1825–1833)

By 1825, Captain William James Mingay took command. Assigned to the Newhaven coast blockade, Hyperion fulfilled a role in suppressing illegal activity and enforcing maritime law during a period of relative peace. Her extended service in this capacity further demonstrates the versatility of fifth-rate frigates, adaptable for both combat and domestic security.

Hyperion’s career concluded in June 1833, when she was decommissioned and broken up at Portsmouth. Throughout her existence, she exemplified the adaptability, durability, and broad deployment typical of Royal Navy frigates.

## Legacy

HMS Hyperion’s service encapsulated the multifaceted role played by Royal Navy frigates throughout the age of sail. From anti-privateering and anti-smuggling duties to high-profile blockades and diplomatic escorts, her operational history reflects the changing demands of global naval warfare and Britain’s international responsibilities during a turbulent period.

Her actions, especially in the War of 1812 and the Pacific independence movements, illustrate both the reach and influence of British naval power. Hyperion’s retirement and scrapping in the 1830s marked the end of a distinguished career spanning twenty-six years, underscoring the shift from wooden warships to ironclads and steam propulsion that would define subsequent decades of naval innovation.
