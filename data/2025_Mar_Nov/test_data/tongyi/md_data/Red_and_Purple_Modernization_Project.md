# Red and Purple Modernization Project

The Red and Purple Modernization (RPM) Project is a multi-phased reconstruction effort coordinated by the Chicago Transit Authority to upgrade the century-old elevated Red and Purple rail lines on the city’s North Side. Beginning construction on October 2, 2019, and largely completed by July 20, 2025, the Phase One segment of the $2.1 billion program rebuilt four stations, added a grade-separated Red-Purple Bypass at Belmont, replaced 1.3 miles of track and signal systems, and created open public space beneath the line between Lawrence and Ardmore avenues.


### Background

The corridor served by the Red and Purple Lines on Chicago’s North Side was constructed in the early 1900s, with much infrastructure now approaching a century old. As one of the busiest rail corridors in the CTA system, daily ridership has grown significantly over the years, reaching more than 175,000 trips. By the late 2000s, existing track structures, embankments, and stations had become undersized and required frequent maintenance, often resulting in slow zones and operational inefficiencies. In response, the CTA initiated planning for modernization in 2011, seeking to replace aging assets and expand capacity to meet projected demand growth.


### Scope and Components

Phase One of the RPM focused on improving a 5.6-mile segment of the corridor running from Belmont to Howard Stations. Key features included the reconstruction of four stations—Lawrence, Argyle, Berwyn, and Bryn Mawr—each upgraded to offer elevators, escalators, wider platforms, and improved accessibility. Track structures totaling 1.3 route miles were replaced, and a grade-separated flyover (also known as the Red/Purple Bypass) was completed to eliminate conflicts where northbound Brown Line trains must cross paths with Red and Purple trains.

The project also introduced new signaling technology across 23 route miles, enhancing reliability and allowing for higher train frequencies. Additionally, 32 new railcars were procured to support expanded service. The installation of these upgrades not only improves passenger comfort and safety but also enables the CTA to increase hourly peak capacity by approximately 30 percent compared to previous levels.


### Funding

The overall Phase One cost was approximately $2.1 billion, combining both federal and local sources. Federal funds comprised roughly 48 percent of the Core Capacity component, provided through the Department of Transportation’s Section 5309 Core Capacity grant program. Additional funding came from CTA sales tax bonds, cash, Transportation Infrastructure Finance and Innovation Act (TIFIA) loans, and revenue generated from a designated Tax Increment Financing (TIF) district. This approach distributed financial responsibilities between federal, state, and local agencies, reflecting collaborative efforts to advance public transit investment.

### Construction Timeline

Construction for Phase One officially began on October 2, 2019. The initial segment saw the launch of the Red-Purple Bypass—the $320 million Belmont Flyover—at the end of November 2021, which removed level-crossing delays for trains serving the northern corridor. Following this, work continued on the reconstruction of Lawrence, Argyle, Berwyn, and Bryn Mawr stations, culminating in their grand reopening to the public on July 20, 2025.

During construction, the CTA faced ongoing challenges related to community engagement, ensuring that neighborhoods affected by work disruptions understood the long-term benefits of the upgrades. Temporary station arrangements and staged closures were implemented to minimize service interruptions while maintaining connectivity.


### Community Impact and Controversies

While the RPM has been broadly supported by transit advocates and policymakers for its capacity and accessibility improvements, it has also faced significant local opposition—particularly regarding the Belmont Flyover. Residents expressed concerns about demolition of 16 buildings, project cost increases (originally estimated at $320 million, later rising to $570 million), and questions about the scale of congestion relief provided by the bypass. Some argued that observed delays at Clark Junction were less severe than projected, casting doubt on the necessity of such a large-scale intervention.

Another major controversy emerged in October 2025, when the federal administration paused disbursement of $2.1 billion in previously approved funding for the RPM, citing new rules governing disadvantaged business enterprise programs. Lawmakers raised concerns about the potential negative effects on jobs and economic development, as well as uncertainty for ongoing projects.

Throughout construction, CTA staff worked to address complaints about noise, neighborhood disruption, and business impacts, emphasizing that the ultimate goal was to create safer, more reliable, and accessible transit service for generations to come.


### Open Space Development

An innovative aspect of the RPM is the creation of ten blocks of open public space beneath the new track structure, stretching between Lawrence and Ardmore Avenues. Designed with input from local stakeholders, these areas are intended to serve as pedestrian trails, dog parks, playgrounds, fitness zones, and event plazas. Construction for this “Under L” public activation initiative is expected to begin in 2026 and be completed by 2027.


### Future Phases

As Phase One concludes, the CTA has launched the RPM Next Phases Planning Study to assess improvements for the remainder of the 9.6-mile corridor, including portions of the Red Line north of Howard and the entire Purple Line Evanston Branch. This study is expected to conclude in 2025 and will set goals, identify alternative options, and evaluate project phasing and implementation strategies. Subsequent stages will involve engineering work, environmental reviews, and securing further funding to complete the modernization effort.

### Conclusion

The Red and Purple Modernization Project represents the largest capital improvement initiative in the history of the Chicago Transit Authority. By updating century-old infrastructure, increasing accessibility, and preparing the network for future growth, the program will fundamentally transform transit service for the city’s North Side communities. Despite ongoing debates around costs, community impacts, and funding uncertainties, the project marks a pivotal moment in advancing equitable, efficient, and sustainable public transportation in Chicago.
