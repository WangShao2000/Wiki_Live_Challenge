# Skiddaw Group SSSI

The Skiddaw Group Site of Special Scientific Interest (SSSI) is a prominent protected landscape located in the Lake District High Fells, Cumbria, England. Covering approximately 10,384 hectares, this extensive area encompasses some of the most dramatic mountainous terrain in England and serves as a key conservation site for both its outstanding geological heritage and its unique upland habitats. First notified as an SSSI in 1990, the area includes iconic peaks such as Skiddaw and Great Calva and supports diverse ecosystems underpinning both national and international conservation designations.


### Location and Extent

Situated near the center of the northern Lake District, the Skiddaw Group SSSI forms a broad, roughly circular zone centered around Great Calva and extending across the surrounding fellside areas. Its boundaries include numerous high-altitude summits, deep valleys, and glacial tarns. The SSSI is further subdivided into several numbered “units” based on habitat, geomorphology, and land management, facilitating detailed monitoring and reporting under Natural England’s conservation assessment framework. Collectively, these units span elevations from just over 126 meters to nearly 930 meters above sea level, with average elevation of approximately 490 meters.


### Designation and Conservation Status

The Skiddaw Group SSSI was officially designated under the Wildlife and Countryside Act in 1990, recognizing its importance for biodiversity, geological interest, and scientific value. The site holds multiple designations, including its status as part of the Lake District High Fells Special Area of Conservation (SAC). Areas within the SSSI are managed and monitored through a system of units, which allows authorities to assess changes in habitat condition and overall site health over time.

Natural England conducts periodic assessments of SSSI units, rating their ecological condition according to standardized criteria. As of recent reviews, several units remain in “unfavourable recovering” or “unfavourable no change” states, primarily due to historic grazing pressure, habitat fragmentation, and the effects of climate change. However, concerted restoration efforts and adaptive management strategies aim to shift these areas towards favourable conditions in coming years.

### Geology and Earth Heritage

Geologically, the Skiddaw Group represents some of the oldest rock sequences in the Lake District, dating back to the early Ordovician period (Tremadoc to Llanvirn stages). These formations comprise thick sequences of laminated mudstones, siltstones, greywackes, and other sedimentary rocks laid down under ancient marine conditions. The group can be up to 5 kilometers thick in places and crops out across the northern and central fells, forming the smooth-sided mountains that dominate the skyline.

Its stratigraphy is notable for its completeness and exposure, making it a focus of research and field study for earth scientists and students. Within the SSSI, there are several GCR-designated sites and important exposures along major drainage lines, such as Whelpo Beck and Caldew Water bodies. The area also contains evidence of past volcanic activity at the margins, where the Skiddaw Group is unconformably overlain by Borrowdale Volcanic Group lavas and tuffs.


### Mineralogy and Mining Heritage

In addition to its geological interest, the Skiddaw Group SSSI has a rich history of mineral extraction and industrial archaeology. Numerous former mine sites are preserved within the area, with spoil heaps and abandoned workings now recognized for their scientific and cultural significance. These sites host rare minerals, such as crystallized lead sulphates, carbonates, phosphates, and unique arsenic compounds, some of which occur nowhere else in Britain. Copper, zinc, tungsten, and graphite deposits were historically exploited, particularly in locations like Red Gill Mine and Carrock Mine.

Many of these former mines are listed as Scheduled Monuments, underscoring their role in understanding the region’s economic past and its contribution to advances in mineralogy and earth science. While active prospecting is prohibited by law, controlled sampling by qualified individuals is sometimes permitted for research purposes.


### Habitat Types and Biodiversity

The Skiddaw Group SSSI hosts a diversity of upland habitats shaped by altitude, geology, hydrology, and disturbance regimes. Among the most widespread are dwarf shrub heathlands—comprising species like Calluna vulgaris (heather) and Erica tetralix (cross-leaved heath)—which form extensive tracts on acid soils and provide critical breeding grounds for birds such as snipe, golden plover, and curlew.

Blanket bogs and peatlands are prominent features, supporting specialized flora like Sphagnum mosses, cotton grasses, and the occasional sundew (Drosera spp.). These wet habitats are essential for carbon storage and serve as refuges for rare insects and waders. Restoring these bog systems remains a priority for conservation organizations aiming to reverse decades of drainage and peat degradation.

At lower altitudes and along rocky slopes, patches of upland broadleaved woodland thrive, featuring native trees such as oak, birch, and ash. Efforts are underway to reintroduce and expand temperate rainforest communities—home to rare lichens, liverworts, and bryophytes—that are among the most biologically rich habitats in the British Isles.


### Conservation and Restoration Efforts

Recent years have seen a surge in landscape-scale restoration projects within the SSSI, notably through the acquisition and management of Skiddaw Forest. Cumbria Wildlife Trust purchased 3,000 acres of forested land in autumn 2024, expanding its holdings to more than 12,000 acres and launching ambitious schemes to reinstate temperate rainforest, restore blanket bogs, and plant tens of thousands of native trees. Such initiatives target the rewilding of previously intensively grazed lands and foster conditions favorable to endemic fauna and flora.

Baseline surveys assessing vegetation, bird populations, and invertebrate communities are informing adaptive management protocols that balance ecological goals with traditional land uses such as controlled grazing and recreation. Collaborative approaches involving local stakeholders, scientists, volunteers, and government agencies ensure that restoration actions are scientifically robust and socially sustainable.


### Public Access and Recreation

Public access to the Skiddaw Group SSSI is generous, with many footpaths traversing its mountainsides and valleys. Popular hiking destinations include Skiddaw, Great Calva, and Blencathra, each offering panoramic views and challenging routes. While certain sensitive areas may experience seasonal restrictions—particularly where grazing exclusion, rewilding, or protection of breeding birds is required—most parts of the SSSI remain open for exploration and enjoyment.

Guided walks, educational visits, and citizen science programs are encouraged to raise awareness of the site's natural and cultural heritage. Management authorities emphasize the importance of responsible recreation practices to minimize impacts on habitats and archaeological features.


### Ongoing Issues and Challenges

Despite successful conservation measures, the Skiddaw Group SSSI faces several challenges. Historic sheep grazing and upland burning have contributed to reduced cover of dwarf shrub heaths and degraded peat soils. Climate change exacerbates stressors such as drought, flooding, and invasive species encroachment, complicating long-term habitat management.

Balancing scientific research, public recreation, and large-scale restoration with existing land use practices remains an ongoing concern. Stakeholder dialogue is essential to ensure that future decisions reflect both ecological integrity and community interests.

**Conclusion**

The Skiddaw Group SSSI stands as an emblem of England’s diverse natural heritage, combining awe-inspiring scenery with exceptional geological, biological, and historical significance. Through integrated management strategies, collaborative partnerships, and scientific stewardship, this landscape continues to be safeguarded for future generations—an enduring symbol of both past human endeavors and the promise of ecological renewal.
