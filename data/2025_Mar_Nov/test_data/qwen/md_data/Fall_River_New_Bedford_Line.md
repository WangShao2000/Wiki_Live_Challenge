# Fall River/New Bedford Line

The Fall River/New Bedford Line is a commuter rail line operated by the Massachusetts Bay Transportation Authority (MBTA) as part of its Commuter Rail division [1], [8]. It connects Boston's South Station to the cities of New Bedford and Fall River in southeastern Massachusetts, marking the restoration of passenger rail service to the South Coast region after a hiatus of more than six decades [1], [5], [8]. The line officially launched its service on March 24, 2025, following extensive construction and testing phases [1], [5]. This event represents a significant reconnection of a historically important transportation corridor, reviving a legacy of 19th-century rail networks that once served the area before their decline in the mid-20th century [7], [12]. The modern incarnation of the line was created by extending the previously existing Middleborough/Lakeville Line southward through Taunton and branching west to Freetown, Fall River, and New Bedford [4], [9]. This project involved the construction of six entirely new, fully accessible stations, the rebuilding of approximately 36 miles of freight track for passenger use, and the implementation of modern safety systems across the entire corridor [1], [7]. As of late 2025, the line operates with diesel-hauled push-pull trains and serves as a critical regional connector, demonstrating notable ridership growth despite early operational challenges [11], [38].

## History and Predecessors

The history of passenger rail service on the Fall River/New Bedford Line is deeply rooted in the 19th-century expansion of the American railroad network, predating the modern MBTA system by more than a century. The story begins not with the MBTA, but with a confluence of several independent railroads that sought to connect the burgeoning industrial centers of southeastern Massachusetts to Boston and beyond. The earliest of these was the Fall River Railroad, which opened its line in phases between June 9, 1845, and December 21, 1846, establishing a vital link between Fall River and South Braintree via Middleborough [7], [11]. Just a year later, in 1846, the Old Colony Railroad began operating a line between Mansfield and New Bedford, further cementing the region's connectivity [7], [15]. These lines, along with others such as the Dighton and Somerset Railroad (opened September 24, 1866), formed a dense web of rail traffic designed to facilitate the movement of goods and people, particularly supporting industries like textiles in Fall River and fishing in New Bedford [7]. The Old Colony Railroad itself was a powerful consolidator, acquiring numerous smaller lines throughout the region by 1888 and eventually expanding its reach into parts of Rhode Island, including Providence and Newport [7], [12]. In 1893, the New York, New Haven and Hartford Railroad (the "New Haven") acquired the Old Colony Railroad, bringing all of these historic lines under a single corporate umbrella [7]. For much of the early 20th century, the region enjoyed robust commuter service, which peaked around this time before beginning a long period of decline after World War I [11].

This era of prosperity came to an abrupt end on September 5, 1958, when the New York, New Haven and Hartford Railroad discontinued its unprofitable Old Colony Division passenger service [7], [12]. While the main line between Boston and Providence remained active, the intricate web of tracks connecting Boston to Fall River and New Bedford was abandoned, severing a critical transportation link for the South Coast communities of Taunton, Freetown, New Bedford, and Fall River [8], [12]. By June 30, 1959, even the last remnants of commuter service on the Fall River line had ceased, leaving the region without direct rail access to Boston for the next 65 years [8], [11]. This long-standing absence became a central point of political and community discourse for decades, driving repeated calls for the restoration of passenger rail. The specific dates of the cessation—September 5, 1958, for most of the network, and June 30, 1959, for the final Fall River branch—serve as crucial markers of the immense time gap the modern South Coast Rail project would ultimately bridge [7], [11]. The historical narrative of the region's rail network is therefore one of vibrant activity followed by a prolonged period of disinvestment and isolation, making the recent revival of service not merely an infrastructural update but a profound act of historical reconnection. The successful launch of the Fall River/New Bedford Line on March 24, 2025, was explicitly framed by officials and media alike as the restoration of passenger rail to the South Coast for the first time in 65 to 67 years, a milestone that resonated deeply with local residents and policymakers who had advocated for such a service for generations [1], [5], [8]. The official renaming of the extended Middleborough/Lakeville Line to the Fall River/New Bedford Line effective March 24, 2025, formally acknowledged this historical continuity and celebrated the return of a piece of regional heritage [4], [8], [65].

## Project Development and Controversies

The journey to restore passenger rail service to the South Coast was a complex, multi-decade process fraught with shifting plans, escalating costs, and significant legal and political hurdles. The desire for renewed rail connectivity dates back to at least the 1990s, with state legislation authorizing feasibility studies as far back as 1994 [43]. Early legislative action included a 1997 law directing the MBTA to begin design and permitting work, followed by a 2000 law instructing the agency to pursue a route via Stoughton, backed by a $225 million bonding capacity [43]. However, progress was consistently hampered by funding shortfalls and political opposition, including documented resistance from former Governor Mitt Romney [43]. A pivotal moment in the project's evolution occurred in 2013, when the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers released the Final Environmental Impact Statement (FEIS) for the South Coast Rail proposal [36], [45]. This document evaluated several alternatives, including routes through Attleboro and Stoughton, as well as a bus-based option [36]. MassDOT's preferred alternative at the time was the Stoughton route, which would have utilized a combination of active freight lines, out-of-service rail beds dating back to 1916 and 1958, and existing commuter rail tracks to reach Boston [35], [45]. This ambitious plan was projected to cost $1.8 billion and be completed by 2021 [46]. However, this alternative proved to be both financially and logistically prohibitive. By 2025, the projected cost for the Stoughton route had ballooned to $3.4 billion, with a completion date pushed to 2030, roughly seven to nine years behind the original projection [46]. Consequently, MassDOT announced a revived Middleborough alternative, a more affordable and faster-to-complete Phase I project centered on upgrading the existing Middleborough Secondary freight line [46], [47]. This decision marked a strategic pivot, allowing for the delivery of service to the South Coast eight years sooner than the full-build Stoughton alternative, albeit with the permanent limitation of diesel-only operation due to the lack of overhead catenary [47], [70].

The financial trajectory of the project reflects this protracted development cycle. Initial projections placed the cost at $600 million in 2002, with an expected opening in 2007 [43]. By 2005, this estimate had already risen to over $1 billion, and by 2014, the projected budget exceeded $2 billion [43]. The final Phase I project, which became the reality, had a construction cost of $1.047 billion [7]. The launch date also experienced numerous delays, moving from the initial 2007 target to 2023, then to summer 2025, and finally settling on March 24, 2025 [31], [37], [39]. The MBTA cited rigorous federal safety certification and testing requirements as the primary reason for the final delay [40], [56]. Beyond financial and scheduling challenges, the project's development was shadowed by contentious legal battles, primarily revolving around the use of eminent domain to acquire land for new stations and layover facilities. In New Bedford, the city challenged the MBTA's authority to seize land, arguing that because New Bedford was not yet a member municipality at the time of the takings (June 2019), the MBTA lacked the statutory power to do so [60], [61]. The city also contended that the compensation offered—a total of $486,627 for five separate parcels—was "grossly inadequate" compared to the net cost of remediation and other damages claimed by the city [59], [60]. Similarly, in Fall River, landowners sued the MBTA over compensation for properties taken for the Weaver's Cove Layover station [58]. These legal disputes underscored the high stakes of large-scale infrastructure projects and the potential for conflict with local communities. Ultimately, the New Bedford dispute settled in July 2024 for $3.5 million, a sum substantially higher than the initial payment, highlighting the complexities and costs associated with land acquisition [58], [59].

## Infrastructure and Operations

The physical infrastructure of the Fall River/New Bedford Line is a testament to the adaptive reuse of legacy assets combined with substantial new construction. The project's core achievement was the transformation of approximately 36.1 miles (58.1 km) of freight railroad track into a modern passenger rail corridor [7], [13]. This massive undertaking involved the complete rebuilding of the track, including the replacement of rails, ties, and ballast to meet the standards required for higher-speed passenger service [7]. The infrastructure upgrade was extensive, encompassing nine new grade crossings, four interlocking systems, and the reconstruction of 34 bridges in addition to the three bridges that were being redesigned for the project [5], [63]. A critical component of this effort was the reconstruction of three aging rail bridges in New Bedford—the Deane, Sawyer, and Coggeshall street bridges—which had been limiting train speeds to a mere 5 mph, rendering them unsuitable for passenger rail [33]. The 'Fast Track New Bedford' project, which received a $20 million TIGER grant, completely rebuilt these structures, enabling trains to travel at higher speeds and improving reliability [33]. The entire line, from South Station in Boston to the termini in New Bedford and Fall River, spans 56.6 miles to Fall River and 60.0 miles to New Bedford [1].

A defining characteristic of the line's infrastructure is its reliance on a network of new and renovated stations, all of which are designed to meet modern accessibility standards. Six entirely new, fully accessible stations were constructed as part of Phase 1: Middleborough, East Taunton, Freetown, Fall River Depot, Church Street (New Bedford), and New Bedford [4], [6], [7]. Each of these stations features full-length high-level platforms, allowing for "all-doors boarding" which expedites passenger flow and improves efficiency [7], [9]. Amenities at the stations include benches, canopies, and secure bicycle storage, catering to the needs of a diverse range of commuters [9]. East Taunton Station serves as a crucial transfer hub, situated at the junction where the line splits into the Fall River and New Bedford branches [4], [64]. This configuration allows for cross-platform transfers, enabling coordinated timed connections between the two branches and maximizing access to trains heading to Boston throughout the day [13], [64]. In addition to the passenger stations, two new layover facilities were built—one in Weaver's Cove, Fall River, and another in Wamsutta near New Bedford—to house and maintain the fleet of diesel coaches [7], [10]. All six new stations, along with the surrounding infrastructure, are designated as MBTA Zone 8, reflecting their distance from Boston's urban core [4], [10].

The operational model of the Fall River/New Bedford Line is defined by its integration into the existing MBTA Commuter Rail system and its unique arrangement for shared track usage. The entire MBTA Commuter Rail system, including the Fall River/New Bedford Line, utilizes a Positive Train Control (PTC) system with Advanced Civil Speed Enforcement System (ACSES), a federally mandated safety technology designed to prevent collisions and derailments [1], [22]. Testing for PTC on the South Coast Rail corridor began in 2024, and the system was declared fully in service on all MBTA Commuter Rail lines by that year [22]. The line is currently operated by Keolis under contract with the MBTA [13]. A key feature of its operation is its use of diesel-powered push-pull trains [7], [40]. Unlike the electrified portions of the MBTA system, the South Coast corridor lacks overhead catenary, necessitating the use of diesel locomotives to haul the passenger coaches [47]. This operational choice, while practical for the Phase I project, imposes certain limitations, such as lower maximum speeds compared to electric trains and the need for maintenance facilities equipped for diesel engines. The line runs on tracks owned by Massachusetts Coastal Railroad (MCRR), a subsidiary of CSX Transportation, creating a shared-use environment with freight operations [54], [67]. This arrangement requires careful coordination between passenger and freight dispatchers and involves a formal liability agreement where CSX contributes to the MBTA's insurance policy if it is found to be willfully at fault in an accident involving a freight train on the shared corridor [23]. State control over dispatch operations was formally transferred from CSX to the MBTA in 2012, eliminating a previous bottleneck in operational planning and enabling more efficient scheduling [26].

## Service and Schedule

The service on the Fall River/New Bedford Line officially commenced on Monday, March 24, 2025, pending final approval from the Federal Railroad Administration (FRA) [5], [6], [19]. This launch marked the culmination of decades of planning and construction, restoring direct commuter rail service to the South Coast region [1]. The service operates with a distinct pattern designed to function as a regional connector rather than a traditional suburban feeder line. The schedule reveals a limited-stop service model, where not every train stops at every station on every trip [21]. Specifically, the northernmost stations—including South Station, Quincy Center, JFK/UMass, and Braintree—are served only by select trips, typically during peak hours in the afternoon and evening [3], [29]. Most weekday and weekend trips originate directly from Middleborough or East Taunton, focusing on providing connectivity between Boston and the South Coast termini [13], [21]. Bicycles are permitted on all trips along the line, accommodating the needs of cyclists [2], [3].

The service frequency is structured to provide regular, though not frequent, connectivity. During the weekday, the line offers approximately 15 direct trips to Fall River and 17 direct trips to New Bedford, resulting in a headway of roughly every 70 minutes [4], [9], [65]. On weekends, service is less frequent, with 26 round-trip trains operating between South Station and East Taunton, leading to a headway of approximately every two hours [4], [9]. Late-night service extends into the next calendar day, with the final train departing Boston just before midnight [3], [4]. The end-to-end travel time from Boston's South Station to either New Bedford or Fall River is approximately 90 minutes [10], [31], [40]. Regarding fare structure, the six new stations are assigned to MBTA Zone 8 [4], [10]. The full one-way fare from Zone 8 to Zone 1A (South Station) is $12.25, with a reduced fare of $6.00 for eligible seniors and individuals with disabilities [4], [10], [13]. To encourage ridership upon its launch, the MBTA offered free weekend fare service for passengers boarding or alighting at the six new stations between April 5 and May 31, 2025, plus on Patriots’ Day and Memorial Day [4]. Free parking was also provided at all six new station lots from March 24 until April 30, 2025 [4], [19]. After this promotional period, standard parking rates were implemented: $4 per day on weekdays and $2 per day on weekends and holidays [4], [19]. Some stations, like the Whale’s Tooth lot in New Bedford, also offer a multi-day rate of $15 for vehicles parked outside of MBTA service hours [51], [52]. Despite its successful launch, the line faced initial operational challenges, including crew shortages and poor on-time performance [11]. As of a public meeting in December 2025, the line's on-time performance was reported at 77%, significantly below the MBTA systemwide average of 90% [73]. However, officials confirmed that the line had achieved six consecutive weekends of full service without cancellations following the initial startup phase [73].

## Ridership and Impact

Since its launch on March 24, 2025, the Fall River/New Bedford Line has demonstrated strong demand, indicating a significant positive impact on regional mobility. Official MBTA ridership data, which measures individual boardings rather than unique riders, showed that the line had 5,261 daily boardings as of October 2022, prior to the extension's completion [1], [11]. More recent data from the period of April through September 2025 reveals a substantial increase in usage. During this time, the line saw an average of 2,000 additional daily trips, representing a 33% overall ridership increase compared to the same period in 2024 [38]. This surge was even more pronounced on weekdays, which saw a 36% rise in ridership, while weekend ridership grew by 13% [38]. This growth occurred even as overall MBTA commuter rail network ridership declined by 6% during the same period, underscoring the line's unique value proposition and its role as a critical transportation artery for the South Coast [38]. The estimated daily ridership for the new service was projected to be around 1,610 passengers, suggesting that actual figures may have surpassed initial expectations [10].

The economic and social impact of the restored rail service extends beyond direct ridership numbers. The project was envisioned as a catalyst for economic development and a solution to chronic traffic congestion in the region [27]. A state environmental analysis from 2017 projected that the line would reduce vehicle miles traveled (VMT) by 66,400 daily, equivalent to eliminating approximately 600 round-trip commutes between New Bedford and Boston each day [38]. However, preliminary traffic counts on Route 24 and Route 140 in the corridor from April to October 2025 showed only slight increases of 2% and 1%, respectively, leading MassDOT to caution that the findings were inconclusive and that the full impact on regional traffic patterns would take longer to assess [38]. On the development front, the arrival of the rail stations has prompted municipalities to implement zoning changes to encourage mixed-use, transit-oriented development. For instance, the City of Middleboro established a Smart Growth Zoning District near its new station, which permits higher-density housing and has already resulted in the construction of 881 housing units, including 250 affordable ones [62]. Similarly, New Bedford is developing special overlay zoning districts around its two new stations to support commercial and residential growth [44]. The line has also become an integral part of the regional transit ecosystem, proving its utility during periods of disruption to other services. During the Red Line Braintree Branch suspension in November 2025, the Fall River/New Bedford Line was designated as a key alternative route, with extra coaches added to accommodate increased ridership and provide fare-free service for the segment between Braintree and South Station [18], [20].

Despite its successes, the line's operation has not been without community concerns. Residents in Fall River have reported severe noise disturbances from idling trains at the Weaver's Cove Layover facility, citing impacts on their health and quality of life [73]. The MBTA has acknowledged these complaints and proposed mitigation measures, such as constructing an earthen berm and a sound wall, though it noted potential visual impacts from such structures [73]. Another significant aspect of the project's legacy is the legal and financial friction it generated with local governments over land acquisition. In New Bedford, the city filed a lawsuit challenging the MBTA's use of eminent domain, arguing that the agency lacked the legal authority to seize city-owned property before formally joining the MBTA system [60], [61]. The city also contested the compensation offered for the land, claiming it was grossly inadequate [59], [60]. The dispute was eventually settled in July 2024, with the MBTA paying the city a total of $3.5 million for the land, a sum significantly higher than the initial payments of $486,627 for five separate parcels [58], [59]. Similar litigation occurred in Fall River over takings for the Weaver's Cove station, illustrating the contentious nature of large-scale infrastructure projects and their profound effects on local communities and municipal finances [58].

## Future Plans

The completion of South Coast Rail Phase I, which brought passenger rail service to the South Coast on March 24, 2025, marks a significant achievement but is not the final chapter for the project. The MBTA has long maintained plans for a subsequent Phase II, which aims to create a more robust and integrated rail connection by extending the line northward from East Taunton to Stoughton [48], [72]. This extension would fundamentally alter the operational characteristics of the service. Currently, the Fall River/New Bedford Line is a diesel-powered system that relies on the existing Middleborough/Lakeville Line for its connection to Boston [40], [47]. Phase II would involve building a new, double-tracked railway between East Taunton and Stoughton, a section that would be capable of supporting electrified service [48], [72]. Electrification would eliminate the need for diesel locomotives, allowing for faster acceleration, quieter operation, and lower emissions, aligning the South Coast Line with the rest of the MBTA Commuter Rail network.

The rationale for Phase II is multifaceted. Operationally, it would address the inherent limitations of the current single-track configuration, which causes delays and restricts service frequency, particularly near Boston [40]. Double-tracking would improve reliability and allow for more frequent departures from both New Bedford and Fall River, enhancing the line's capacity and appeal to commuters. From a financial perspective, electrifying the line would reduce long-term operating and maintenance costs compared to the diesel fleet currently in use [47]. Furthermore, connecting to the existing commuter rail line at Stoughton would integrate the South Coast Line more seamlessly into the broader regional rail network, potentially increasing its ridership and utility. The estimated cost for the full build-out, including Phase II, is projected to be $2.3 billion, although the exact cost for Phase II alone has not been specified in the provided sources [46], [63]. The development of these future plans continues to navigate the complex landscape of funding, environmental permitting, and political will that characterized the earlier phases of the project.
