# Edwardsville Amazon Warehouse Collapse

On the evening of Friday, December 10, 2021, an EF-3 tornado with peak winds estimated between 136 and 165 mph struck the Amazon DLI4 delivery station in Edwardsville, Illinois, causing the partial collapse of the 1.1-million-square-foot facility [11], [12]. The disaster resulted in the deaths of six individuals and left one other person critically injured [2], [18]. The incident, which occurred during a shift change, exposed a cascade of failures across multiple domains, including structural engineering, corporate emergency preparedness, and regulatory oversight [1], [8]. The tornado touched down at approximately 8:27 p.m. Central Standard Time, cutting through the building from west to east and triggering progressive structural failure that led to the collapse of two massive concrete walls and the roof [1], [5]. All fatalities and injuries were concentrated in a restroom located at the southern end of the building, while employees who had successfully navigated to a designated shelter area in the northern part of the facility survived [36], [51]. The event became a catalyst for significant legislative reform in the state of Illinois, prompting the enactment of new laws governing warehouse safety, structural design, and building inspector certification [40], [41]. It also sparked numerous wrongful death lawsuits against Amazon and various parties involved in the construction and development of the warehouse, alleging systemic negligence [43], [46]. The aftermath of the collapse serves as a case study in the complex interplay between natural disasters, industrial safety protocols, and the limitations of existing regulatory frameworks.

## Event Description and Casualties

The Edwardsville Amazon warehouse collapse was precipitated by a powerful tornado that formed as part of a larger multi-state outbreak of severe weather on the night of December 10, 2021 [9], [18]. The National Weather Service (NWS) later rated the tornado that struck the facility as an EF-3, indicating "severe" damage, with wind speeds measured between 136 and 165 miles per hour [12], [14]. This intensity significantly exceeded the 90 mph straight-line wind resistance requirement stipulated by the local building code under which the warehouse was constructed [4], [33]. Severe storms entered the Edwardsville area around 8:00 p.m. CST, with electronic tornado warnings delivered via a web-based application between 8:06 p.m. and 8:16 p.m. [1], [2]. Tornado sirens activated at approximately 8:18 p.m. [1]. The tornado itself touched down near I-270 and Illinois Route 255, southwest of the warehouse, traveled northeast through the south half of the building, and remained on the ground for approximately five minutes before dissipating [1], [5]. The impact was devastating; the tornado first impacted the west-facing wall, causing its collapse, which then triggered a progressive structural failure leading to the inward collapse of two 40-foot-tall, 11-inch-thick concrete exterior walls and the downward collapse of the entire roof over a significant portion of the facility [5], [25], [29].

The human toll of the collapse was concentrated among a small group of workers who took shelter in a restroom at the southern end of the building, unaware that the designated shelter was located in the north end [1], [20]. The collapsing southwest wall crushed the restroom, resulting in six confirmed fatalities and one critical injury from blunt force trauma and crushing [1]. A total of 45 people were rescued from the rubble [25]. The incident occurred during a busy shift change, complicating headcounts and initial assessments of casualties [11], [30]. Search-and-rescue operations officially transitioned to a recovery-only mode by Saturday evening with no expectation of finding survivors [25], [30]. The victims were identified by the Madison County Coroner Stephen Nonn on Sunday, December 12, 2021 [11], [24].

In the immediate aftermath, Amazon issued statements expressing condolences and extending support to affected employees and partners [13], [31], [44]. The company founder, Jeff Bezos, tweeted his heartbreak over the loss of "our teammates" [13]. Illinois Governor J.B. Pritzker spoke with Amazon officials and urged the company to provide every assistance to the community [25]. The event was part of a historic tornado outbreak that killed 89 people across four states and caused billions of dollars in damages [9].

## Structural Integrity and Building Code Compliance

The structural failure of the Edwardsville Amazon warehouse was a central focus of post-collapse investigations and has been described by independent experts as a catastrophic violation of building codes. Multiple sources, including government engineers and fire marshals who conducted on-site assessments shortly after the collapse, concluded that the building's support columns were inadequately anchored to the foundation [6], [28]. A government engineer's report prepared on the night of the disaster found that "a considerable number" of structural support columns were lifted out of the floor by the force of the EF-3 tornado's winds [6]. Fire Marshal Dan Bruno, a structures specialist for the West County EMS and Fire Protection District, observed that the columns appeared "lifted out of the floor," not ripped or torn at the base, indicating a fundamental failure in their anchorage against wind uplift forces [7], [16]. His contemporaneous memo documented that the columns were not secured with bolts or welds as required by the International Building Code (IBC); instead, only a bead of caulk-like material was present at the column bases [28], [33], [34]. This condition was described by another specialist as "a peg being lifted out of a hole" [16]. These findings constituted a grave violation of the IBC, specifically Section 1604.8.1, which addresses wind uplift resistance [34].

The building's design was based on a 90 mph straight-line wind resistance standard, which was the prevailing local building code requirement at the time of its construction in 2018 [4]. However, the EF-3 tornado produced peak winds exceeding 150 mph, making it clear that the structure was not designed to withstand known environmental loads common to the region [4], [33]. Even after the rebuild of the facility, city inspectors discovered nearly a dozen columns with "loose or missing anchor bolts," which were subsequently repaired within a month [28]. This discovery corroborated the initial findings of the structural failure and raised serious questions about the quality control and inspection processes that governed the original construction. The warehouse was built by Contegra Construction Co. and developed by TriStar Properties [7]. Despite these alarming findings from independent observers, Amazon maintained that the building "was built to code and fully inspected" and never released the results of its own joint forensic investigation with the building owner [28], [34].

This narrative of potential non-compliance stood in stark contrast to the official municipal inspection records. City documents released in 2022 showed that the warehouse passed all 49 municipal inspections during and after its construction and a re-inspection in 2020 [4], [7]. However, a critical discrepancy emerged: unlike inspection reports for other municipal buildings, such as the Edwardsville Township Offices, the Amazon warehouse reports contained no written notes or photographs from the inspectors [4]. The city declined to offer an explanation for this notable difference, fueling public suspicion that the pre-construction and post-construction building code enforcement may have been less rigorous than for other facilities [4]. The building's tilt-up concrete construction, a common method for large commercial structures, proved vulnerable under the extreme stress of the tornado's uplift forces [34]. The failure of the structural system highlights a profound disconnect between the documented evidence of physical failure and the assurances provided by the building's developer and tenant. This has led to ongoing calls for more stringent oversight and transparency in building inspections, particularly for large industrial facilities in tornado-prone areas.

## Corporate Emergency Response and Procedural Failures

The corporate response to the tornado at the Edwardsville Amazon warehouse was marked by significant procedural failures that directly contributed to the concentration of fatalities in a single area of the facility. While Amazon had a formal Emergency Action Plan (EAP), it was criticized for lacking specificity and failing to account for the primary regional threat of tornadoes [23], [27]. The plan included generic content, such as hurricane preparedness guidance, but omitted crucial information relevant to the Illinois location [23], [27]. Furthermore, the EAP failed to explicitly identify the designated tornado shelter location, relying instead on posted evacuation maps that were insufficient to guide employees correctly during a high-stress emergency [20], [49]. This lack of customization was a key deficiency noted by investigators [19].

Communication and training were also profoundly inadequate. The primary means of broadcasting the severe weather alert—a megaphone specified in the EAP—was locked in a cage and rendered inaccessible during the crisis [20], [23], [32]. This forced managers to run through the vast 1.1-million-square-foot facility to verbally instruct employees to take shelter, creating delays and confusion [23]. Survivors reported chaotic instructions, with managers shouting directions like "go to the bathrooms" without providing precise locations or clarifying which restrooms were safe [28], [32]. This vague directive led a group of workers, including five of the six eventual fatalities, to seek refuge in a restroom at the southern end of the building [1], [23]. They were unaware that the designated and structurally sound shelter was located in the northern end of the building, a fact that many employees, particularly contractors, could not recall due to inconsistent or non-existent tornado drills [8], [20]. OSHA's investigation found that some workers were unable to recall participating in shelter-in-place drills or knew the location of the designated storm shelter [8], [20].

Amazon's policy on productivity expectations further complicated the situation. Workers were reportedly forbidden from using personal cell phones during severe weather alerts, cutting them off from external communication and emergency updates [21]. Allegations surfaced that the company threatened to fire employees who attempted to leave the facility to seek shelter elsewhere, suggesting a work culture that prioritized operational continuity over worker safety [45], [46]. Text messages attributed to the girlfriend of victim Austin McEwen allegedly stated, "Amazon won’t let us leave until after the storm blows over," a claim corroborated by accounts from employees at neighboring Amazon warehouses who reported similar pressures to continue working during prior tornado events [45]. The sequence of events began when site management instructed contracted delivery company dispatchers to decide whether drivers should return to the facility or seek local shelter [1]. The on-site manager and process assistant then ran through the warehouse shouting to direct employees toward the designated tornado shelter in the northern end [1]. However, this effort was too late for those who had already taken shelter in the southern restroom, which was subsequently destroyed by the collapsing southwest wall [1]. The tragic outcome underscores how a combination of poor planning, inaccessible technology, inadequate training, and unclear communication can lead to fatal consequences, even if a general sheltering procedure exists on paper.

## Federal Regulatory Investigation and Findings

In the immediate aftermath of the collapse, the U.S. Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) launched a federal workplace safety investigation into the incident [2], [51]. Inspectors were onsite by December 11, 2021, just one day after the disaster, and a formal investigation was announced on Monday, December 13, 2021 [2], [44], [51]. The investigation was given a statutory six-month timeline to complete its findings and issue any citations or penalties [2], [18], [29]. A critical aspect of OSHA's mandate and subsequent findings was its explicit clarification of its jurisdictional boundaries. The agency repeatedly stated that it does not regulate building integrity, construction standards, or the structural components of a completed building; these matters fall under the purview of local and state building code enforcement agencies [3], [49], [51]. Therefore, OSHA's investigation focused solely on whether Amazon complied with federal workplace safety rules related to emergency action plans and employee protection [3], [18].

After completing its investigation, OSHA concluded its findings without issuing any citations or proposing penalties against Amazon [3], [36]. On April 26, 2022, the agency issued a non-punitive Hazard Alert Letter to the company outlining its findings and recommending three key improvements to its severe weather emergency procedures [3], [17], [50]. The letter stated that Amazon's existing severe weather emergency procedures met the minimum federal safety guidelines for storm sheltering but required improvement under the General Duty Clause of the Occupational Safety and Health Act [3], [22]. The three recommendations were:
1.  Ensuring all employees receive training and participate in emergency weather drills.
2.  Including site-specific information in severe weather emergency plans, such as exit routes and shelter-in-place guidance.
3.  Clearly identifying all audible warning devices and their locations in the plan and ensuring they are readily accessible [3], [17], [26].

The Hazard Alert Letter highlighted specific deficiencies observed during the investigation. These included the inaccessible, locked megaphone used for emergency alerts, the absence of mandatory shelter-in-place drills, and the generic nature of the EAP which lacked customization for tornado hazards in the Midwest [23], [32], [49]. OSHA also noted that some employees did not know the location of the designated storm shelter [19]. One source indicates that OSHA invoked the General Duty Clause to justify its findings, while another states the agency did not invoke the clause but affirmed it would do so in the future if necessary [49], [50]. This ambiguity suggests a cautious, non-punitive approach that prioritized procedural compliance over substantive safety outcomes. Ultimately, OSHA's final determination was that while Amazon's *procedures* were technically compliant, the execution during the actual emergency was flawed, leading to a tragic outcome that fell outside the agency's structural oversight authority [49]. This limited scope of the federal investigation created a significant regulatory vacuum that advocates and state legislators sought to fill through legislative action [40].

## Legislative and Policy Aftermath

The Edwardsville Amazon warehouse collapse served as a powerful catalyst for sweeping legislative reforms in the state of Illinois, aimed at closing the regulatory gaps exposed by the tragedy. The most significant piece of legislation to emerge from this effort was Public Act 104-0262, titled 'WAREHOUSE TORNADO PREPAREDNESS', which was signed into law on August 15, 2025 [40], [41]. This act represents a comprehensive response that moves beyond OSHA's limited focus on workplace procedures to address both operational planning and structural engineering standards. The law mandates that all warehouse operators in Illinois must develop and coordinate tornado safety plans with local emergency services, fire departments, and disaster agencies [40], [41]. These plans must be filed with the coordinating local entities, not with state-level offices, and must include detailed floor plans, emergency exit routes, designated shelter areas, and specific employee action protocols for all phases of a tornado event [40].

Perhaps the most impactful provision of the new law addresses the structural integrity of warehouses. For all newly constructed warehouse facilities, the legislation requires that their structural design must meet or exceed "life-safety performance levels for tornado loading equivalent to the most onerous other building code-prescribed extreme environmental loading events" [40], [41]. This effectively mandates that new warehouses in Illinois be engineered to withstand significant tornado-force winds, directly addressing the primary cause of the Edwardsville collapse. The law provides flexibility for design professionals to use either performance-based rational analysis or prescriptive methods outlined in FEMA P-431 and the Best Available Refuge Area Checklist [40], [41]. To ensure these new standards are properly enforced, the legislation also amends the Counties Code and Illinois Municipal Code to require building inspectors in counties and municipalities to hold International Code Council (ICC) certifications [40], [41]. This requirement, which takes effect on January 1, 2027, aims to elevate the professional qualifications of those responsible for inspecting industrial buildings [40].

The passage of this legislation followed the formation of the Illinois Warehouse Safety Standards Task Force, which was convened after the 2021 collapse and issued its final report in November 2024 [5], [52]. The task force's recommendations, which largely mirrored the provisions of the new law, called for amending the International Building Code adopted statewide in Illinois to require tornado shelters in warehouses [5], [10]. Although the final law removed the mandate for dedicated shelters, shifting focus to performance-based structural design, the advocacy for shelters played a crucial role in raising awareness [41]. The broader legislative landscape in Illinois was also shaped by the incident. The event highlighted systemic issues in warehouse labor conditions, contributing to the passage of the Warehouse Worker Protection Act (HB 2547), which expands protections for workers regarding productivity quotas and ensures rights to breaks [37], [38]. While this bill was vetoed by the governor in March 2025, his veto letter directed the Illinois Department of Labor to convene stakeholders to develop a new proposal, demonstrating the lasting influence of the Edwardsville tragedy on workplace safety discourse in the state [39]. The rebuilding of the Edwardsville facility began in June 2023, and the new warehouse reopened in September 2024, though Amazon confirmed it does not plan to install storm shelters or safe rooms because no jurisdiction requires them for such facilities [5], [36].

## Legal Proceedings and Corporate Accountability

Following the collapse, a series of wrongful death lawsuits were filed against Amazon and multiple other defendants, including the companies and individuals involved in the warehouse's construction and development [43], [46]. These legal actions allege a wide range of negligence and failures that contributed to the deaths of the six workers. The lawsuit filed on behalf of Austin McEwen's family, for example, named Amazon.com, the construction company, and the project's developer as defendants, seeking damages for claims that Amazon failed to warn employees of imminent danger, lacked an adequate emergency plan, did not evacuate personnel, and improperly directed workers to a known unsafe location—the restroom where they ultimately perished [43]. The suit also alleged that Amazon "carelessly required individuals … to continue working up until the moments before the tornado struck," prioritizing profits over safety during the busy holiday season [43].

Other lawsuits expanded the list of defendants to include design and engineering firms such as Stock & Associates Consulting Engineers, Gray Design Group, and McNealy Engineering, alleging that they were negligent in designing a structure that could not withstand foreseeable tornado-force winds [46]. The litigation also cited unverified but publicly reported text messages from the girlfriend of victim Austin McEwen, stating "Amazon won’t let us leave until after the storm blows over," as evidence that the company prohibited evacuation attempts despite warnings [45]. Another lawsuit filed on behalf of surviving driver Jamarco Hickman alleged he suffered from negligent infliction of emotional distress as a result of the event [46]. The legal arguments centered on the contention that Amazon, through its control over the facility and its policies, had a duty to protect its workers and their contractors from foreseeable dangers, including severe weather [45].

Despite the filing of multiple lawsuits, Amazon's defense strategy has largely relied on asserting that the building was "built to code and fully inspected" [28]. The company has not released the findings of its own internal forensic investigation, which it was conducting jointly with the building owner, creating a significant information gap in the legal proceedings [34]. One legal matter was resolved when Alice and Randall McEwen settled their wrongful death claims against six defendants for a total of $165,000 in August 2023 [42]. The settlement, which came with legal fees and expenses totaling $105,000, resolved claims arising from the verified incident but did not establish liability through a trial [42]. The broader legal battles continued, targeting planned depositions of Amazon management in both Edwardsville and Seattle with the goal of proceeding to trial [12]. The lawsuits not only sought financial compensation but also aimed to bring corporate accountability for what plaintiffs argued was a systemic failure to prioritize worker safety. The advocacy efforts of the victims' families, particularly Carla Cope, sister of Clayton Cope, were instrumental in keeping the issue in the public eye and pushing for legislative change, transforming a workplace tragedy into a national conversation about industrial safety [9], [52].
