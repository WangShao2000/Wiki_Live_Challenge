# Ang Totoong Narcolist

## Genesis and Content of the Video Series

The "Ang Totoong Narcolist" phenomenon emerged in the Philippines in early 2019 as a viral YouTube-based video series that ignited a significant political firestorm [1]. The project was the work of Peter Joemel Advincula, who operated under the alias "Bikoy" [2], [19]. The series consisted of five videos uploaded between April 2, 2019, and May 6, 2019, though some sources note an upload timeline ending on April 22, 2019 [2], [8]. These videos were hosted on a YouTube channel also named "Ang Totoong Narcolist" and presented themselves as a confessional exposé by a former member of a powerful illegal drug syndicate [10]. The narration, delivered by a hooded Advincula, detailed his purported experiences working within the organization, claiming he had started as a CCTV operator, then progressed to scanning the tattoos of senior syndicate members, and ultimately handled the depositing of drug money [18], [19]. This self-presentation lent an air of authenticity to the allegations, positioning him as an insider with firsthand knowledge.

The content of the videos was explosive, directly accusing several prominent individuals aligned with the administration of President Rodrigo Duterte of complicity in the illegal drug trade [1]. Among the most high-profile figures targeted were members of the Duterte family and their close associates. Specifically, the videos accused Paolo Duterte, the president's son; Waldo Carpio, the brother-in-law of First Lady Sara Duterte; Honeylet Avanceña, another relative of the First Lady; and her daughter Kitty Duterte of being patrons of the syndicate [2], [3]. Episode 4 of the series explicitly named Paolo Duterte, Veronica Salvador, Bong Go, and Mans Carpio as "main players from the Davao side" of a supposed "Tausug drug group" [3]. The narrator claimed he had personally scanned the alleged syndicate tattoos of these individuals at the Misibis Bay resort in Albay [3].

Further accusations extended to Senator Christopher "Bong" Go, who served as a Special Assistant to the President (SAP) at the time [1], [2]. The videos also implicated businessman Elizaldy Co, whose resort was mentioned as a location for the syndicate's activities [3]. In one instance, Advincula even stated his intention to sue Paolo Duterte, Bong Go, and Manases Carpio for defamation [19]. The entire series was framed around the existence of a sophisticated drug operation, referred to at times as the "Quadrangle Drug Syndicate," allegedly operating out of the Misibis Bay resort [3]. The production of these videos appears to have been carefully staged and coordinated. According to an affidavit filed by Advincula, the meetings related to the creation of the videos, dubbed "Project Sodoma," began in August 2018 [8]. Video shooting commenced on March 29, 2019, inside the Jesuit Communications Building (Jescom) at Ateneo de Manila University (ADMU) [8]. The first video was uploaded on April 1, 2019, and the final video was uploaded on April 22, 2019 [8]. Notably, the videos were uploaded using the ADMU server, specifically the "Guest 1" SSID attached to the university's Management Information System, from a room inside the faculty housing compound [8].

Advincula's credibility as the source of these revelations was immediately questioned due to his personal history. Born on March 22, 1989, in Donsol, Sorsogon, he was identified as a convicted fraudster [2]. He had served a six-year prison sentence for fraud, which he completed and from which he was released early in 2017 [2]. This background cast a shadow over his claims, establishing a baseline of skepticism regarding his motives and reliability. Furthermore, his narrative contained elements of continuity with past political conflicts. In a December 2016 document he authored, known as the "Sotto document," Advincula had previously accused officials from the preceding administration, including then-President Benigno Aquino III, Interior Secretary Mar Roxas, and Senator Leila de Lima, of meeting with the same syndicate figure, Elizaldy "Zaldy" Co, at the Misibis Bay Resort [13]. This earlier document, however, was unnotarized and lacked the detail present in his later "Bikoy" videos, where these Aquino-era figures were conspicuously absent from the list of accused patrons, replaced instead by Duterte-aligned politicians and businessmen [13]. This shift in targets added another layer of complexity and potential inconsistency to his overall story.

## Institutional Responses and Political Polarization

The release of the "Ang Totoong Narcolist" videos triggered an immediate and deeply polarized response from various branches of the Philippine government, revealing a stark division in how to interpret and act upon politically charged allegations. The reactions ranged from swift endorsement and prosecution to cautious dismissal and outright condemnation, reflecting the intense political climate surrounding the Duterte administration. The Department of Justice (DOJ) initiated an investigation into the videos on April 17, 2019, signaling official scrutiny [2]. However, the most aggressive institutional response came from the Philippine National Police (PNP). Under the leadership of Chief General Oscar Albayalde, the PNP Criminal Investigation and Detection Group (CIDG) treated the allegations with serious credence. On an unspecified date in 2019, following the videos' release and Advincula's initial public appearance, Albayalde stated that the accusations leveled by "Bikoy" were "substantially accurate" [7]. He further asserted that these claims were supported by "other documentary evidence and other sources" gathered by the CIDG, which had already begun filing criminal charges against several opposition figures [7]. Based on this assessment, the PNP filed a sweeping set of complaints—including sedition, inciting to sedition, cyberlibel, libel, estafa (fraud), harboring a criminal, and obstruction of justice—against Vice President Leni Robredo and 34 other individuals linked to the opposition [3], [9]. The charges were explicitly grounded in Advincula’s testimony and the documentary evidence collected by the CIDG, with the PNP chief emphasizing that the ultimate assessment of a witness's credibility rested with the courts, not with the police itself [9].

In contrast to the PNP's enthusiastic endorsement, the DOJ, led by Secretary Menardo Guevarra, adopted a more measured and procedural approach. While advancing the investigation, Guevarra clarified that the decision to grant Peter Joemel Advincula provisional protection under the Witness Protection Program (WPP) would depend on the DOJ's evaluation of his credibility as a prosecution witness [9]. He confirmed that Advincula could receive such protection upon submitting a sworn affidavit and formally applying for the program, but stressed that the DOJ's evaluation was pending [9]. This cautious stance was mirrored by the legislative branch. Senator Panfilo Lacson, then chair of the Senate Committee on Public Order and Dangerous Drugs, announced on April 9, 2019, that his committee was prepared to schedule a hearing on Advincula's allegations "within this week" [12]. However, this readiness was conditional; Lacson stipulated that Advincula must first personally present a sworn statement and verifiable supporting evidence, such as bank documents or wire transfer records, to substantiate his accusations against the president's inner circle [12]. This demand for concrete proof highlighted a critical procedural check-and-balance mechanism that stood in tension with the PNP's more direct acceptance of Advincula's narrative.

The executive branch, through Malacañang Palace, responded with a preemptive counter-narrative that quickly labeled the entire affair a deliberate political conspiracy. Presidential Spokesperson Salvador Panelo announced the discovery of an "Oust Duterte" matrix on April 22, 2019, which listed journalists and opposition figures allegedly involved in plots to remove the president [2]. He later revealed a "deliberate conspiracy" orchestrated by the Liberal Party (LP) and the Magdalo group, characterizing the "Ang Totoong Narcolist" videos as an election-related gambit designed to discredit the Duterte family [20]. Communications Secretary Martin Andanar and Panelo subsequently named specific co-conspirators, including Gary Alejano, Manuel Roxas, and Edwin Lacierda [20]. The administration's executive branch effectively shut down any potential for a neutral legislative inquiry before it could even begin. Following Advincula's public unmasking at the Integrated Bar of the Philippines (IBP) office on May 6, 2019, Senate President Vicente Sotto III declared that Advincula had previously attempted to peddle similar falsehoods against officials from the Aquino administration, labeling him a "gun-for-hire" [20]. This characterization was echoed by Senate Minority Leader Franklin Drilon, who dismissed Advincula's subsequent recantation as "ridiculous" and called for charges to be filed against him [11]. The cancellation of the scheduled Senate hearing on May 8, 2019, marked the end of any formal legislative process, solidifying the administration's position that the issue was closed and the accuser was a discredited figure [18], [20]. This institutional polarization demonstrated how state apparatuses became tools in a larger political battle, with the PNP pursuing prosecutions based on a controversial witness while the executive and legislative branches worked to delegitimize both the witness and the allegations themselves.

## The Recantation and the Project Sodoma Narrative

The trajectory of the "Ang Totoong Narcolist" saga took a dramatic and pivotal turn on May 6, 2019, when Peter Joemel Advincula publicly revealed himself as the hooded narrator "Bikoy" during a news conference at the Integrated Bar of the Philippines (IBP) office [1], [11], [12]. Initially, he reiterated his support for the original allegations, standing by the accusations made in the videos against individuals close to President Duterte [12], [19]. However, this public declaration was short-lived. Just days later, on May 22-23, 2019, Advincula surrendered to authorities at Camp Crame and retracted all his previous statements [1], [11]. In a stunning reversal, he claimed that the entire "Ang Totoong Narcolist" series was a fabrication, orchestrated by the Liberal Party and Senator Antonio Trillanes IV as part of a larger, coordinated political operation [1], [7], [11]. This new narrative introduced the concept of "Project Sodoma," a code name for what he described as a concerted effort by the opposition to destabilize the Duterte administration and orchestrate an ousting plot [10], [11].

Under this revised account, the goal of "Project Sodoma" was to install Vice President Leni Robredo as president and Antonio Trillanes as vice president [7]. Advincula alleged that he was promised a ₱500,000 payment for his participation and claimed the videos were part of a script written by the Liberal Party [1], [2]. This pivot transformed the entire affair from a matter of public interest into a high-stakes political confrontation centered on the accusation of conspiracy versus the defense of innocence. Advincula shifted from being a witness providing explosive testimony to becoming the primary suspect in a potential crime, facing charges of perjury for his contradictory statements. His new claims required a complete reversal of the existing narrative, demanding that the very institutions that had pursued charges against the opposition now investigate him and his new accusers. To bolster his new story, Advincula submitted two CIDG affidavits dated May 29, 2019, and July 17, 2019, which explicitly linked "Project Sodoma" to the opposition and were used to support the inciting-to-sedition complaint filed on August 9, 2019 against a wide array of opposition figures [3].

This recantation and the introduction of the "Project Sodoma" theory had profound consequences. It forced a re-evaluation of the initial wave of criminal complaints filed by the PNP. The credibility of the entire case against the opposition crumbled with the withdrawal of its primary witness. The administration, which had initially positioned itself to defend the president's family, was now faced with a claim that the entire scandal was a manufactured attack. The opposition figures who had been charged with sedition, cyberlibel, and other crimes found their legal situation fundamentally altered. They were no longer just defendants in a case brought by a whistleblower; they were now accused of being the architects of a massive conspiracy involving a disgraced informant. Businessman Elizaldy Co, whose resort was mentioned in the original videos, filed a separate cyber libel complaint seeking over ₱1 billion in damages against Advincula, Facebook, and YouTube for the false accusations [11]. The situation created a complex legal and political quagmire, with each side presenting a compelling but mutually exclusive narrative. The government's institutions were now tasked with investigating whether Advincula's recantation was genuine or another fabricated lie, adding another layer of uncertainty to an already volatile situation. The episode underscored the fragility of truth in highly politicized environments and demonstrated how easily a narrative can be flipped from an exposé into an allegation of conspiracy, with devastating implications for all parties involved.

## Legal Proceedings and Unresolved Charges

The legal fallout from the "Ang Totoong Narcolist" videos was extensive and prolonged, resulting in a complex web of criminal complaints, dismissals, and protracted court battles. Immediately following the release of the videos, the Philippine National Police (PNP) Criminal Investigation and Detection Group (CIDG) filed a comprehensive set of charges against Vice President Leni Robredo and 34 other prominent opposition figures [9]. These charges included sedition, inciting to sedition, cyberlibel, libel, estafa (fraud), harboring a criminal, and obstruction of justice, all explicitly grounded in the testimony and revelations provided by Peter Joemel Advincula [3], [9]. A Quezon City court issued an arrest order for Antonio Trillanes IV and ten others on February 14, 2020, for their alleged involvement in "conspiracy to commit sedition" [16]. Trillanes promptly posted bail on February 18, 2020, and subsequently challenged the legality of the charges [16]. On October 19, 2020, Trillanes filed a Motion to Quash the Information, arguing that the charging document was legally defective for failing to provide specific factual details establishing the elements of conspiracy or sedition, thereby violating his constitutional right to be informed of the nature of the accusation [17]. This motion, joined by co-accused Joselito Saracho, sought a dismissal of the case to prevent a protracted trial on a void charging document, highlighting the ongoing legal challenges that kept the case active in the courts well into late 2020 [17].

Despite the flurry of activity from the PNP and the judiciary, the Department of Justice (DOJ) eventually took a decisive step that significantly altered the legal landscape. On February 10, 2020, the DOJ officially dismissed the cases of sedition, cyberlibel, libel, estafa, harboring a criminal, and obstruction of justice that had been filed against Vice President Leni Robredo and 23 other individuals, including senators, religious leaders, and lawyers [14]. The dismissal was based on a finding of lack of probable cause [14]. This resolution effectively cleared the primary targets of the initial wave of accusations and dealt a major blow to the administration's legal strategy. However, the dismissal of these cases did not bring the legal proceedings to a definitive close. The case against Antonio Trillanes IV remained in limbo, with arraignment delays continuing due to motions to quash as of October 22, 2020 [16]. The DOJ's resolution in the main case was later cited in a broader context on February 10, 2025, which charged 11 respondents, including Advincula and Trillanes, with conspiracy to commit sedition under Article 141 of the Revised Penal Code, indicating the enduring legal weight of the affair [10].

Peter Joemel Advincula himself did not escape legal repercussions for his actions. His career as a political actor was fraught with legal troubles stemming from the scandal. In January 2021, he was charged with perjury for his contradictory statements regarding the videos [2]. Later, in November 2021, he faced murder charges related to the so-called "Albay killings" [2]. These charges, along with his conviction for fraud, painted a picture of a man with a troubled legal history who leveraged a national political scandal for his own purposes. Despite these issues, Advincula continued to be involved in the political sphere. After being disqualified by the Commission on Elections (COMELEC) in October 2024, he filed as a nuisance candidate for the 2025 Senate election, demonstrating his persistence in trying to maintain a political presence [2]. The legal aftermath thus reveals a messy and unresolved conclusion. While the administration failed to secure convictions against its political opponents, it successfully launched damaging legal proceedings that tied up their time and resources for years. Conversely, the opposition figures, particularly Trillanes, remained entangled in a protracted legal battle, illustrating the long-term costs of being ensnared in such politically motivated attacks. The saga ended not with a clean victory for either side, but with a series of partial victories and ongoing legal disputes that reflected the deep divisions within the country's justice system.

## Credibility Controversies and Inconsistencies

The entire "Ang Totoong Narcolist" affair was fundamentally undermined by persistent questions regarding the credibility of its creator, Peter Joemel Advincula, and the glaring inconsistencies within his own narratives. From the outset, his background as a convicted fraudster with a six-year prison sentence, released early in 2017, cast a long shadow over his claims [2]. This history established a baseline of suspicion that his motives were questionable. This skepticism was further fueled by his own admission that he had a history of attempting to sell false information to law enforcement agencies for payment. On May 8, 2019, Philippine National Police (PNP) Chief Gen. Oscar Albayalde publicly labeled Advincula an "information peddler," citing two prior instances where Advincula allegedly offered false information to police for sums ranging from ₱40,000 to ₱70,000 but failed to deliver or provided baseless claims [18]. This characterization directly contradicted the PNP chief's earlier statement that Advincula's initial accusations were "substantially accurate" and severely damaged his reputation as a reliable witness [7], [18].

Beyond his personal history, a detailed analysis by VERA Files Fact Check exposed recurring themes, missing information, and glaring inconsistencies across Advincula's various pronouncements and sworn statements [13]. One of the most significant contradictions was the shifting target of his accusations. In a December 2016 document he wrote, the "Sotto document," Advincula had accused officials from the preceding Aquino administration, including President Benigno Aquino III, Interior Secretary Mar Roxas, and Senator Leila de Lima, of having meetings with the drug syndicate figure Elizaldy "Zaldy" Co [13]. However, in his May 6, 2019 affidavit and press briefing detailing the "Ang Totoong Narcolist" videos, these Aquino-era figures were entirely omitted from his list of accused patrons. Instead, his focus shifted entirely to Duterte-aligned figures, naming Paolo Duterte and Bong Go as the syndicate's patrons in the Davao area [13]. This abrupt change in targets raised serious questions about the factual basis of his claims and suggested that his accusations might be tailored to fit a current political agenda rather than reflecting a consistent reality. The fact-checking analysis concluded that his accounts of the drug syndicate's structure and operations varied significantly across different affidavits and press conferences, further eroding confidence in his narrative [13].

The controversy also spilled over into the realm of traditional media. When the "matrix" report accusing journalists and opposition figures of plotting to oust President Duterte surfaced, the Philippine Center for Investigative Journalism (PCIJ) issued a strong denial. PCIJ executive director Malou Mangahas stated that the organization did not receive any email from Vera Files president Ellen Tordesillas about the videos, denied posting or distributing any stories related to them, and confirmed that the video was originally accessed by the public via YouTube [4]. She emphasized that PCIJ is a non-stock, independent media organization and warned that those behind the "matrix" may have committed a crime by invading privacy through unauthorized access to journalists' emails [4]. This clarification was crucial in distinguishing legitimate investigative journalism from the unsubstantiated allegations circulating online. The combination of Advincula's questionable background, his admitted propensity for peddling false information, and the demonstrable inconsistencies in his own stories created a mountain of evidence against his credibility. This body of evidence provided objective support for the conclusions reached by critics like Senator Panfilo Lacson, who had dismissed the entire episode as "nonsense" and Advincula as a "gun-for-hire" even before the full extent of the inconsistencies became public [18], [20]. Ultimately, the credibility crisis surrounding Peter Joemel Advincula was the single most important factor in the collapse of the "Ang Totoong Narcolist" scandal, transforming it from a potential political earthquake into a cautionary tale about the dangers of unchecked misinformation.

## Aftermath and Enduring Legacy

The "Ang Totoong Narcolist" saga concluded without a clear victor, leaving a complex and lasting legacy that reshaped the political landscape of the Philippines and left deep scars on its institutions. The most immediate outcome was the failure of the Duterte administration to secure convictions against its political opponents. The Department of Justice's dismissal of the sedition and other charges against Vice President Leni Robredo and 23 others on February 10, 2020, for lack of probable cause, represented a significant legal and political defeat for the executive branch [14]. This outcome signaled that even in a highly charged political environment, the standard of probable cause and judicial review could serve as a formidable barrier to politically motivated prosecutions. However, the administration was not without success. By launching these damaging legal proceedings, it succeeded in tying up the time and resources of prominent opposition figures for years, creating a chilling effect and forcing them to divert attention from policy-making to defending themselves in court. The protracted legal battles, particularly the ongoing challenge to the charges against Antonio Trillanes IV, exemplified the long-term strategic cost of being targeted in such a manner [16], [17].

For Peter Joemel Advincula, the affair cemented his reputation as a controversial and unreliable figure, yet it also propelled him back into the public eye and allowed him to pursue his own political ambitions. Despite being charged with perjury in January 2021 and murder in November 2021, he continued to be a fixture in Philippine politics [2]. His attempt to file as a nuisance candidate for the 2025 Senate election, even after being disqualified by the COMELEC, demonstrates his resilience and his ability to leverage the notoriety gained from the scandal to remain relevant [2]. His journey from a convicted fraudster and "information peddler" to a key player in a national political drama illustrates the blurred lines between fact, fiction, and political opportunism in modern Philippine society.

Perhaps the most enduring aspect of the "Ang Totoong Narcolist" affair is the persistence of the "Project Sodoma" conspiracy theory in the nation's political lexicon. Although unsubstantiated and largely debunked, the term has become a shorthand for describing perceived anti-government plots. This demonstrates how powerful ideas and narratives can endure long after the specific facts that gave rise to them have crumbled. The incident serves as a stark case study on the weaponization of law and technology. It showcased how legal tools like sedition and cyberlibel could be deployed as instruments of political warfare against opponents, while digital platforms like YouTube could be used to bypass traditional media gatekeepers and spread explosive allegations directly to a mass audience [1], [9]. The use of a prestigious institution like Ateneo de Manila University's server to host the videos added another layer of complexity, implicating academic and religious spaces in the political fray and raising questions about accountability [8].

In summary, the legacy of "Ang Totoong Narcolist" is multifaceted. It exposed the deep polarization within the Philippines' state institutions, revealing how they could be mobilized to serve opposing political ends. It highlighted the immense power and danger of social media as a tool for political mobilization and disinformation. Most importantly, it left a lasting imprint on the country's political discourse, embedding concepts like "narco-politics" and "ouster plots" as permanent features of the political debate. The saga stands as a testament to the fragility of truth in a hyper-partisan environment and serves as a critical lesson on the importance of due process, credible evidence, and journalistic integrity in navigating the turbulent waters of modern democracy.
