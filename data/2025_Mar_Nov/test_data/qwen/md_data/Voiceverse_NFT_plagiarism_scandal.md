# The Voiceverse NFT Plagiarism Scandal

The Voiceverse NFT Plagiarism Scandal, which erupted in early 2022, stands as a seminal event in the history of the nascent Web3 and artificial intelligence industries. It was not merely an instance of intellectual property theft but a complex case study exposing deep-seated issues of haste, deception, and a lack of ethical governance within the burgeoning NFT ecosystem [2]. The controversy centered on Voiceverse, a blockchain-based startup that marketed proprietary AI voice cloning technology sold as non-fungible tokens (NFTs) [2], [5]. The scandal began when the project's creators were exposed for using a free, non-commercial text-to-speech service named 15.ai to generate promotional voice clips without permission or attribution [1], [2]. To conceal their actions, they manipulated the audio files through pitch-shifting, attempting to pass off the stolen content as their own original technology [2], [3]. This brazen act was swiftly uncovered through forensic evidence provided by the 15.ai developer, whose server logs irrefutably linked the infringing content to Voiceverse's promotional materials [2], [7]. The incident transformed from a niche tech controversy into a mainstream media event following the involvement of prominent voice actor Troy Baker, whose controversial partnership announcement amplified the backlash [5], [22]. His subsequent termination of the collaboration underscored the severity of the reputational damage inflicted upon Voiceverse [3], [7]. The scandal did not occur in isolation; it was a symptom of a larger industry-wide plague of plagiarism that plagued major NFT marketplaces like OpenSea and Cent, where hundreds of thousands of fraudulent assets were being minted daily [6], [23]. Furthermore, the investigation into Voiceverse extended beyond the initial incident to scrutinize its founder, artist Saba Moeel, revealing additional allegations of visual art tracing and the commodification of Black culture, leading to her censure by professional institutions like the Toronto Comic Arts Festival [9], [26]. The long-term repercussions of the Voiceverse scandal proved profound, providing critical evidence in subsequent class-action lawsuits against Voiceverse's parent company, LOVO, Inc. [7], [14]. These legal battles established new precedents, demonstrating that while traditional copyright law may be ill-equipped to handle AI-generated clones, state-level "right of publicity" laws and breach-of-contract claims offer powerful new avenues for holding companies accountable for deceptive data acquisition practices [17], [18]. Thus, the Voiceverse scandal serves as a foundational case, illustrating how a single act of misconduct can ripple outward, shaping platform policies, influencing legal frameworks, and setting enduring standards for accountability in the digital age.

## The Deception Unveiled: A Timeline of Plagiarism and Exposure

The Voiceverse scandal unfolded over a ten-day period in January 2022, a timeline marked by premeditated deception, rapid discovery, and a series of contradictory corporate responses. The core of the incident involved the unauthorized use of creative work from the open-source, non-commercial AI research project 15.ai, which was then misrepresented as proprietary technology [1], [2]. The sequence of events reveals a calculated attempt by the Voiceverse marketing team to create a high-impact demonstration for a major partnership announcement while simultaneously evading attribution and violating terms of service [2], [3]. On January 4, 2022, just ten days before a highly anticipated partnership announcement, members of the Voiceverse team utilized 15.ai's text-to-speech platform to generate voice lines for characters from the popular animated series 'My Little Pony: Friendship Is Magic' [3], [7]. Specifically, they created the character lines "Hi Frens! We giggle in the Chubbiverse..." attributed to Twilight Sparkle and "Hi Frens! i got an alpha drop for you?!?" attributed to Rainbow Dash [3], [7]. This initial act constituted a direct violation of 15.ai's terms of service, which explicitly prohibited commercial use and required mandatory attribution for any derivative works [3], [24]. In an effort to hide their theft, the Voiceverse team employed a technical maneuver: they applied pitch-shifting to the generated audio files [2], [3], [7]. This manipulation was designed to alter the vocal characteristics enough to make the origin less obvious, allowing them to falsely market the clips as their own proprietary AI voice cloning technology in promotional animations for their "Chubbiverse" project [2], [24]. This act of misrepresentation was central to the deception, framing a clear-cut case of plagiarism as an innovative technological achievement.

The meticulously crafted facade of innovation was shattered on January 14, 2022, when the pseudonymous creator of 15.ai publicly exposed the deception [2]. Using irrefutable evidence from the 15.ai server logs, the developer demonstrated that Voiceverse had indeed used their service to generate the infringing content nine days prior to the public announcement [7]. The server logs timestamped the generation of the exact audio files and directly correlated them with Voiceverse's promotional videos, which were themselves timestamped on January 4, 2022 [7]. This forensic evidence left no room for plausible deniability, proving the theft occurred during a frantic rush to prepare a tech demo for the upcoming partnership announcement [2], [3]. Within hours of this public revelation, Voiceverse issued a response. Initially, the company denied the allegations [4]. However, this stance was quickly abandoned as the evidence mounted. At 2:09 p.m. EST on the same day, January 14, 2022, Voiceverse posted a public apology on Twitter [3], [24]. The apology tweet read: "Hey @fifteenai we are extremely sorry about this. The voice was indeed taken from your platform, which our marketing team used without giving proper credit. Chubbiverse team has no knowledge of this. We will make sure this never happens again." [24]. While acknowledging the wrongdoing, this statement attempted to mitigate responsibility by attributing the entire incident to a rogue "marketing team," thereby distancing the rest of the organization, specifically the "Chubbiverse team," from the act [4], [24]. This narrative was a classic deflection tactic, suggesting a lack of internal oversight and accountability rather than a coordinated strategy of deception. The apology was met with further criticism, particularly because it came from a platform known for supporting NFT integration, a stance that had been opposed by the 15.ai developer since December 2021 [1].

The aftermath of the exposure saw a swift and decisive break from one of its most prominent figures. The partnership with voice actor Troy Baker, announced on January 14, became untenable. Despite the apology, the damage to Voiceverse's credibility was severe, and the company faced immediate community backlash upon its launch [5]. The incident also cast a long shadow over the broader NFT market, which was already grappling with rampant fraud and plagiarism [6], [30]. The Voiceverse case became a cautionary tale, cited in AI incident databases and by news outlets like Eurogamer and Kotaku Australia as a prime example of theft stemming from generative artificial intelligence [2]. The scandal's legacy extended far beyond the initial apology, providing crucial evidence in future legal proceedings that would hold the company's parent entity accountable for similar patterns of misconduct [7], [14]. The detailed timeline underscores a critical lesson for emerging tech companies: in the digital realm, every action leaves a traceable record, and attempts to conceal unethical behavior are often futile in the face of modern forensic tools.

## Human Element and Fallout: The Troy Baker Partnership and Its Demise

The involvement of acclaimed voice actor Troy Baker transformed the Voiceverse scandal from a specialized critique of a crypto startup into a widely reported cultural moment, amplifying its impact and highlighting the precarious position of celebrity endorsers in the volatile world of Web3. On January 14, 2022, Baker announced his partnership with Voiceverse via a social media post that immediately set a confrontational tone [2], [5]. His message included the inflammatory phrase, "You can hate. Or you can create. What'll it be?" [5], [22]. This rhetoric framed any skepticism or criticism of NFTs or the Voiceverse project itself as mere "hating," effectively dismissing dissent and creating an adversarial atmosphere before the plagiarism was even revealed [22]. Known for his roles in major video games like *The Last of Us* and various Batman titles, Baker's endorsement lent significant legitimacy to the project, positioning it as a forward-thinking venture aimed at empowering creators in the Metaverse [4], [5]. However, his choice of words backfired spectacularly in the ensuing media firestorm. The hashtag #hate started trending, and his tweet was widely criticized for its antagonistic and dismissive nature [5], [22]. The backlash was not limited to anonymous online users; it was echoed by peers in the creative community, including game developers Mike Bithell, Alanah Pearce, and Austin Wintory, who dedicated a segment of their podcast *Play, Watch, Listen* to discussing the controversy with him shortly after his initial announcement [22].

When the 15.ai exposé broke on the same day as his announcement, the situation escalated rapidly. Facing intense public pressure, Baker reversed his position nine days later. On January 31, 2022, he terminated his partnership with Voiceverse, issuing a formal statement on Twitter [3], [7]. His retraction was notable for its direct acknowledgment of the failure of his initial communication strategy. He wrote, "Thank you all for your feedback and patience. After careful consideration, I’ve decided to not continue the partnership with VoiceVerseNFT. Intentions aside, I’ve heard you and apologize for accusing anyone of 'hating' just by simply disagreeing with me" [22]. This apology directly addressed the public and peer criticism he had received, showing that the toxic environment fostered by his original tweet was a significant factor in his decision to sever ties [22]. The mutual termination was officially confirmed by both parties. Voiceverse responded with a tweet stating, "We have mutually decided to end our partnership with Troy Baker" [25]. In this statement, the company sought to project an image of resilience, reaffirming its commitment to executing its roadmap and becoming "the voice of Web3.0" [25]. This response starkly contrasted with the reality of the situation: the company had just admitted to a foundational act of theft and was facing widespread condemnation [25]. The dismissal of the partnership served as a final blow to Voiceverse's credibility, signaling to the market that even a high-profile celebrity could not salvage a project built on a foundation of deception. The episode illustrates the double-edged sword of celebrity endorsements in emerging industries; while they can provide a veneer of legitimacy, they also expose individuals to immense personal risk and scrutiny. Baker's experience demonstrates that in the era of instantaneous social media, a single poorly considered statement can become a focal point of failure during a crisis, and that the integrity of a project's leadership ultimately outweighs the value of its celebrity supporters.

## The Ecosystem of Plagiarism: Systemic Issues in Early NFT Marketplaces

The Voiceverse scandal did not occur in a vacuum; it was a prominent symptom of a much larger, systemic crisis of plagiarism and fraud that plagued the early NFT marketplaces in 2022. The brazenness of Voiceverse's actions was enabled by a permissive ecosystem where lax enforcement, user-friendly minting tools, and a general ethos of "move fast and break things" created fertile ground for bad actors. The timing of the Voiceverse incident coincided with a period of unprecedented abuse across the dominant platforms, most notably OpenSea. In January 2022, OpenSea publicly acknowledged the scale of the problem, stating that over 80% of the NFTs created for free on its platform were "plagiarized works, fake collections, and spam" [6], [30], [31], [32]. This staggering statistic revealed that Voiceverse was not an isolated offender but rather a participant in a widespread culture of intellectual property theft [6]. The primary vector for this abuse was the "lazy minting" feature, which allowed users to create NFTs without paying the blockchain gas fees until a sale was made, drastically lowering the barrier to entry for mass-producing counterfeit goods [6]. Artists and rights holders found themselves inundated with unauthorized copies of their work, often finding their creations tokenized and sold by strangers [16].

In response to the mounting crisis, major NFT marketplaces were forced to take drastic measures, though their solutions were often reactive and imperfect. OpenSea attempted to curb the abuse by announcing a policy limiting free mints to 50 items per user [6]. However, this move was met with significant community backlash and was quickly reversed, highlighting the difficulty platforms faced in balancing anti-fraud efforts with user accessibility [6]. Meanwhile, the marketplace Cent took a more extreme approach. Following reports of rampant unauthorized re-minting of others' work, Cent suspended all NFT sales on February 6, 2022, except for its dedicated "Valuables" marketplace for tweet NFTs [23], [31], [33]. CEO Cameron Hejazi described the problem as a "fundamental problem" in Web3 and compared existing enforcement efforts to a "whack-a-mole" game, noting that account bans were unsustainable and temporary measures like free-mint limits had failed [23], [33]. This admission from a platform that had famously sold Jack Dorsey's first tweet NFT for $2.9 million underscored the fragility of the trust model underpinning the early NFT space [33].

The failure of centralized platforms to adequately police their marketplaces led to a shift in defensive strategies among artists and communities. Many creators publicly denounced platforms like OpenSea for their slow and inadequate responses to DMCA takedown requests [16]. Prominent artists such as Liam Sharp and RJ Palmer expressed frustration with having to report repeated thefts only to have them ignored [16]. This prompted third-party initiatives to fill the void. DeviantArt, for instance, launched a machine learning tool called 'DeviantArt Protect,' which scans Ethereum and Polygon blockchains for possible infringements, sending over 50,000 alerts since its deployment [16]. The development of such tools highlights a critical gap in the ecosystem: the lack of robust, automated systems for intellectual property protection, a problem that remains unresolved today. The Voiceverse scandal fits squarely within this context. The project's ability to commit such a blatant act of plagiarism so close to its launch, and to use a platform like OpenSea for distribution, was a direct result of the systemic failures of the time. The incident serves as a powerful microcosm of the "Wild West" era of Web3, where technological innovation outpaced the development of governance, legal frameworks, and ethical standards. The fact that a high-profile startup could engage in such egregious behavior with relative impunity speaks volumes about the perceived lack of accountability in an environment where enforcement was weak and tools for copying were ubiquitous. The industry's subsequent struggles to combat this wave of fraud demonstrate that the problems identified during the Voiceverse scandal were not unique to one company, but endemic to the entire digital asset landscape at the time.

## Beyond the Code: Controversies Surrounding the Artist Saba Moeel

While the Voiceverse scandal was primarily defined by its technical act of plagiarism, deeper investigations revealed troubling patterns of misconduct associated with its artist, Saba Moeel, also known by her artistic name Pink Cat. Her involvement with the project brought the focus onto the human element behind the technology, uncovering a history of visual art tracing, cultural appropriation, and problematic online conduct that ultimately led to her condemnation by professional institutions [9], [26]. The allegations against Moeel went far beyond the scope of the 15.ai incident, painting a picture of an artist accused of systematically misappropriating the work of others for commercial gain. She was accused of tracing copyrighted artwork from well-known properties such as 'Tank Girl' and 'Tom and Jerry', as well as incorporating uncredited artwork from multiple other Instagram artists into her own portfolio [9]. These accusations of "swiping" or tracing artworks—creating derivative works based on another's original creation without permission—escalated the controversy beyond standard NFT-related criticisms like environmental concerns or over-commercialization, shifting the focus to questions of artistic integrity and ethics [27]. The issue gained significant traction within the comics and digital art communities, who pointed to these alleged transgressions as evidence of a pattern of behavior that was unacceptable for a featured guest at a major festival [9], [27].

Perhaps the most serious and damaging aspect of the controversy surrounding Moeel involved allegations of the commodification and appropriation of Black culture. Moeel herself identified her alter ego, "Pink Cat," as Black in a 2019 profile [26]. However, she faced sharp criticism for allegedly profiting from this identity while being connected to projects and expressing views seen as harmful to the Black community [9], [29]. One artist, Ngozi Ukazu, withdrew from the Toronto Comic Arts Festival (TCAF) in protest of Moeel's actions, citing the explicit commodification of Black culture as a primary reason [9]. This accusation moved the debate from a simple question of plagiarism to a more complex discussion about cultural sensitivity, exploitation, and the responsibilities of artists who represent marginalized identities. The combination of visual art tracing and cultural appropriation created a perfect storm of community outrage, leading to sustained calls for accountability.

The collective pressure from the comics community culminated in a swift and decisive institutional response from TCAF. On May 31, 2022, the festival announced that it was rescinding its invitation to Saba Moeel to appear as a featured guest [26], [29]. In its official statement, TCAF cited "code of conduct violations and the concerns expressed by the comics community," explicitly mentioning "Moeel's online conduct, plagiarism, or allegations of tracing" as the reasons for its decision [27], [28]. The statement was significant in its candor; TCAF admitted it had been "unaware of Moeel’s online conduct, plagiarism, or allegations of tracing" at the time of the initial invitation and apologized for "programming and promoting this artist" [26], [28]. The festival's Marketing Manager later confirmed via email that the "online conduct" cited included Moeel's past transphobic tweets from 2016 and 2017, as well as recent responses to criticism from the comics community [26]. TCAF affirmed its zero-tolerance policy for racist or transphobic behavior, making it clear that the decision was a reflection of its institutional values [26]. This case represents a critical dimension of the Voiceverse saga, demonstrating that the reputation of a project can be severely damaged not only by the technical misconduct of its creators but also by the deeply problematic behavior of its associated artists. The condemnation from TCAF signaled a growing intolerance within professional communities for artists who engage in appropriation, plagiarism, and discriminatory speech, regardless of their digital presence or popularity. For Voiceverse, the fallout from Moeel's controversies added another layer of stigma, reinforcing the perception that the project was built on a foundation of questionable ethics.

## Legal Ramifications: Intellectual Property and the Rise of AI Misuse Litigation

The repercussions of the Voiceverse NFT Plagiarism Scandal extended far beyond public relations damage, evolving into a significant legal precedent that reshaped the understanding of intellectual property rights in the age of artificial intelligence. The incident, committed by a subsidiary of LOVO, Inc., provided the evidentiary backbone for a subsequent class-action lawsuit filed against the parent company on September 25, 2024 [7], [14]. This lawsuit did not merely seek redress for the 2022 theft but aimed to expose what plaintiffs claimed was a "systemic intellectual property theft" culture within LOVO, Inc. [7], [14]. The legal battle highlighted the complexities of applying traditional copyright law to AI-generated outputs and underscored the power of alternative legal theories, particularly the "right of publicity," in holding corporations accountable for deceptive data acquisition practices. The case against LOVO, Inc. centered on a pattern of fraud that mirrored the tactics used by Voiceverse. Professional voice-over artists Paul Skye Lehrman and Linnea Sage sued the company, alleging that their voices were cloned and sold commercially without their authorization [14], [19]. The artists discovered their voices were being used as the commercial AI voice clones "Kyle Snow" and "Sally Coleman" on LOVO's Genny text-to-speech platform [14], [20]. The method of acquisition was identical to the Voiceverse scandal: in 2019–2020, anonymous LOVO employees had hired Lehrman and Sage on the freelance platform Fiverr, promising that their recordings would be used exclusively for "academic research purposes only" and "test scripts for radio ads" with "no external disclosure" [14], [20]. Instead, LOVO incorporated their recordings into its AI models, trained the system on their unique vocal signatures, and subsequently marketed the resulting clones to subscribers for use in audiobooks, advertisements, and podcasts [20].

The judicial handling of the lawsuit produced a landmark ruling that clarified the boundaries of liability in AI-driven IP disputes. In a pivotal opinion issued on July 10, 2025, Judge J. Paul Oetken of the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York granted in part and denied in part LOVO's motion to dismiss the case [18], [20]. The court's decision had two critical components. First, it dismissed the federal copyright claims related to the AI-generated voice clones themselves. The judge ruled that under current U.S. law, copyright protects fixed sound recordings and the specific expression within them, but it does not protect abstract qualities like a person's voice or an AI's imitation of it [11], [18]. This ruling reinforces the principle that works entirely generated by AI without sufficient human authorship are not copyrightable, a doctrine solidified by cases like *Thaler v. Perlmutter* and *Zarya of the Dawn* [10], [11]. However, the second component of the ruling was far more consequential. The court allowed several state-law claims to proceed, establishing a powerful new avenue for legal challenge [19], [20]. These surviving claims included:
1.  **Breach of Contract:** The court found that LOVO's use of the voice recordings for commercial purposes, in direct contradiction to the promises made on Fiverr, constituted a clear breach of contract [18], [19].
2.  **Right of Publicity (NY Civil Rights Law §§ 50–51):** The court affirmed that the unauthorized commercial use of recognizable voices constitutes a violation of an individual's right to control the commercial use of their name, image, and likeness [17], [20]. This claim was bolstered by legal precedents in jurisdictions like California, Tennessee, and New York, which protect distinctive aesthetic signatures and vocal styles from unauthorized commercial use [10].
3.  **New York Consumer Protection Violations (§§ 349–350):** The deceptive practices used to acquire the training data were deemed actionable under state consumer protection statutes [18], [19].

This judicial outcome represents a paradigm shift in the fight against AI-driven IP infringement. While the AI clone itself may fall outside the purview of federal copyright law, the deceptive methods used to build the very AI model that produces the clone are now subject to legal challenge. The Voiceverse scandal, with its documented pattern of lying to acquire data, provided the crucial link that allowed plaintiffs in the LOVO case to argue that the company's business model was built on a foundation of fraud. By allowing breach-of-contract and right-of-publicity claims to proceed, the courts have given creators and consumers a potent weapon against companies that exploit AI technology through unethical means. The Voiceverse incident, therefore, transcends its status as a singular act of plagiarism; it became the real-world catalyst that enabled future litigation to establish new legal precedents, forcing the industry to confront the ethical and legal obligations that accompany the development and deployment of generative AI.
