# Closures of the University of Wisconsin Branch Campuses

The University of Wisconsin (UW) System has undergone a significant restructuring of its network of two-year branch campuses, leading to a wave of closures, consolidations, and relocations that began in earnest around 2022. This process represents a multi-stage transformation driven by a confluence of long-term demographic shifts, sustained declines in student enrollment, systemic fiscal pressures within the state, and a major administrative reorganization in 2018 [27], [34]. The closures are not isolated incidents but part of a deliberate strategy by the UW System to adapt its infrastructure to meet contemporary educational and economic demands, though this strategy has been met with considerable controversy and local impact [3], [15]. The issue is deeply embedded in Wisconsin's political landscape, with partisan debates centering on the role of state funding, the effectiveness of the 2018 merger, and the future of accessible higher education in rural communities [12], [31]. Each closure has profound consequences for students seeking associate degrees, for the host counties that own the properties, and for the regional economies that rely on these institutions as workforce hubs [10], [15]. As of late 2025, eight branch campuses have been officially designated for closure or significant downsizing, while five remain operational, albeit facing their own enrollment challenges [2], [4]. The vacated campuses present both a financial burden and a potential opportunity for redevelopment, with some counties successfully repurposing the facilities into multi-use community assets [10], [13].

## Chronology of Campus Closures and Status Changes

The recent history of the University of Wisconsin's branch campuses is defined by a clear and accelerating timeline of institutional change, moving from initial consolidation to widespread closures. This progression can be broken down into distinct phases, beginning with the landmark 2018 merger of all 13 two-year campuses into branches under seven comprehensive universities [25], [36]. While intended to improve efficiency, this merger failed to halt a systemic enrollment decline, which subsequently triggered a new wave of closures starting in 2022 [14], [34]. The process involves various outcomes, including permanent cessation of in-person instruction, transition to exclusively online learning, and strategic relocation to partner institutions [1], [11]. A detailed examination of each affected campus reveals the scale and pace of this transformation.

The initial phase of closures began in 2022 and 2023, marking the first definitive action to shutter campuses within this restructured system. The first of these was the UW–Platteville Richland campus, announced for closure in November 2022 and officially ceasing in-person classes after the Spring 2023 semester, with an effective closure date of July 1, 2023 [1], [7], [13]. It is consistently identified as the first campus to close in this wave of events [1], [8]. Following this, a second round of closures was announced in October 2023, affecting three additional campuses. These included the UW–Milwaukee Washington County (West Bend) and the UW–Oshkosh at Fond du Lac, both of which officially ceased operations after the Spring 2024 semester, with closures effective June 2024 [1], [15]. In January 2024, it was announced that the UW–Green Bay at Marinette campus would suspend its in-person classes after the Spring 2024 semester, a move effective June 2024; however, it continues to offer limited online courses and hosts community arts events under its existing lease [1], [6].

The third and fourth waves of closures accelerated through 2024 and 2025, demonstrating the increasing urgency of the situation. In March 2024, the UW–Milwaukee Waukesha branch was announced for closure and was subsequently shuttered at the end of the 2023–24 academic year [1]. In June 2024, two more campuses were targeted: the UW–Oshkosh Fox Cities campus was scheduled to close after the Spring 2025 semester (June 2025), and the UW–Stevens Point at Wausau campus announced plans to relocate rather than close entirely [1], [6]. This relocation to Northcentral Technical College's campus in Wausau for Fall 2026 is a co-location partnership designed to strengthen regional economic alignment and streamline transfer pathways, distinguishing it from a formal closure [11], [32]. The most recent development detailed in the provided sources is the announcement made on December 13, 2025, concerning the UW–Platteville Baraboo Sauk County campus. This campus was slated to permanently cease all in-person operations on May 22, 2026, following an announcement made on that same day [3], [4], [5]. This closure makes it the eighth branch campus to be designated for shutdown or drastic downsizing since 2023 [2], [3].

The variety of responses—from full closure to relocation—also reflects an evolving strategy by the UW System to manage its physical footprint and resources in a challenging environment.

## Root Causes: Enrollment Decline and Financial Pressures

The closures of University of Wisconsin branch campuses are primarily justified by the UW System as a necessary response to severe and sustained declines in student enrollment, a trend that has rendered many campuses financially unsustainable [15], [30]. This enrollment crisis is the direct result of a complex interplay between shifting demographics, increased competition from other educational institutions, and a challenging fiscal environment exacerbated by state-level policy decisions [8], [14]. The system-wide attrition highlights a stark divergence between the growth of comprehensive universities and the precipitous decline of the two-year branch campuses [18].

The most frequently cited driver of the closures is the dramatic drop in student headcount. System-wide, total enrollment across the 13 branch campuses fell by a staggering 22 percent, or 1,092 students, in the fall of 2024 compared to the previous year [18]. This figure stands in sharp contrast to the overall system-wide enrollment increase of 1.2 percent during the same period, illustrating the disproportionate impact on the branch campus model [18]. The individual campus stories paint an even grimmer picture. At UW–Platteville Richland, enrollment plummeted by 83% over a five-year period from 2020 to 2025, falling from over 500 students historically to near-zero in its final semester [17]. Another analysis showed an 84% drop from 567 students in 2014 to just 60 in 2022 following the 2018 merger [34]. Similarly, UW–Stevens Point at Marshfield experienced a nearly 45% decline in enrollment between fall 2023 and fall 2024, reflecting intense systemic pressure [19]. The campus at Wausau saw its numbers drop by 73% between 2015 and 2024, from 978 to 265 students [11]. Projections for the UW–Oshkosh Fox Cities campus were equally dire, predicting it would enroll fewer than 100 students by 2032 [15]. Even the UW–Platteville Baraboo Sauk County campus, which was slated for closure in 2026, saw its fall 2024 enrollment fall to 116-126 students, down from a capacity of 350 and nearly 500 students in 2018 when UW–Platteville assumed oversight [2], [3], [4], [7]. This consistent, system-wide collapse in demand for in-person two-year programs is the central justification for the closures.

This enrollment crisis occurred within a challenging fiscal context for the entire UW System. The institution relies heavily on tuition and fees, which accounted for 58% of its $13.7 billion biennial budget for 2023–25 [12]. Consequently, a reduction in student numbers directly translates to a significant loss of revenue, creating untenable financial situations at campuses that cannot sustain themselves without adequate student populations. The problem was compounded by decades of state funding cuts and tuition freezes. Beginning in 2013, a series of legislative actions froze tuition and cut $66 million from the UW System budget, a move described by experts as effectively defunding the two-year campuses [14]. This was followed by a 14-year freeze on in-state tuition since 2015, which severely constrained the system's ability to generate revenue to offset declining enrollment [17]. The overall ranking of the UW System for public funding declined to 44th out of 50 states in 2024, indicating a persistent challenge in securing adequate state support relative to peers [12].

Furthermore, the 2017-2018 administrative merger of the 13 two-year colleges into branches of four-year universities, while intended to improve efficiency, may have inadvertently accelerated the enrollment decline at some locations [25], [27]. Former UW System President Ray Cross advocated for this merger to expand programming and reduce administrative costs, but sources indicate that collaboration between the newly merged units "didn’t come to fruition" system-wide [17], [34]. At campuses like Richland, the integration led to the loss of dedicated recruitment staff, such as a campus recruiter and an international student program coordinator, which crippled local outreach capabilities post-merger [17]. This suggests that the structural change, rather than solving the enrollment problem, created new challenges that contributed to the downward spiral. The combination of external demographic pressures and internal administrative failures within the post-merger structure created a perfect storm, making the continuation of all branch campuses financially impossible and leading directly to the wave of closures seen in the 2022-2026 period.

## Political and Legislative Context

The closures of the University of Wisconsin branch campuses are deeply entangled in the state's political discourse, serving as a flashpoint in the ongoing debate over public funding for higher education. The issue is framed differently by legislators from opposing parties, with Republicans largely attributing the closures to unavoidable demographic realities and fiscally responsible budgetary decisions, while Democrats point to years of systemic underfunding and a failure of Republican-led legislatures to adequately invest in public institutions [12], [15], [29]. This partisan divide shapes public perception and influences the legislative recommendations being considered to address the crisis.

From a Republican perspective, the closures are presented as a logical consequence of demographic trends and a necessary step to ensure fiscal responsibility. During legislative hearings, Republican lawmakers questioned the need for additional state funding, citing declining birth rates as a primary cause for potential future enrollment drops [12]. They argue that the closures reflect market realities and that the focus should be on managing taxpayer dollars efficiently. Republican-controlled state Legislatures are also noted as having imposed fiscal restraints that contributed to the financial decisions made by the UW System [15]. Assembly Speaker Robin Vos famously dismissed Governor Tony Evers’ proposed $856 million increase for the UW System as a "non-starter," highlighting the deep-seated disagreement over funding levels [12]. This viewpoint frames the closures as an unfortunate but inevitable outcome of broader societal shifts, for which the state is not solely responsible.

In stark contrast, Democratic legislators and advocates attribute the closures directly to a prolonged period of underinvestment in public higher education. State Senator Kelda Roys (D-Madison) explicitly linked the closures to "15 years of underfunding public higher education" and warned that the decisions "shut doors of opportunity for young people" in service of "tax breaks for billionaires" [3], [29]. Similarly, State Senator Sarah Keyeski (D-Lodi) called the closure of the UW–Platteville Baraboo Sauk County campus part of a "trend of two-year colleges closing across our state" and blamed it on a "continuous lack of investment in higher education and in our rural communities" by her Republican colleagues [31]. This narrative positions the closures not as a neutral response to demographics, but as the direct result of policy choices made by the Republican-led legislature, arguing that adequate state funding could have prevented or mitigated the crisis. This perspective emphasizes the social cost of the closures, framing them as a betrayal of access to affordable education for Wisconsin residents.

The political tension extends beyond ideological arguments into concrete legislative actions and vetoes. In one notable instance, a proposal to merge the UW–Milwaukee at Washington County campus with Moraine Park Technical College was put forward and funded in the state budget. However, Governor Tony Evers vetoed the measure, stating that it improperly bypassed the authority of the UW System Board of Regents and reflected a broader legislative failure to provide adequate funding for branch campuses [35]. His veto message underscored his objection to singling out one campus while others faced similar challenges due to inadequate funding, and he asserted that such matters are the purview of the university system's governing board [35]. This event illustrates the power struggle between the executive and legislative branches over the management of state educational institutions. The creation of a bicameral study committee by the Wisconsin Legislature in 2024 to evaluate UW System infrastructure and deliver recommendations further demonstrates the high level of political scrutiny the issue receives [21]. Ultimately, the closures are not merely an administrative decision but a politically charged symbol of the fundamental disagreements over the role, funding, and future of public higher education in Wisconsin.

## Community Impact and Redevelopment

The closure of University of Wisconsin branch campuses creates multifaceted consequences for the host communities, extending far beyond the immediate loss of educational services. A critical distinction is that these campuses are typically owned by the host counties, not the state, which places a direct and often substantial financial burden on local governments [8]. This reality forces counties to grapple with the costs of maintaining abandoned buildings, navigating environmental remediation, and planning for the future use of valuable real estate. At the same time, these vacated properties represent a significant opportunity for redevelopment, with some counties successfully transforming them into vibrant community assets that serve new economic and social functions [10].

The financial liabilities placed upon host counties are a primary concern. After the UW–Platteville Richland campus closed, Richland County officials described the site as being in disrepair, with coffee cups, pens, and plates left behind, and estimated that bringing the buildings to a minimum usable condition would cost $30 million [13]. The county now faces ongoing monthly utility bills of $10,000–$12,000 for the vacant structures, with no fiscally responsible path identified to preserve them [13]. Similarly, Sauk County, home to the soon-to-be-closed UW–Platteville Baraboo Sauk County campus, assumed $390,000 in operational responsibilities for the property in 2025, including tasks like snow-shoveling and lawn-mowing [2], [5]. This shift of responsibility from the state-supported university to the local taxpayer creates a significant long-term drain on county budgets, forcing difficult choices between investing in the former campus or other essential services.

However, the story of community impact is not solely one of financial strain. The redevelopment of these campuses offers a pathway to mitigate losses and create new value. The Fond du Lac County redevelopment of the former UW–Oshkosh Fond du Lac campus serves as a powerful case study in proactive and successful repurposing [10]. Using a combination of federal funding, a state grant, and existing revenues, the county transformed the property into a multi-use facility. This includes a 400-seat event center, a 340-seat Prairie Theater for cultural performances, space for law enforcement training, county offices, and a full basketball gym [10]. The project is projected to generate $600,000–$700,000 in annual revenue once fully operational and is expected to create new economic opportunities and cultural attractions for the region, directly addressing local concerns about a lack of engagement for younger residents [10]. This approach demonstrates how a community can turn a liability into an asset.

Other counties have pursued different strategies. Marinette County, where the UW–Green Bay Marinette campus ended its in-person operations, sold 18.6 acres of the campus fieldhouse to the Greater Marinette-Menominee YMCA for $1 [1]. The county allocated $500,000 of its $2 million state grant for closed UW campuses toward improvements for the new owner, showcasing a collaborative approach to redevelopment [1]. Marathon County, which owns the UW–Stevens Point at Wausau campus, is beginning to plan its future use by early 2026, recognizing the importance of finding a productive next chapter for the facility [11]. The overarching challenge for all host communities is to balance the immense cost of maintaining these large properties with the need to find sustainable, economically viable uses that honor the legacy of the campuses while meeting the evolving needs of the region. The varying approaches highlight the diverse ways in which communities are responding to the profound and lasting impact of these closures.

## The Broader Higher Education Landscape

The closures of the University of Wisconsin's branch campuses are not an isolated phenomenon but part of a larger national conversation about the future of two-year colleges in the United States. These institutions have historically served as a crucial gateway to higher education, particularly for students from underserved and rural communities, offering accessible and affordable pathways to associate degrees and transfer to four-year universities [28]. The challenges facing the UW System mirror a broader trend of declining enrollment and financial instability affecting many community colleges across the country, which are grappling with shifting demographics, increased competition, and a changing job market [8]. The UW System's response—consolidation and closure—represents one of several possible strategies being debated nationwide.

One of the core issues is the intense competition from other educational providers, particularly technical colleges and universities. The proliferation of online degree programs has further complicated the landscape, allowing four-year universities to compete directly for students who might have previously enrolled in a traditional two-year college. This dynamic has contributed to the decline in two-year enrollment nationally, which fell by over 17% in the same period that the UW system was experiencing its own struggles [17]. The UW System's own 2018 merger, which integrated two-year campuses into comprehensive universities, was partly motivated by a desire to expand programming and better compete for students [17]. However, as some evidence suggests, this strategy did not universally succeed in reversing enrollment declines and may have created new administrative hurdles [34]. The question of how best to structure the relationship between liberal arts-focused universities and career-and-technical education remains a contentious issue within the state, with former UW System President Ray Cross advocating for a unified system but deeming legislative action unlikely without strong political will [17].

The financial models of these institutions are also under strain. The heavy reliance on tuition revenue makes them vulnerable to enrollment fluctuations, a fact that became starkly apparent in the UW System [12]. Furthermore, the high cost of maintaining physical campuses, especially in areas with low population density, presents a significant barrier to sustainability. This has led to discussions about transitioning more programs to online delivery, a strategy being implemented at campuses like Marinette [1]. This shift reflects a broader national trend towards hybrid and online learning, but it also raises questions about equity and access for students who may require in-person support services, hands-on training, or reliable internet access that is not always available in rural areas.

Looking ahead, the future of the two-year college model in Wisconsin appears uncertain. While five branch campuses remain open, they all face declining enrollment trends, suggesting the pressures are systemic and ongoing [4]. The state's ranking for public funding for higher education at 44th nationally underscores the chronic under-resourcing that contributes to these challenges [12]. In response to the destabilizing effects of the closures and consolidations, the 2025–27 Biennial Budget includes over $250 million for the UW System, with a portion specifically allocated to help stabilize the system and support campuses statewide [23]. This funding aims to address immediate fallout from the closures, such as layoffs and program cuts, and to ensure that the remaining UW institutions continue to function as vital economic and workforce hubs across the state [23]. However, whether this injection of funds is sufficient to reverse the long-term demographic and financial headwinds remains a critical unanswered question.

## Current Status and Future Outlook

As of late 2025, the University of Wisconsin System has significantly reduced its network of two-year branch campuses, with eight locations either permanently closed, downsized to online-only instruction, or relocated to partner institutions since 2023 [2], [3]. Five campuses remain fully operational as of the latest available information, continuing to serve students in their respective regions [4], [32]. However, the long-term stability of this remaining cohort is uncertain, as several of these campuses are also contending with declining enrollment, indicating that the systemic pressures that drove the recent wave of closures are not yet resolved [4]. The future of the two-year college model within the UW System will likely depend on its ability to adapt to demographic shifts, secure stable state funding, and redefine its role in the evolving higher education landscape.

The five campuses that remain open are located in Barron, Manitowoc, Sheboygan, Marshfield, and Rock Counties [4], [32]. Of these, only UW-Eau Claire Barron County and UW-Whitewater Rock County are projected to see slight enrollment increases in the coming years, according to budget documents released in June 2024 [6]. In contrast, the remaining three—UW-Green Bay Manitowoc, UW-Green Bay Sheboygan, and UW-Stevens Point Marshfield—are all projected to experience steady or modest declines in full-time equivalent (FTE) student numbers [6]. UW-Stevens Point at Marshfield, in particular, has already demonstrated a dramatic enrollment drop of nearly 45% between fall 2023 and fall 2024, making it one of the most vulnerable of the remaining campuses [19]. This data suggests that while the most acute crisis may have passed with the closures of the least viable campuses, the underlying financial and demographic challenges persist and threaten the viability of even the surviving branch campuses.

The fate of the closed campuses is varied, reflecting the differing capacities and priorities of the host counties. Some counties have struggled with the immense financial burden of maintaining abandoned properties. Richland County, for example, faces millions in potential renovation costs and thousands of dollars per month in utilities for the former UW–Platteville Richland site, with no clear path forward for preservation [13]. In other cases, proactive redevelopment has turned liabilities into assets. Fond du Lac County’s successful transformation of the former UW-Oshkosh Fond du Lac campus into a profitable multi-use facility generating revenue is a prime example of a positive outcome [10]. Other counties, like Marinette, have opted for a sale-and-repurpose strategy, transferring ownership of parts of the campus to organizations like the YMCA [1]. The future of these properties will continue to be a focal point for local governance, with the state providing a pool of up to $20 million in redevelopment grants to assist counties in finding new uses for the land and buildings [8].

In conclusion, the closures of the University of Wisconsin branch campuses represent a pivotal moment in the state's public higher education system. Driven by a perfect storm of demographic decline, systemic underfunding, and the unintended consequences of a major administrative merger, the process has reshaped the landscape of accessible education in Wisconsin. While the immediate crisis has led to the consolidation of the system's physical footprint, the long-term challenges of securing funding, ensuring equitable access, and adapting to a changing world remain unresolved. The actions taken by the remaining campuses and their host counties in the coming years will be crucial in determining the ultimate trajectory of the two-year college model in the state.
