# Skiddaw Group Site of Special Scientific Interest

## Location, Extent, and Governance

The Skiddaw Group Site of Special Scientific Interest (SSSI) is a legally protected area in the northern English Lake District, recognized for its profound national importance in both geology and ecology [2], [3]. A Site of Special Scientific Interest is a statutory designation established under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 in England and Wales, designed to protect land that possesses features of special interest due to its wildlife, geology, or landform [[1], [12], [19]-in-england-and-wales.aspx)]. This designation imposes a legal obligation on Natural England, the governing body, to ensure the site's special features are maintained or enhanced, and it places responsibilities on landowners and operators to seek consent for certain activities that could damage the site's scientific value [1], [12]. The Skiddaw Group SSSI is not merely a collection of rocks but an entire landscape encompassing extensive high ground, diverse ecosystems, and a rich history of geological processes that shaped the region [3], [18]. Its governance is managed by Natural England, the principal nature conservation body for England, which is responsible for all aspects of the site from notification to monitoring [5], [15].

The site is situated in the Keswick district of Cumbria, forming the smooth-sided mountains of the north of the Lake District National Park [18]. It is centred near Great Calva, with its geographical coordinates specified as 54°40′08″N 3°06′07″W / 54.669°N 3.102°W [3]. The SSSI covers a substantial area of 10,256.3 hectares, which is equivalent to approximately 39.600 square miles [3]. This vast expanse includes the prominent Skiddaw Forest nature reserve, highlighting its significance as a key component of the regional landscape [3]. The site's location places it at a critical hydrological crossroads, where its high ground acts as a watershed dividing catchments [3]. Water flows northwards into the Caldew Operational Catchment, which drains towards Carlisle, while water to the west flows into the Ellen and West Coast operational catchments, ultimately reaching Workington and Maryport [3]. This role in shaping the local drainage patterns is a fundamental aspect of its landform-based scientific interest. The entire area is classified under the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) category IV, which pertains to habitat/species management areas, underscoring the dual focus on protecting both geological structures and the biological communities they support [3].

The legal framework for the Skiddaw Group SSSI is rooted in the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, which provides the mechanism for designating sites of national importance [1], [12]. The process begins with Natural England identifying an area that exhibits features of special scientific interest. Once designated, the site is formally notified through a 'notification' letter to the relevant landowners and occupiers [1], [12]. This document is a crucial piece of statutory paperwork; it officially establishes the site's boundaries, details the specific reasons for its designation (the notified features), outlines Natural England's views on appropriate management, specifies a list of operations requiring prior consent (known as ORNEC - Operations Requiring Notification and Consent), and sets out the legal responsibilities of those in control of the land [1], [12]. For the Skiddaw Group SSSI, the notified features are explicitly stated as its flora and fauna, as well as its geology [3]. This dual notification reflects the integrated approach to conservation on the site, where the geological foundation directly supports the unique ecological communities. All authoritative information regarding the Skiddaw Group SSSI, including its official citation sheet (designated as Unit 3838), detailed boundary maps, and conservation objectives, is held and disseminated by Natural England [15], [22]. Official datasets containing the precise polygon definitions of the site's boundaries are available for download in various geospatial formats, such as GeoJSON, Shapefile, and GeoPackage, through platforms like the Defra Data Services Platform and the Natural England Open Data Geoportal [22], [27]. These datasets are licensed under the Open Government Licence, making them freely accessible for use by planners, developers, researchers, and the public [16], [22]. Interactive mapping tools, such as Defra’s Magic Map application, also provide access to this data, allowing users to visualize the site's boundaries alongside other environmental features [16]. The official boundary dataset was last updated on October 9, 2025, ensuring that the most current spatial information is available for management and planning purposes [22]. The existence of this authoritative, publicly accessible data is paramount for ensuring that development proposals and other activities are assessed against the full extent of the site's protected status [16].

## Geological Composition and Stratigraphy

The Skiddaw Group SSSI is named after and primarily protects the Skiddaw Group itself, which is a Lower Palaeozoic sedimentary sequence deposited during the early to mid-Ordovician period [2], [5]. This rock succession represents the oldest recorded unit in the Keswick district, with deposition occurring in a deep marine environment along the Avalonian margin of the ancient Iapetus Ocean [2], [20]. The age of the formation spans from the Tremadoc Series (O1) to the Llanvirn Series (OE), corresponding to a time interval of approximately 485 to 470 million years ago [6], [14]. The group consists predominantly of turbidite deposits, which are sediments laid down by underwater gravity flows known as turbidity currents [2], [21]. These deposits are composed mainly of mudstone and siltstone, often interbedded with sandstone beds that form greywackes [2], [14]. The overall thickness of the Skiddaw Slates succession is immense, reaching up to 7,500 feet (approximately 2,286 meters) in some areas, though local variations exist, with thicknesses of less than 5000m noted in certain inliers [10], [11]. This thick sequence of marine-origin rocks forms the bedrock of the northern Lake District's characteristic smooth-sided mountains [18].

A defining feature of the Skiddaw Group is its internal structure, which is divided into two distinct stratigraphical belts separated by the major east-north-east-trending Causey Pike Fault [6], [14]. The belt to the south of this fault is known as the Northern Fells Belt, while the area to the north is the Central Fells Belt [6], [14]. Each belt contains a different set of rock formations, reflecting variations in depositional environments and later tectonic activity. The Northern Fells Belt is more complex, comprising five distinct formations in ascending order: the Bitter Beck, Watch Hill, Hope Beck, Loweswater, and Kirk Stile formations [5], [21], [26]. The Bitter Beck Formation is the oldest, consisting of dark grey mudstone with sporadic thin sandstone beds, representing low-density turbidite deposits from the upper Tremadoc period [14]. Overlying it is the Watch Hill Formation, characterized by lithic wacke sandstones, and the Hope Beck Formation, which marks the transition from the Tremadoc to the Arenig period [14], [26]. The Kirk Stile Formation is the thickest, estimated at between 1500 and 2500 meters, and consists mainly of laminated siltstone and mudstone interpreted as very low-density turbidite deposits [14], [26].

In contrast, the Central Fells Belt, located to the north of the Causey Pike Fault, is dominated by two formations: the Buttermere Formation and the overlying Tarn Moor Formation [6], [21]. The Buttermere Formation is a remarkable geological deposit known as an olistostrome—a chaotic mixture of large clasts of older rock embedded in a finer-grained matrix, moved downslope by massive gravity flows [5], [26]. This formation is at least 1500 meters thick and contains clasts up to 1 kilometer across [14], [26]. Its emplacement occurred in the late Arenig period, at the boundary between the gibberulus and cucullus biozones, marking a significant event in the region's geological history [5], [26]. Unconformably overlain by the Buttermere Formation is the Tarn Moor Formation, which is 1000 to 1500 meters thick and extends from the late Arenig into the early Llanvirn [26]. This formation is distinguished by the presence of volcaniclastic turbidite sandstone beds and bentonitic claystone, indicating contemporaneous volcanism during its deposition [26]. The Tarn Moor Formation is considered stratigraphically equivalent to the Kirkland Formation in the Cross Fell inlier to the north [26]. The Skiddaw Group is unconformably overlain by younger volcanic successions, such as the Eycott Volcanic Group and the Borrowdale Volcanic Group, which were deposited during a subsequent phase of subduction-related volcanic activity [11], [20].

The immense scientific value of the Skiddaw Group lies in its exceptionally well-preserved and diverse fossil content, which provides a powerful tool for biostratigraphy—the science of using fossils to date and correlate rock layers. The site is internationally renowned for its graptolite faunas, which are extinct colonial marine organisms whose remains are abundant in the mudstones and shales [23], [24]. These fossils allow for the subdivision of the Ordovician rocks into a series of distinct biozones, each defined by the first appearance of a particular graptolite species [5], [26]. For example, the Bitter Beck Formation is correlated with the Araneograptus murrayi Biozone, the Watch Hill Formation with the latest Tremadoc murrayi Biozone, and the Kirk Stile Formation spans from the Didymograptus simulans to the D. artus biozones [5], [26]. The Skiddaw Group serves as the primary biostratigraphical standard for graptolitic facies in England and Wales and for the Arenig Series on the western margin of the supercontinent Gondwana, meaning its rock sequences can be matched precisely with those in distant locations like Scandinavia [2], [24]. This level of international correlation is a cornerstone of its designation. In addition to graptolites, the Buttermere Formation also contains a rich assemblage of acritarchs—microscopic organic-walled fossils—which further refine the stratigraphic framework [5], [21]. This combination of microfossils has allowed geologists to build a detailed timeline of Earth history for this critical period, making the Skiddaw Group an invaluable resource for understanding Ordovician palaeogeography and evolution [2].

## Structural Geology and Tectonic History

Beyond its stratigraphic significance, the Skiddaw Group SSSI is of paramount importance for understanding the large-scale tectonic processes that shaped the British Isles. The rocks of the Skiddaw Group are not pristine and undisturbed; they bear the scars of multiple phases of deformation that record a complex history of mountain-building events associated with the closure of the Iapetus Ocean and the construction of the Caledonian-Acadian orogenic belt [2], [20]. This makes the site a textbook example for studying the dynamics of accretionary prisms and thrust tectonics. The structural complexity is evident in the varied folding, faulting, and foliation observed throughout the sequence. Four distinct phases of deformation (F1–F4) have been identified in the Skiddaw Slates succession, each reflecting a different stage of compressional stress [10]. Phase F1 involved the formation of pre-Borrowdale Volcanic, north-south striking overturned folds without a cleavage, while later phases (F2, F3, and F4) were driven by end-Silurian compression, resulting in open folds with different orientations and the development of crenulation cleavages [10]. The most significant structural event documented in the Skiddaw Group is the Acadian (late Caledonian) deformation, which produced a dominant northeast- to east-trending regional cleavage that is axial planar to large-scale folds [20]. This event was accompanied by southward-directed thrusts, which caused the overthrusting of the Skiddaw Group strata over younger rock units, a classic feature of continental collision zones [20]. The Causey Pike Fault, which separates the Northern and Central Fells Belts, is itself a major structure exhibiting south-directed thrusting and sinistral strike-slip shear, demonstrating the intense shearing forces at play [14].

One of the most dramatic features preserved in the Skiddaw Group is the olistostrome nature of the Buttermere Formation [5], [26]. An olistostrome is a mass transport deposit characterized by a chaotic jumble of large blocks of rock (olistoliths) of varying sizes and ages, embedded within a finer-grained matrix [5]. In the case of the Buttermere Formation, these clasts can be up to 1 kilometer across and contain fragments ranging from Tremadoc to late Arenig age, juxtaposed together within metres of each other [5], [26]. This indicates a catastrophic event involving the collapse and rapid downslope movement of a vast portion of the continental slope, likely triggered by seismic activity associated with the ongoing plate convergence [14]. The emplacement of this giant olistostrome in the late Arenig period, at the boundary between the gibberulus and cucullus graptolite biozones, is a pivotal moment in the geological history of the Lake District, marking a shift from relatively quiet marine sedimentation to a highly dynamic tectonic regime [5], [26]. The widespread synsedimentary slump folding observed in formations like the Kirk Stile Formation further attests to the unstable and rapidly subsiding nature of the depositional basin during this time [14].

The tectonic story recorded in the Skiddaw Group is intrinsically linked to the broader evolution of the Iapetus Ocean. The Skiddaw Group was deposited on the passive margin of the continent of Avalonia, which was drifting northward away from Gondwana [2], [20]. As Avalonia approached Laurentia (the precursor to North America), the Iapetus Ocean began to close, initiating a long and protracted period of continental collision [2]. The deformation events seen in the Skiddaw Group are direct evidence of this process. Regional uplift and tilting occurred in mid- to late Ordovician times, likely driven by melt generation from the subducting oceanic crust beneath Avalonia [20]. The subsequent Acadian deformation in the late Silurian and early Devonian represents the culmination of this collision, when Avalonia finally docked with Laurentia to form the supercontinent Pangaea [20]. The structural contrasts between the Skiddaw Group and the younger, more intensely deformed rocks of the Lake District inlier are attributed to differences in their rheological properties (how rocks deform) and possible impedance to thrusting by intervening bodies like the Borrowdale Volcanic Group and the Lake District batholith [20]. Thus, the Skiddaw Group acts as a crucial window into the deep-time processes that created the Caledonian mountain chain, providing critical evidence for reconstructing the palaeogeography of the British Isles [2]. Its exposed, fossiliferous, and structurally significant Ordovician sequence makes it a nationally important Earth science teaching site, where students and researchers can observe firsthand the effects of plate tectonics on a sedimentary succession [5]. The site's inclusion in the Geological Conservation Review series further underscores its status as a premier location for the study of Caledonian geology [5].

## Ecological Significance and Habitats

While the Skiddaw Group SSSI is principally designated for its outstanding geological features, it also holds significant ecological importance, supporting a range of habitats and species typical of the upland fells of the Lake District [3], [18]. The relationship between the site's geology and its ecology is fundamental; the underlying rock composition dictates soil types, drainage patterns, and physical structure, which in turn create the conditions necessary for these specialized habitats to thrive [18]. The high ground of the Skiddaw Group forms a vital part of the region's moorland ecosystem, characterized by extensive areas of blanket bog and montane heaths and grasslands [18]. These habitats are themselves of national importance and are a key reason for the site's designation. Blanket bog, in particular, occurs extensively on the Skiddaw, Shap, and Armboth fells, covering large tracts of peaty soil that have accumulated in cool, wet, and acidic conditions [18]. Montane heaths and grasslands are found on the massif, typically on drier, more exposed slopes, supporting a different suite of plant life adapted to harsh weather and shallow soils [18]. Another important habitat within the SSSI is juniper scrub, a rare and threatened community dominated by the native Juniperus communis [13]. These shrublands often occur in sheltered locations and contribute significantly to the site's biodiversity.

The cliffs, rock outcrops, and scree slopes provided by the exposed geology of the Skiddaw Group create a mosaic of microhabitats that are crucial for certain plant species, including arctic-alpine plants [18]. These are species that are relics from the last Ice Age, now surviving in isolated pockets on the highest, coldest parts of the landscape. The Skiddaw Group supports populations of these hardy plants, which are adapted to survive in extreme conditions of cold, wind, and limited nutrients [18]. Among the most notable is the endemic Borrowdale Hawkweed (Hieracium bordeadaleense), a plant found only in this specific locality, highlighting the site's unique contribution to the nation's botanical heritage [18]. The moorland habitats, particularly the heather and moss-covered expanses, are equally important, serving as critical breeding grounds for a variety of upland bird species [13], [18]. The site is home to large populations of curlew, ring ouzel, and merlin, all of which are dependent on the open, treeless character of the high fells for nesting and foraging [18]. Some of these bird populations are currently considered to be in decline across the UK, making the Skiddaw Group an important stronghold for their conservation [18].

The conservation of these ecological features is a central component of the site's management. The presence of important habitats such as heather and moss moorland, blanket bog, and juniper scrub is explicitly noted in the site's designation [13]. Management activities are focused on maintaining these habitats in a favourable condition through sensitive control of factors like grazing levels, scrub encroachment, and water table levels [[19]-in-england-and-wales.aspx)]. For instance, the annual surveying of upland breeding birds is a key monitoring activity that helps track the health of these populations and informs management decisions [13]. An innovative example of active habitat recovery is the establishment of three fenced enclosures totalling 36.2 hectares on Caldbeck Common within the SSSI in October 2024 [13]. This five-year experimental project, led by the Lake District National Park Authority, the Caldbeck Commoners’ Association, and Natural England, aims to restore degraded habitat by excluding grazing animals, demonstrating a proactive approach to ecological enhancement [13]. The fencing was granted a temporary exemption from the normal requirements of the Commons Act 2006 because the enclosures occupy less than 1% of the registered common land and serve a clear conservation objective [13]. This integration of ecological protection with geological conservation underscores the holistic approach taken for the Skiddaw Group SSSI, recognizing that the landscape's value is derived from the interplay between its solid geology and its living ecosystems.

## Conservation Status and Management

The Skiddaw Group SSSI is subject to a rigorous system of conservation management and condition assessment administered by Natural England, in line with the provisions of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 [1], [12]. The primary goal of this system is to maintain or improve the site's special features—its nationally important geology and its unique ecological habitats—in a "favourable condition" [[19]-in-england-and-wales.aspx)]. To achieve this, Natural England develops a Monitoring Specification (MS) for each SSSI, which sets out mandatory targets for the condition of its notified features [1], [12]. Land managers and owners are then expected to implement practices that help meet these targets. The condition of the site's features is formally assessed at least once every six years, although this frequency may be higher for rapidly changing features like grasslands and lower for slow-changing ones like woodlands [1], [12]. The results of these assessments are categorized using a standardized set of criteria to provide a consistent measure of the site's health across the country [[19]-in-england-and-wales.aspx)]. The condition categories are: Favourable, Unfavourable recovering, Unfavourable no change, Unfavourable declining, Part destroyed, and Destroyed [[1], [12], [19]-in-england-and-wales.aspx)].

An important recent development in the management of SSSIs is the implementation of Whole Feature Assessment by Natural England in April 2025 [17]. This new methodology replaces the previous unit-based approach, which assessed the condition of small, discrete patches of habitat or geology. Instead, Whole Feature Assessment evaluates the condition of a feature across the entire site, providing a more holistic view of its health and better reflecting the landscape-scale pressures and interactions that affect it [17]. This shift acknowledges that ecological and geological systems operate at a landscape scale and that management needs to consider these broader contexts. The UK Government’s Environmental Improvement Plan (EIP) has set ambitious targets for the condition of SSSIs, aiming for all features to have up-to-date condition assessments by January 2028 and for actions to be on track to achieve a favourable condition for 50% of all SSSI features by that date. The long-term target is for 75% of SSSI features to be in a favourable condition by 2042 [17]. While the specific condition assessment report for the Skiddaw Group SSSI is not detailed in the provided sources, the principles of this modern management framework apply to its governance.

Furthermore, the Skiddaw Group SSSI is not only an SSSI but also forms part of the larger Lake District High Fells Special Area of Conservation (SAC) [4]. A Special Area of Conservation is a site designated under the European Union's Habitats Directive (now retained in UK law) to protect Europe's most valuable and threatened habitats and species [4]. This dual designation means that the site operates under a combined legal framework, integrating the objectives of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 with those of the EU-derived Habitats Regulations. This often leads to more stringent management controls and a focus on conserving specific habitat types and species listed in the SAC designation. The licence conditions associated with the SAC designation for the Skiddaw Group SSSI illustrate this heightened level of protection. For example, all shooting activities are prohibited from March 1st to August 31st to avoid disturbing birds during the breeding season [4]. Additionally, vehicle access is strictly restricted to existing tracks currently in use by vehicular traffic, and any trapping is limited to existing sett footprints, minimizing disturbance to wildlife and preventing damage to sensitive habitats [4]. This demonstrates how the site's statutory status translates into concrete, on-the-ground management rules designed to safeguard its conservation values.

## Associated Features and Research Importance

The Skiddaw Group SSSI designation extends beyond the primary rock succession to include other significant features that contribute to its overall scientific and historical value. One such feature is the former mining area of the Caldbeck Fells, which is incorporated into the SSSI [3]. This highlights the site's importance not only in terms of natural geology but also in the context of human industrial history. The geological formations of the Skiddaw Group contain veins of minerals that were historically exploited, and remnants of this past activity are now part of the site's cultural and geological tapestry. The inclusion of these areas in the SSSI ensures that this aspect of the landscape's heritage is also protected and studied. The site also encompasses several areas that have been recognized for their particular geological importance, leading to their designation as Geological Conservation Review (GCR) sites [3]. The GCR is a program that identifies and promotes the best examples of geological and geomorphological features in the UK for their scientific importance. Across the Skiddaw Group SSSI, there are 42 documented GCR sites spread across 12 different geological units, each contributing to a deeper understanding of the region's history [3]. These GCR sites cover a wide range of topics, from the mineralogy of the Lake District and the igneous rocks of the Ordovician period to the Caledonian structures and the Quaternary deposits that post-date the main mountain-building events [3]. Specific examples cited include Carrock Fell for its Ordovician igneous rocks, Burdell Gill for its mineralogical significance, and Raven Crags for its Caledonian structures, among many others [3]. This concentration of GCR sites within a single SSSI underscores the site's exceptional status as a repository of geological knowledge.

The research importance of the Skiddaw Group is immense, cementing its reputation as one of England's most important early Palaeozoic stratigraphical units [2]. For decades, it has served as a fundamental field area for university-level Earth science teaching and advanced research [5]. Its well-exposed, fossiliferous, and structurally complex sequence provides an unparalleled opportunity for students and scientists to study the depositional processes of an ancient deep-marine basin, the evolution of graptolite faunas during the Ordovician period, and the mechanics of continental collision and mountain building [2], [5]. The site's role as a biostratigraphical standard for graptolitic facies in England and Wales means that it is a benchmark against which other Ordovician successions are compared and correlated [2]. This international relevance is further demonstrated by the fact that its graptolite faunas can be correlated with those in Scandinavia, linking the geological history of the Lake District to the wider story of the Caledonian orogeny [24]. Peer-reviewed publications, such as those from the Proceedings of the Yorkshire Geological Society and the Memoirs of the Geological Survey of Great Britain, consistently cite the Skiddaw Group as a key reference section for understanding Ordovician stratigraphy, graptolite evolution, and tectonic processes [5], [11], [23]. The wealth of academic literature generated from research conducted in the area attests to its enduring value as a living laboratory for geologists.

In summary, the Skiddaw Group Site of Special Scientific Interest is far more than a collection of protected rocks; it is a cornerstone of British geological science and a vital component of the Lake District's natural heritage. Its designation is justified by a compelling combination of factors. Geologically, it is a world-class exposure of Lower Palaeozoic sedimentary rocks, providing critical evidence for understanding the destruction of the Iapetus Ocean and the construction of the Caledonian mountain belt [2], [20]. Its exceptionally well-preserved graptolite and acritarch faunas offer a precise chronological framework for the early Ordovician period, making it an internationally recognized stratigraphic standard [2], [23]. Ecologically, the underlying geology supports unique and important habitats, including extensive blanket bog and montane heath, which in turn provide refuge for characteristic upland flora and fauna, including rare and endemic species [13], [18]. Governed by a robust legal framework that combines the protections of an SSSI with the more stringent requirements of a Special Area of Conservation, the site is managed to preserve its scientific integrity [[4], [19]-in-england-and-wales.aspx)]. With its numerous Geological Conservation Review sites and its long-standing role as a premier field area for education and research, the Skiddaw Group SSSI stands as a testament to the deep, interconnected history of our planet's geology and biology, a treasure of national importance that demands careful stewardship and continued scientific inquiry [3], [5].
