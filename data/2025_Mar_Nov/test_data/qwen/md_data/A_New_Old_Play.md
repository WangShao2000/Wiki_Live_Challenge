# A New Old Play

*A New Old Play* (Chinese title: 椒麻堂會; Hanyu Pinyin: Jiāomá tánghuì) is a 2021 epic drama directed by Chinese contemporary artist and filmmaker Qiu Jiongjiong [1], [6]. It represents his first work of fiction and serves as a culmination of his prior experimental documentary practice, blending personal memoir with sweeping historical panorama [21], [23]. The film is deeply rooted in the director's familial history, specifically chronicling the life of his grandfather, Qiu Fu-xin, a celebrated Sichuan opera clown actor who lived through some of the most tumultuous decades of 20th-century China [6], [7]. Set against a backdrop of civil war, foreign invasion, political revolution, and social upheaval, the film traces the fortunes of a fictionalized Sichuan opera troupe across more than fifty years [7], [24]. Its most distinctive characteristic is its radical departure from cinematic naturalism; instead, it employs a highly stylized, deliberately artificial aesthetic inspired by traditional Chinese theatre and painting, using handcrafted dioramic sets, theatrical props, and non-linear storytelling to evoke an archetypal sense of time and memory [5], [6]. Upon its world premiere at the Locarno Film Festival in 2021, the film garnered significant international acclaim, winning the Special Jury Prize and establishing Qiu Jiongjiong as a major voice in independent cinema [1], [3]. It went on to win further accolades at prestigious festivals like the Hong Kong International Film Festival, where it received both the Firebird Award for Best Film and the FIPRESCI Prize [14], [16]. Despite this success, the film has remained absent from public screenings in mainland China, having never passed the country's state censorship apparatus, a fact that profoundly shapes its cultural significance [49], [60]. Distributed primarily within niche arthouse channels in North America and made available on curated streaming platforms, *A New Old Play* stands as a powerful, poetic, and politically resonant memorial to a lost era and a resilient artistic spirit [50], [53].

## Origins and Artistic Vision

The genesis of *A New Old Play* is intrinsically tied to the personal history of its director, Qiu Jiongjiong, and his deep-seated connection to the traditions of Sichuan opera [21], [36]. Born in 1977 in Leshan, Sichuan province, Qiu Jiongjiong grew up immersed in the local culture, first performing Sichuan opera at the age of three and being introduced to painting by his grandfather at two [38], [54]. This early exposure formed the foundation of his artistic sensibilities, shaping his understanding of humor, performance, and visual composition [37]. His grandfather, Qiu Fu-xin (also referred to as Qiu Fuxin), was a renowned Sichuan opera "clown" (*xiaochou*) performer with the Xinyouxin Opera Troupe, and his life story became the direct inspiration for the film's protagonist, Qiu Fu [23], [36]. Qiu had an exceptionally close relationship with his grandfather during his first decade of life, who served as his foundational artistic influence across multiple disciplines, including painting, choreography, music, and acting [37]. The film was conceived in 2017, marking the 30th anniversary of his grandfather's death, after Qiu illustrated his father's biographical account of him and conducted extensive historical research to reconstruct the life of an "ordinary man embodying the spirit of a clown" [37]. This process was not merely an act of filmmaking but a deliberate act of memorialization and reclamation, aiming to preserve the fading legacy of a tradition and a family member [36].

Before directing *A New Old Play*, Qiu Jiongjiong was primarily known as a contemporary visual artist and painter who transitioned into filmmaking in 2006 without any formal cinematic training, beginning his career with a mini-DV camera [21], [23], [38]. He left school at eighteen to pursue art full-time and built a reputation for his experimental and often politically charged documentaries [19], [54]. His prior work, which includes films like *Mr. Zhang Believes* (2015), established him as one of China’s most important independent directors, particularly within the international art-house circuit [3], [29]. These earlier documentaries, such as *Ode to Joy* (2008)—a short film about his grandfather—and the "chatterbox" trilogy (*Portrait of Mr. Huang*, *Madame*, *Mr. Zhang Believes*), reflect a consistent thematic interest in exploring personal and collective memory through intimate portraits and unconventional narrative techniques [23], [33]. *A New Old Play* is thus positioned as the summation of this body of work, synthesizing the stylistic concerns and thematic preoccupations of his six earlier documentaries into a single, ambitious feature-length project [23]. The film integrates recurring motifs from his previous films, including rapid multilayered editing, decoupled sound/image, and visual themes of birds, rivers, and landscapes, while also drawing heavily on traditional Sichuan opera soundscapes to evoke China’s recent history [23].

The film's title, *Jiāomá tánghuì*, translates literally to "The Gathering and Celebration of Peppery and Chili Flavors," a reference to a classic Sichuan culinary taste [37]. Metaphorically, the phrase evokes the vibrant, contradictory, and complex character of the Sichuan dialect itself, which is described as a product of centuries of migration and cultural fusion following the late Ming dynasty massacres [37]. This linguistic complexity—marked by "vivacity, vulgarity, voracity, and something indistinguishable between ecstasy and melancholy"—serves as a fitting metaphor for the film's own tone and content [37]. By choosing to perform the entire film in authentic Sichuanese dialect, Qiu Jiongjiong grounds the epic narrative in a specific cultural and geographical identity, demonstrating a commitment to authenticity that was influenced by the 1962 Sichuan-dialect film *Conscription*, which he felt proved the dialect's capacity for "homely, authentic, and vibrant" expression even when used for didactic purposes [37]. The choice of language is therefore a core component of the film's artistic vision, reinforcing its roots in a particular regional tradition while telling a story that resonates on a national level. The film is explicitly based on the life of the director's grandfather, which infuses it with an "intimate, reverent, and unvarnished historical observation" [26]. While dreamlike in its execution, the film presents itself as summarily true—a conglomeration of many similar stories from that era—rendering its portrayal of historical events authentically resonant [26].

## Narrative and Thematic Structure

The narrative of *A New Old Play* is structured as a picaresque and fantastical depiction of five decades of Chinese history, framed by the death of its protagonist and his subsequent journey through the afterlife [6], [30]. The story begins with the death of Qiu Fu, a renowned clown-role actor in 20th-century Sichuan opera, in the 1980s [17]. As his soul is escorted to the netherworld by two underworld envoys, Ox-Head and Horse-Face, he is granted one final opportunity to relive his memories before crossing over [5], [34]. This framing device allows the film to unfold in a non-linear fashion, weaving together the personal biography of Qiu Fu and the history of his family's opera troupe with the major political and social upheavals of 20th-century China [12], [30]. The narrative spans a vast chronological range, beginning in the 1920s Republican era and traversing through the Second Sino-Japanese War, the Chinese Civil War, the founding of the People's Republic of China in 1949, the Great Leap Forward, the Cultural Revolution, and finally into the late 1970s reform period [7], [8], [34]. This epic scope is achieved through extended flashbacks triggered by Qiu Fu's reflections, creating a tapestry that interweaves individual lives with national history [17].

The central character, Qiu Fu, is a mythicized version of the director's grandfather, Qiu Fu-xin, a celebrated Sichuan opera clown [6], [23]. The role of the clown, or *xiaochou*, is central to the film's thematic exploration. Qiu Jiongjiong describes the "clown spirit" as a resilient, darkly humorous, and contradictory figure who embodies nobility beneath coarseness, observes the world alongside participating in it, and carries a profound melancholy beneath a playful exterior [8]. This ethos informs all of the director's work and is embodied by Qiu Fu, who uses the tools of performance and humor to navigate immense tragedy, persecution, and political turmoil [8], [20]. The film portrays his life from his childhood as an abandoned orphan found by the military commander Pocky (played by Qiu Zhimin, the director's father) and adopted into the troupe, to his rise as the head clown and leader of the company [6], [22]. Throughout his journey, he experiences love, loss, artistic struggle, and ideological conflict, reflecting the broader fate of artists and intellectuals under various regimes [19]. The narrative follows the troupe's evolution from its origins in the chaos of civil war to its eventual dissolution amidst the catastrophic disruptions of the Cultural Revolution [22].

Thematically, the film operates on multiple levels, exploring the nature of memory, the role of art in society, and the resilience of the human spirit amid historical trauma. Memory is a paramount theme, articulated through the protagonist's final recollections before drinking a memory-losing soup in the netherworld, a symbolic act underscoring the fragility and importance of preserving the past [6], [12]. The film treats memory not as a simple record of facts but as a re-lived, collective experience—one insistently comic in mode yet expansively humane in vision [32]. The narrative structure itself becomes a meditation on how history is remembered and forgotten, juxtaposing harrowing episodes with absurdist, comedic staging to evoke resilience and memorialize collective memory [8]. Another key theme is the tension between performance and reality. The film deliberately blurs this boundary, presenting its story as a kind of magical realist effect where the line between the stage and life dissolves [6], [12]. This self-awareness extends to the film's meta-theatrical framing, which explores performativity in modern Chinese history—from the Republic era to the Maoist period—through the lens of a theater company forced to adapt to shifting political winds [25]. The film also engages with Taoist cosmology, particularly the concept of Diyu, the Taoist underworld, and incorporates figures from Chinese mythology like the Chicken Foot God (Ji Jiao Shen), an animist clown-spirit and authorial surrogate, to create a richly layered spiritual rumination on time and existence [8], [27]. Through these complex layers, *A New Old Play* becomes a profound exploration of how individuals and communities use art and storytelling to make sense of a chaotic and often brutal history.

## Production and Aesthetic Style

The production of *A New Old Play* is characterized by an artisanal, craft-like mode of filmmaking that prioritizes conceptual depth and visual poetry over conventional cinematic realism [41]. The entire production took place within a 400-square meter studio, a method that reflects Qiu Jiongjiong's 'artisanal, craft-like mode of filmmaking' entirely separate from the industry [29], [41]. Principal photography began in August 2019 in Leshan, Sichuan, China, lasting for 83 days over three months, followed by post-production in France [1]. This controlled studio environment allowed for meticulous control over every visual element, enabling the realization of the film's unique aesthetic vision. The screenplay was written by Qiu Jiongjiong himself over seven months, from May 2017 to February 2018, resulting in a document exceeding 100,000 words that forms the basis of this elaborate construction [1]. The production team included key collaborators such as producers Ding Ningyuan and Yang Jin, with co-producers ZHAO Jin, LI Yuwen, Jordane Oudin, and HAN Yi [3]. Cinematography was handled by Feng Yuchao, also known as Robbin Feng, whose work contributes significantly to the film's distinctive visual language [3], [7].

The film's most defining feature is its radical commitment to theatricality and artificiality, a conscious rejection of realistic depiction in favor of a stylized, painterly aesthetic [5], [19]. All scenes were constructed on handcrafted, rich, and painterly dioramic sets, employing a 'cloud-like', omniscient camera movement that treats every element—including background extras, bricks, and trees—as narratively functional [6]. The sets themselves are conspicuously flat and distressed, with backdrops painted to simulate landscapes, rejecting traditional cinematic depth of field [5]. This approach is designed to create an "archetypal, theater-infused continuity between 'real world' and supernatural/unreal spaces" [5]. The film's visual grammar draws heavily from traditional Chinese scroll paintings, utilizing horizontal tracking shots and planimetric frontal framing to evoke a sense of timeless, panoramic history [8]. The sets incorporate a variety of materials and methods, such as using billowing fabric to simulate ocean currents, cotton wool for clouds, and painted plywood to represent brick walls, all of which emphasize the artifice of the constructed world [5], [6]. This aesthetic is further informed by Qiu Jiongjiong's background as a visual artist, integrating his own large-format paintings directly into the mise-en-scène [8].

The casting strategy reinforces the film's thematic focus on performance and authenticity. While professional Sichuan opera actors filled the roles of the troupe teachers, the lead roles, including that of the adult Qiu Fu, were played by untrained actors [1]. Yi Sicheng, a well-known actor from a Sichuan opera troupe, made his acting debut in the lead role, bringing a "uniquely lilting voice" and "quiet and compelling empathy" to the character [6], [17]. This decision to cast amateurs in the central roles creates a fascinating dynamic where the raw, unpolished performances contrast with the highly polished, theatrical setting, further blurring the lines between reality and artifice. The supporting cast includes Guan Nan as Qiu Fu's wife Tong Huafeng, Qiu Zhimin as Pocky, Gu Tao as Crooky, and Xue Xuchun in dual roles as Qiu Fu's son Hei and the gnome-like figure Chicken Foot [1], [7]. The film's soundscape is equally integral to its atmosphere, featuring music composed by DIAO Lili and sound design by WANG Ran, complemented by costumes by BAI Yun and art direction by LV Xin [3], [7]. The runtime of the film is consistently cited as approximately 179 to 180 minutes, though some sources list it as 2 hours and 59 minutes, indicating a runtime of nearly three hours [3], [15], [18], [20]. This lengthy duration allows for a leisurely, immersive pacing that befits its epic scope and contemplative tone. The combination of these production choices results in a film that is less a documentary of history and more a phantasmagorical, spiritual rumination on time, memory, and the enduring role of the artist in a world defined by change [24].

## Critical Reception and Awards

Upon its world premiere, *A New Old Play* was met with widespread critical acclaim within the international arthouse and festival circuits, with reviewers praising its ambition, originality, and unique visual language. The film premiered in competition for the Golden Leopard at the 74th Locarno Film Festival on August 13, 2021, immediately establishing its credentials on the global stage [1], [45]. At Locarno, it won the Special Jury Prize in the Concorso internazionale section, a significant honor that highlighted its artistic merit and distinguished it among other major international contenders [1], [3], [46]. Critics lauded the film for its "magnificently layered historical epic" quality and its ability to "breathe new life into a story as old as civilization" [15]. Writing for *The New York Times*, Austin Considine gave the film a Critic’s Pick! designation, describing it as "a magnificently layered historical epic... breathes new life into a story as old as civilization" [15]. *The New Yorker*'s Richard Brody called it "daring and imaginative, with affecting intimacy," while *Senses of Cinema*'s Maria Giovanna Vagenas hailed it as "a huge epochal fresco" that revives Sichuan Opera "with the magic touch of a modern-day Méliès" [15]. In *The Austin Chronicle*, Richard Whittaker characterized it as "an epic and fantastical depiction of five decades of Chinese history," and *Cinema Scope* named it "the brightest light in the Chinese independent cinema world at this moment," positioning it as a landmark contribution to the genre [15].

Following its success at Locarno, the film continued to receive strong recognition at numerous other international film festivals. It was selected for screening at prominent events including Busan, Tallinn Black Nights, Rotterdam, Gothenburg, and Toronto's Mulan International Film Festival, where it served as the closing film [14], [52]. One of its most significant victories came at the 46th Hong Kong International Film Festival (HKIFF) in 2022, where it was officially selected for the Young Cinema Competition (Chinese-language) section [43]. There, it won two major prizes: the Firebird Award for Best Film, which praised it as "one of this year's most distinguished films both creatively and artistically," and the FIPRESCI Prize, awarded by the International Federation of Film Critics for "its masterful approach and inventive visual style" [14], [16], [40], [63]. This double win underscored the film's importance within the landscape of contemporary Chinese-language independent cinema. It also received the Junior Jury Award (Second Prize) at the 74th Locarno Film Festival and the Young Jury Award at the Festival des 3 Continents [3], [33], [54]. Furthermore, the film was nominated for Sight & Sound's "The best films of 2021" poll, with critic Robert Koehler voting for it, indicating its resonance among respected film critics worldwide [28].

The critical praise for *A New Old Play* centered on several key aspects of the film. Its visual style was frequently highlighted, with reviewers noting its painterly, tableaux-driven aesthetic and the way it seamlessly blends emotional immediacy with ironically distanced visual methods [15], [19]. The film's unique hybrid of fiction, historical reminiscence, and experimental theatre was seen as a strength, allowing it to dramatize twentieth-century Chinese political history in a fresh and compelling way [15], [32]. The performances, particularly Yi Sicheng's acclaimed acting debut as the lead, were noted for their quiet depth and compelling empathy [6]. The film's innovative approach to storytelling, which deliberately juxtaposes harrowing historical episodes like the Great Leap Forward famine with absurdist, comedic staging, was interpreted as a sophisticated technique for conveying resilience and processing collective memory [8]. Some analyses drew comparisons to canonical works of Chinese cinema, such as Chen Kaige’s *Farewell My Concubine* (1993) and Zhang Yimou’s *To Live* (1994), situating *A New Old Play* as a significant continuation of the tradition of epic historical dramas in China [29]. However, the film's challenging, non-naturalistic style and its dense, episodic narrative structure may have limited its appeal to mainstream audiences, confining its primary reception to cinephiles and academic circles focused on art-house cinema. Overall, the critical consensus positioned the film as a daring, ambitious, and artistically successful achievement that solidifies Qiu Jiongjiong's status as a major talent in contemporary world cinema.

## Release History and Censorship

The distribution and release history of *A New Old Play* reveals a stark dichotomy between its enthusiastic reception in the international festival circuit and its complete exclusion from public exhibition in mainland China. After its world premiere at the Locarno Film Festival in August 2021, the film embarked on a wide-ranging tour of international film festivals, showcasing its unique artistic vision to global audiences [14], [45]. Its commercial release was carefully managed through specialized distributors catering to the arthouse market. In North America, the film was acquired by Icarus Films through its dGenerate Films Collection, a distributor known for curating and releasing important works of independent Chinese cinema [36], [48]. It had its limited theatrical release in the United States on May 20, 2022, targeting cinephile audiences in select cities [18], [51]. This theatrical rollout was part of a broader strategy that also included digital distribution. The film was later made available on the streaming platform OVID.tv, a collaborative SVOD service founded by eight independent U.S. distributors to fill the gap left by major streaming services in offering high-quality independent and international films [50], [53]. Its streaming premiere occurred on December 19, 2025, making it accessible to a wider audience beyond those attending physical screenings [50].

In Asia, the film was distributed by Parallax Films, the international sales and distribution brand of Beijing-based Midnight Blur Films, which secured deals with Carlotta Films in France, Katsuben Cinema Club in Japan, and Cineaste Production House in Singapore for theatrical releases [19]. The Japanese premiere took place at the SKIP CITY INTERNATIONAL D-Cinema FESTIVAL 2023, marking its first official screening in the country [31]. Screenings were also organized in other parts of the world, including retrospectives at institutions like the University of Chicago and Hong Kong University in early 2024, and planned series at venues like London’s Garden Cinema and Berlin’s CiLENS for later in 2025, indicating its ongoing presence in academic and cultural programming [19]. This pattern of release highlights a clear strategy aimed at building a long-term reputation within the global art-house community rather than pursuing mass-market commercial success.

The most defining aspect of the film's release history, however, is its definitive absence from mainland China. Multiple sources confirm that *A New Old Play* "had no public screenings" there and "never passed the Chinese censors" [49]. This conclusion is strongly supported by official documentation from China's National Film Administration (NFA). Public notices listing approved domestic feature films for theatrical release contain no entry for the film under its Chinese title, *椒麻堂會*, or any variant spelling or transliteration [60]. Similarly, official filings for newly approved film projects in 2024 also show zero references to the film, confirming it was never submitted for or granted a production filing approval by the NFA [61], [62]. As a Sino-French co-production, the film would fall outside the list of exclusively domestically produced films, but its complete absence from these official records confirms its non-circulation within mainland China [60]. This censorship is likely linked to the film's sensitive historical subject matter, which includes graphic depictions of the Great Leap Forward famine and the Cultural Revolution-era persecution of artists [8], [49]. The film's explicit focus on the struggles of a traditional art form like Sichuan opera, potentially critiquing the ways in which it was co-opted or suppressed by successive political regimes, may also contribute to its banned status. This complete separation from its home market frames the film's entire cultural context, transforming it from a piece of Chinese cinema into a diasporic artifact—a powerful testament to a history and a cultural heritage that remains officially silenced within its country of origin.

## Legacy and Significance

*A New Old Play* holds a significant place in contemporary world cinema as a unique and ambitious work that pushes the boundaries of historical epic storytelling. Its legacy is multifaceted, encompassing its artistic innovation, its contribution to the discourse on Chinese history and memory, and its status as a symbol of artistic resistance. The film's most profound impact lies in its radical aesthetic approach, which deliberately eschews the conventions of naturalistic cinema in favor of a highly stylized, theatrical, and painterly language [5], [19]. By constructing its entire narrative universe within a studio using meticulously crafted dioramas and embracing the artifice of performance, Qiu Jiongjiong creates a space where history is not simply depicted but is experienced as a kind of magical realist spectacle [6], [12]. This technique allows the film to operate on multiple registers simultaneously, treating the horrors of the Great Leap Forward famine or the absurdities of the Cultural Revolution with a blend of tragic grandeur and dark comedy that reflects the "clown spirit" of resilience and observation [8], [20]. This approach distinguishes it from more conventional historical dramas and establishes it as a singular artistic statement that prioritizes emotional and philosophical resonance over factual reconstruction.

In the context of Chinese-language cinema, *A New Old Play* is significant for its intimate and personal engagement with a turbulent national history. By centering the story on the life of a Sichuan opera clown, a figure often relegated to the margins of official histories, the film offers a microcosm of the nation's macro-trauma [8], [23]. It provides a nuanced portrayal of how ordinary people navigated, endured, and ultimately struggled against overwhelming political forces, using the art of performance as a tool for survival and commentary. The film's focus on the preservation and celebration of Sichuan opera, a popular art form, serves as a subtle critique of the erasure of cultural diversity and local traditions under centralized ideological campaigns [21], [23]. Its comparison to canonical films like *Farewell My Concubine* and *To Live* underscores its ambition to join the ranks of great Chinese historical epics that grapple with the complex relationship between individual lives and national destiny [29]. However, unlike those films, *A New Old Play* achieves this through a more abstract, poetic, and formally experimental lens, making it a distinct and valuable addition to the genre.

Ultimately, the film's greatest legacy may be its status as a testament to artistic freedom and a powerful artifact of diasporic culture. Its complete exclusion from mainland China due to censorship transforms it from a mere film into a political statement [49]. The fact that a work so deeply rooted in Chinese history and culture could not be shown to the people of its homeland speaks volumes about the ongoing challenges faced by artists working within China's restrictive media environment. The film's success abroad, therefore, takes on a different meaning—it becomes a channel through which a suppressed history can be told and remembered. Its journey through international festivals and its presence on curated streaming platforms ensure that the stories of Qiu Fu and his family, along with the rich cultural world of Sichuan opera, are preserved and disseminated to a global audience. In this sense, *A New Old Play* is not just a film about memory; it is a living act of remembrance, a defiant assertion of the right to tell difficult truths, and a poignant reminder of the power of art to transcend political borders.
