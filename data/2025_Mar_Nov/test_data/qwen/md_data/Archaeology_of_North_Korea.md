# Archaeology of North Korea

## Prehistory and Early Settlement

The archaeological record of the Korean Peninsula indicates that humans have inhabited the region for tens of thousands of years, with the earliest evidence dating back to at least 40,000 BCE [15]. While the peninsula's proximity to mainland Asia provided a corridor for migration, its hilly terrain and northern mountain ranges created a degree of natural separation from Manchuria, fostering a distinct cultural development over millennia [37]. In North Korea, the study of this deep past is characterized by both foundational discoveries and significant evidentiary gaps, particularly concerning precise chronology. The earliest confirmed human presence is documented at sites like Komun Moru in Pyeongannam-do province, where chipped stone tools and animal horn implements indicate a lifestyle centered on hunting and gathering [78]. This period is part of the broader East Asian Upper Paleolithic, which saw the emergence of sophisticated toolkits. The Early Upper Paleolithic (EUP) in Korea, established by around 43,000–40,000 calibrated years before present (cal BP), is defined by diagnostic artifacts such as tanged points and blades manufactured from fine-grained lithic raw materials like silicified tuff and siliceous shale [14]. Sites like Hajin-ri, Yongsujaeul, and Songam-ri in South Korea provide some of the most reliable radiocarbon dates for this era, establishing a timeline for the initial peopling of the peninsula [14]. However, a critical limitation in the North Korean context is the scarcity of comparable, rigorously dated EUP sites. One source explicitly notes that, as far as is known, there are no reliable Accelerator Mass Spectrometry (AMS) radiocarbon data available from North Korean Paleolithic sites, highlighting a major gap in the chronological framework compared to more extensively researched areas in the south [65].

Following the Paleolithic, the Neolithic period, known locally as the Jeulmun (Comb-pattern Pottery) or Chulmun period, began around 8000–7000 BCE [3], [15]. This era marks a fundamental shift towards a more sedentary lifestyle, characterized by the widespread use of pottery, the construction of pit dwellings, and the establishment of villages, often situated near rivers, coasts, or on inland floodplains [1], [3]. The name "Jeulmun" derives from the comb-patterned designs that became prominent on pottery vessels, a feature that defines much of the culture [5]. North Korea is home to numerous Chulmun sites, though site density differences between North and South Korea are widely believed to reflect disparities in research effort rather than actual distribution [3]. This period also saw the development of broad-spectrum economies, where communities relied on a diverse range of resources, including marine life, freshwater fish, and terrestrial plants and animals [4]. A pivotal development during the Middle Chulmun period (~5500 cal BP / ~3500 BCE) was the appearance of early domesticated crops, primarily foxtail millet (*Setaria italica*) and broomcorn millet (*Panicum miliaceum*), which likely spread from northeast China [1], [34]. Evidence of millet cultivation has been found at various North Korean sites, including Munam-ri, where carbonized grains and a dry field were excavated [3]. However, the extent of its contribution to the overall subsistence base remains a subject of scholarly debate. Stable isotope analysis suggests that while reliance on C3 plants increased, communities continued to heavily depend on marine resources, indicating that agriculture was not yet dominant [4]. Furthermore, acorns from oak wood constituted a primary carbohydrate source before the adoption of full-scale farming, as evidenced by storage pits containing large quantities of acorns found at sites across the peninsula [2]. The authenticity of some early millet finds, such as those reported at the Jitam-ri (Chitam-ni) site, has even been contested by some researchers due to concerns over systematic recovery methods and the lack of contextual documentation in published reports [5].

The transition from the Neolithic to the Bronze Age is marked by the Mumun Pottery Period (c. 1500–100 BC), a time of profound social and economic transformation [4], [67]. This era witnessed an intensification of agriculture, population growth, and the emergence of hierarchical societies and chiefdoms [4], [64]. Rice cultivation, introduced from Shandong and Liaodong peninsulas around 1500 BC, became a crucial component of the economy alongside millet [34]. The Mumun period is named for its characteristic plain, undecorated pottery used for cooking and storage [67]. In North Korea, this period is archaeologically linked to the late phase of Old Chosŏn (Gojoseon). The material culture of this time includes distinctive bronze artifacts, most notably the "mandolin-shaped" or lute-shaped bronze dagger, which is considered a hallmark of the Liaoning bronze dagger culture that spread to the peninsula around 1000 BC [28], [76]. These daggers are distinct from their Chinese counterparts and are thought to have been produced locally, with their ornamentation level correlating to social prestige, providing material evidence of a stratified society [[77]/03%3A_Bronze_Age_(2000_BCE__500_BCE)/3.05%3A_Bronze_Age-_Gojoseon_-_Korea_(2333_BCE__108_BCE))]. Dolmens, megalithic burial chambers, also proliferated during this time, with over 35,000 found on the peninsula, suggesting the mobilization of significant communal labor under centralized authority [[77]/03%3A_Bronze_Age_(2000_BCE__500_BCE)/3.05%3A_Bronze_Age-_Gojoseon_-_Korea_(2333_BCE__108_BCE)), [80]]. The advent of iron technology also occurred during this late Bronze Age phase. Archaeological evidence suggests the establishment of a distinct indigenous iron culture in the Taedong River basin as early as the 2nd century BCE, challenging assumptions of passive technological adoption and supporting the idea that Gojoseon was a regional center of innovation [39]. Despite these developments, the traditional chronology for Gojoseon, which places its legendary founding by Dangun in 2333 BCE, lacks archaeological corroboration; the emergence of a state-level society is supported by material evidence only from the 7th–4th centuries BCE onwards [79], [80].

## Ancient Kingdoms and State Formation

The archaeological landscape of North Korea is dominated by the legacy of several powerful ancient kingdoms whose historical narratives are central to modern national identity. The earliest of these is Old Chosŏn (Gojoseon), traditionally founded in 2333 BCE by the legendary figure Dangun, a narrative deeply embedded in Korean mythology recorded in texts like the *Samguk yusa* [6], [22]. However, archaeological evidence places the emergence of a recognizable state-level society much later, in the 7th to 4th centuries BCE, in the river basins of the Liao and Taedong [79], [80]. The capital of Gojoseon, Wanggeomseong, is identified with the Pyongyang area, and its final phase, Wiman Joseon (established 194 BCE), solidified its control over the region [79], [80]. The material culture of this period provides tangible insights into its structure and power. Burial cairns excavated along the Liaodong peninsula, jointly studied by Chinese and North Korean teams, reveal elite graves containing bronze regalia and early iron implements, indicative of a centralized authority and a stratified society [76], [79]. The most iconic artifact associated with Gojoseon is the "mandolin-shaped" or lute-shaped bronze dagger, a regional variant of the broader Liaoning bronze dagger culture [36], [76]. Over 60 such daggers have been excavated in Korea, distinguished by their pear-shaped blades and higher zinc content, suggesting localized production and a system of distributing prestige objects among elites [[28], [77]/03%3A_Bronze_Age_(2000_BCE__500_BCE)/3.05%3A_Bronze_Age-_Gojoseon_-_Korea_(2333_BCE__108_BCE))]. These artifacts, along with burnished red wares appearing in dolmen burials by the 6th century BCE, form the empirical basis for understanding Gojoseon's social and political organization [76]. The fall of Gojoseon in 108 BCE after its conquest by the Han dynasty led to the establishment of four commanderies, including the influential Lelang Commandery, which profoundly impacted the region's development through the introduction of Chinese coinage, seals, and building techniques [37].

Following the collapse of Han control in the north, the powerful kingdom of Goguryeo emerged, eventually becoming one of the Three Kingdoms of Korea [31]. At its peak, its territory spanned the middle of the Korean peninsula, parts of northeastern China, and the Primorsky region of Russia [44]. The archaeological remains of Goguryeo, concentrated in what is now North Korea, form a cornerstone of the nation's cultural heritage and historical narrative. The kingdom's legacy is visible in its impressive fortresses, capitals, and especially its vast network of tombs. The capital was moved to Pyongyang in 427 CE, making the region a focal point of Goguryeo's later history [22], [27]. The kingdom's expansion southward under King Gwanggaeto in the late 4th century brought it into conflict with Silla, a dynamic that North Korean historiography interprets as a struggle against treacherous pro-foreign powers [16], [38]. This narrative frames Goguryeo as the true embodiment of "Koreanness," championing self-reliance and cultural superiority [16]. The kingdom's extensive interaction sphere extended across East Asia, connecting it with contemporaneous empires like Tang China and Nara Japan, as evidenced by shared artifact types like Han Dynasty TLV mirrors and socketed spearheads found on both the peninsula and the Japanese islands [31], [33], [64]. The arrival of Buddhism in 372 CE further influenced Goguryeo's art and architecture, a development reflected in tomb murals and temple foundations [22], [70].

The successor state to Goguryeo, Balhae (also known as Bohai), ruled from 698 to 926 CE. Its origins are contested, but North Korean scholarship, led by figures like Pak Se-yong in the 1960s, firmly asserts its continuity with Goguryeo, claiming it was founded by people of Goguryeo bloodline and culture [47]. This perspective positions Balhae as part of the "Northern and Southern States period" alongside Unified Silla in the south [47]. Archaeological evidence supports this claim, particularly from joint North Korean-Chinese excavations. A project at the Bugeori site in Cheongjin City, North Hamgyeong Province, uncovered steles, official seals, and administrative artifacts attributed to Dongjing, one of Balhae's five capitals, confirming its territorial presence on the eastern coast of the peninsula [82], [83]. Excavations at Kraskino Fortress in Russia's Maritime Province, identified as Yeom Ju, a major administrative region of Balhae, revealed relics confirming its cultural roots in Goguryeo-period earthenware steamers and its role as a gateway to trade routes connecting Balhae with Silla and Japan [81]. These findings underscore Balhae's status as a flourishing, multi-ethnic empire with sophisticated political structures, modeled after the Tang capital of Chang'an, and a rich material culture documented in extensive Chinese excavation reports [81], [84]. The ongoing dispute over the historical identity of Balhae is a direct consequence of the geopolitical tensions surrounding the earlier Goguryeo controversy, with North Korean interpretations consistently rejecting any notion of Balhae being subordinate to China [47], [48].

## Goguryeo Cultural Legacy

The archaeological legacy of the Goguryeo kingdom is most vividly expressed in its funerary art, particularly the wall paintings discovered within its tombs. The "Complex of the Koguryo Tombs," a UNESCO World Heritage Site inscribed in 2004, encompasses over 60 tombs located in Pyongyang and Nampo, representing the kingdom's later period after its capital was moved to Pyongyang in 427 AD [16], [27], [46]. These tombs, dating from the 3rd to the 7th century AD, served as royal and elite burial grounds and contain vibrant mural paintings that offer an unparalleled glimpse into Goguryeo cosmology, courtly life, and beliefs about the afterlife [19], [21], [46]. Scientific analysis of these murals has revealed a sophisticated and unique artistic technique. Researchers have determined that the paintings were executed using a "pseudo-fresco" method—a hybrid approach that involved mixing traditional soft binders and applying pigments via secco techniques, which differs from both true buon fresco and standard secco methods [71]. This technique allowed for post-drying corrections and the application of pigments directly onto a prepared surface [71]. Detailed studies of tombs like Jinpari Tomb No. 4 have reconstructed the wall's layered composition: a plaster layer (primarily earthen mixed with lime), a white ground layer, a semitransparent layer, and finally the painting layer itself, which is remarkably thin, ranging from 14.2 to 26.2 micrometers thick [45].

The iconography of the Goguryeo murals is rich and complex, reflecting a syncretic belief system incorporating elements of Taoism, Buddhism, and indigenous shamanistic traditions. A common motif on vaulted ceilings is the celestial world, depicted with constellations, lotus flowers, and mystical beings, symbolizing rebirth and the journey of the soul to the heavens [45], [75]. Another prominent theme is the depiction of guardian deities, known as Sasindo, who protect the deceased in the afterlife. These deities are typically represented as four mythical animals corresponding to the four cardinal directions: the Blue Dragon (Cheongnyong) in the east, the White Tiger (Baekho) in the west, the Red Phoenix (Jujak) in the south, and the Black Turtle-and-Snake (Hyeonmu) in the north [44], [75]. Scenes from the human world are also prevalent, showing military processions, dancers, servants, wrestling matches, and tea ceremonies, providing invaluable ethnographic details about Goguryeo society [38], [75]. The identification of specific individuals in some murals, such as the portraits in Anak Tomb No. 3, has sparked scholarly debate, with differing interpretations of whether the occupant was a king or a high-ranking officer [70]. The dating of these murals spans the 4th to 7th centuries, with stylistic shifts reflecting changes in religious and political ideologies over time, such as the increasing prominence of Buddhist motifs like lotus blossoms and the evolution of guardian deity imagery [45], [70].

The palette of the Goguryeo artists was derived from readily available mineral pigments, and scientific analysis has successfully identified the chemical composition of many colors used in the murals. This research confirms the use of malachite (Cu₂(CO₃)(OH)₂) for green, cinnabar (HgS) for red, hematite (Fe₃O₄) for reddish-brown, orpiment (As₂S₃) for yellow, and gold (Au) for gilding [45]. These pigments were consistent with 5th- to 6th-century Goguryeo material practices, lending strong support to the authenticity of the murals [45]. The discovery of a granite stele in Ji’an, China, in 2012, provides crucial textual context for interpreting the murals' symbolic language [26]. Similar in format to the famous Kwanggaet’o Stele erected in 414 CE, the Ji’an Stele contains an inscription detailing the foundation myth of Goguryeo, royal achievements, and regulations for a state-administered system of tomb custodians (Sumyoje) [26]. This stele helps explain the elaborate rituals and social structures depicted in the tombs, revealing a highly organized society with a centralized bureaucracy responsible for maintaining royal necropolises. The combination of these monumental stone inscriptions and the intimate, colorful murals creates a powerful and multifaceted record of a civilization that left an indelible mark on the history of East Asia.

## Historic Successor States and Dynasties

Beyond the grandeur of Goguryeo, the archaeological record of North Korea reveals important remnants of subsequent dynasties, most notably the Goryeo dynasty (918–1392 CE) and the short-lived Joseon period. The historic city of Gaeseong (Kaesong) served as the capital of the Goryeo dynasty, and its archaeological remains represent a crucial link to this transformative era in Korean history [19], [21]. The "Historic Monuments and Sites in Kaesong" were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2013, encompassing 12 distinct monuments and sites that illustrate the Goryeo dynasty's architectural achievements and its cultural transition from Buddhism to neo-Confucianism [19], [25]. Among the most significant is the sprawling Manwoldae palace complex, located at the base of Mount Songak [42]. Covering over six acres, this royal complex contained more than 100 structures, including audience halls, libraries, shrines, temples, and living quarters [42]. Although largely destroyed by fire in 1361, excavations since 1954 have unearthed thousands of artifacts, including thousands of patterned ceramic roof tiles decorated with dragons and vines, celadon cups, and inscribed tiles bearing Sanskrit characters, all testifying to the dynasty's advanced craftsmanship and integration of Buddhist principles [42]. Ongoing joint North-South Korean excavations, set to resume in 2026 after a hiatus since 2018, aim to further explore this vast site and understand the layout and function of its various zones [18], [42].

Further north, the city of Kaesong itself is a testament to Goryeo urban planning. It features fortified palaces like Jangan and Taesong, an astronomical observatory, schools, and a meticulously laid-out city grid reflecting geomantic principles, all of which are part of the UNESCO-inscribed property [19], [21]. Other notable sites recommended for potential UNESCO listing include the Mausoleum of King Wanggeon, the founder of the dynasty, and the Mausoleum of King Gongmin, showcasing the architectural styles and burial customs of the period [25]. These sites collectively demonstrate the assimilation of pre-existing Korean cultural values and cross-border exchanges with neighboring states like the Liao and Jin dynasties, serving as exceptional testimony to the unified Goryeo civilization during a period of significant regional transition [25]. The preservation and study of these Goryeo-era sites are vital for understanding the political, spiritual, and cultural foundations of the Korean state.

The Joseon dynasty (1392–1897), which followed the Goryeo, also left its mark on North Korean soil, though its influence was less pronounced than that of its predecessor. The Joseon dynasty established four commanderies in the northern part of the Korean Peninsula to suppress the Xiongnu, leading to the widespread adoption of Chinese cultural elements [37]. Historical records from this period document the existence of small states in Korea and Manchuria, including Goguryeo and Buyeo, with fluid ethnic identities shaped by interactions with nomadic groups like the Xiongnu and Xianbei [38]. While specific archaeological sites from the Joseon period in North Korea are not as prominently featured in the provided sources as those from the Goryeo or Goguryeo eras, their presence is confirmed through historical accounts and the continuation of certain cultural practices [37]. For instance, the use of Chinese writing, first seen in Goguryeo with the Kwanggaet’o Stele, became more widespread during the Joseon period, influencing administration and scholarship [38]. The legacy of the Han Commanderies, which preceded the Joseon, continued to shape the region's development long after their formal dissolution, laying the groundwork for future state formation on the peninsula [37]. The archaeological investigation of these later periods, while perhaps less spectacular than the murals of Goguryeo, is essential for completing the full historical narrative of the Korean people and their complex relationships with neighboring civilizations.

## Geopolitical Context and International Collaboration

The study of North Korean archaeology cannot be divorced from the intense geopolitical dynamics of the Korean Peninsula and Northeast Asia. The most significant contemporary issue shaping the interpretation of ancient history is the Northeast Project, a large-scale academic initiative launched by the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences in 2002 [48]. This project sought to reclassify the ancient kingdoms of Goguryeo (37 BC–668 AD) and Balhae (698–926 AD) as regional governments of ancient China, thereby asserting historical and territorial claims over parts of modern-day North Korea [[48], [49])]. The strategic motivation behind this endeavor reportedly included preparing for a potential regime collapse in North Korea by establishing a historical precedent to justify intervention or annexation, framing such actions as "stabilization" [48]. This assertion directly contradicts the historical narratives of both South and North Korea, which view Goguryeo and Balhae as sovereign Korean states. The project sparked major diplomatic tensions with South Korea in 2004 and continues to influence public discourse and curriculum changes in China, such as the removal of the Sui dynasty's failed invasion of Goguryeo from textbooks [[49])]. North Korean scholarship has responded with a firm rejection of these Chinese claims, dismissing them as "a pathetic attempt to manipulate history for its own interests" and a politically motivated challenge to Korean sovereignty [16]. This divergence in historical interpretation highlights how archaeology becomes a battleground for national identity and territorial integrity.

Mount Paektu (Paektusan), an active volcano straddling the North Korea-China border, serves as a potent symbol of this geopolitical tension [9], [10]. The mountain holds immense sacred and mythological significance for Koreans, being the birthplace of Dangun, the legendary founder of Gojoseon, in Korean mythology [6], [8]. Control over the mountain's landscape is therefore tied to issues of national sovereignty and historical legitimacy. The 1962 Sino-DPRK Border Treaty, the full details of which remained undisclosed for decades, divided the mountain and its iconic Heaven Lake caldera, assigning approximately 55% of the lake and adjacent peaks to North Korea [10]. This agreement directly shaped North Korea's territorial control over key archaeological landscapes associated with Gojoseon, Goguryeo, and Balhae, and remains a cornerstone of its state-directed heritage management policies [10]. The mountain's geological history adds another layer of complexity. The "Millennium Eruption" of 946 CE, one of the largest volcanic eruptions in recorded history, deposited ash across Japan and Greenland, with contemporary Korean and Japanese historical chronicles describing its effects [6], [11]. This event has become a focal point for international scientific collaboration, demonstrating that even in a highly restrictive environment, geoscience can serve as a channel for engagement. A multi-year project involving British vulcanologists, North Korean scientists, and institutions in the UK and US secured UN sanctions exemptions to conduct fieldwork, collect seismic data, and analyze tephra rocks, integrating multiple lines of evidence to precisely date the eruption [11], [13]. This unprecedented cooperation, led by Professor Clive Oppenheimer of the University of Cambridge, underscores how high-profile scientific research can navigate political barriers and foster dialogue [13].

Despite the overarching political tensions, scientific collaboration remains one of the few viable channels for external scholarly access to North Korea [17]. Formalized, state-sanctioned partnerships have yielded significant archaeological results. Joint excavations at Goryeo-era sites like Manwoldae in Kaesong, which resumed in 2007 after a delay caused by nuclear tensions, have been instrumental in uncovering the palace's layout and artifacts [42]. Similarly, collaborative projects with Chinese scholars have expanded the known archaeological footprint of Balhae, with joint excavations at the Bugeori site in Cheongjin City documenting administrative artifacts that support the claim of Balhae's continuity with Goguryeo [82]. The restoration of Yujeomsa Temple in Mount Kumkang, a site recently inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List, is planned in collaboration with civil society organizations, further illustrating the potential for cooperative ventures [18]. These examples, however, remain exceptions. Access for independent, peer-reviewed archaeological work is severely restricted, and much of the published research originates from domestic teams or is the product of carefully vetted international partnerships. The legacy of the Northeast Project and the sensitive nature of historical interpretation mean that any foreign involvement must navigate a complex web of political sensitivities, making genuine academic freedom a significant challenge [47].

## Modern Archaeology and Statecraft

In North Korea, archaeology is fundamentally an instrument of statecraft, meticulously curated to reinforce the official ideology of Juche and legitimize the dynastic rule of the Kim family. The nation's archaeological narrative is not merely a collection of objective facts but a constructed story designed to forge a continuous and proud lineage from the mythical origins of the Korean people to the present-day leadership. This principle of ideological alignment permeates every aspect of the discipline, from the selection of research topics to the interpretation of findings and their presentation to the public. The state-controlled media consistently promotes a version of history that emphasizes themes of self-reliance, uniqueness, and superiority, positioning North Korea as the true heir to the great ancient civilizations of the peninsula [16]. This narrative is powerfully embodied in the promotion of Mount Paektu. Official state media depicts the mountain as the sacred birthplace of both Dangun, the legendary founder of Gojoseon, and Kim Jong-il, the second leader of North Korea [7], [8]. This deliberate fusion of pre-existing national mythology with the Kim family's own origin story serves to create a divine, divinely mandated lineage, anchoring the regime's legitimacy in 'indigenous' sacred geography [7], [12]. The ritualized use of the mountain in state propaganda, such as Kim Jong-un's visits immediately preceding major political events, demonstrates its systematic deployment as a tool for ideological signaling and regime consolidation [7].

This ideological framework is institutionalized through legislation and state-led initiatives. The Law of the Democratic People's Republic of Korea on Protection of Cultural Property, adopted in 1994, outlines a strict system for managing and preserving cultural assets, with the stated purpose of enhancing national pride and ensuring their proper inheritance and development [72]. Major archaeological projects are often initiated or guided by the highest levels of government. The excavation and subsequent renovation of the Tomb of King Tongmyŏng in Pyongyang in the mid-1970s is a prime example of this process. While the identification of the tomb was based on its location and the artifacts found within, the final assignment of its identity as the tomb of Goguryeo's founding king was made by direct guidance from Kim Il-sung, underscoring the subordination of academic inquiry to political decree [41]. Similarly, the construction of the Tomb of Dangun mausoleum in Pyongyang, which houses purported remains of the mythical founder and his wife, was a state project explicitly designed to fuse the national origin myth with the Kim dynasty's claim to power, creating a linear genealogy from Dangun to Kim Il-sung that establishes Pyongyang as the historical and political center of Korean orthodoxy [8], [41]. These acts transform archaeology from a science into a tool for constructing and reinforcing a specific, state-sanctioned national identity.

The international community recognizes North Korea's significant contributions to the world's cultural heritage through designations such as UNESCO World Heritage Sites. The "Complex of the Koguryo Tombs" in Pyongyang and Nampo was North Korea's first World Heritage site, inscribed in 2004, followed by the "Historic Monuments and Sites in Kaesong" in 2013 [16], [19], [21]. These inscriptions serve as powerful validation of the nation's historical importance and the richness of its cultural legacy, providing international recognition for the state-sponsored narrative of a glorious past. Beyond these major sites, North Korea maintains a list of properties on its tentative list for future consideration, including the "Caves in Kujang Area" and the "Historical Relics in Pyongyang," indicating an ongoing effort to systematically identify and preserve its heritage [24]. In addition to tangible heritage, intangible cultural practices are also promoted. The "Tradition of Kimchi-making in the DPR of Korea" was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2015, with the nomination framing the practice as essential for women to become "good mothers" and foundational for social cohesion, thereby reinforcing state-promoted gender norms [58]. Ultimately, the archaeology of North Korea operates within a tightly controlled framework where the pursuit of knowledge is secondary to the imperative of national identity construction. The archaeological record is selectively interpreted and presented not just to understand the past, but to shape the present and secure the future of the state.
