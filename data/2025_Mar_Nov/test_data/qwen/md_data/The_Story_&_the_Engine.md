# The Story & the Engine

## Production and Personnel

"The Story & the Engine" is the fifth episode of Series 15, marketed globally as Season 2, of the British science fiction television series *Doctor Who* [[1]), [2]]. It represents a significant point in the show's history, marking the debut of writer Inua Ellams and introducing a richly textured exploration of narrative power, postcolonial identity, and Afrofuturist mythmaking [7], [22]. The episode's creation was a deliberate convergence of strategic casting, culturally specific authorship, and innovative production design aimed at expanding the franchise's thematic and geographical scope [34]. Its development was overseen by showrunner Russell T Davies, who has been instrumental in guiding the series' revival since 2021 [34]. Davies confirmed that new episodes would include moments acknowledging the show's 62-year history, describing the series as "a living text," a concept directly realized in the episode's climax [13].

The episode was written by Nigerian-British poet and playwright Inua Ellams, whose appointment was a landmark moment for the series, making him the first Black man to write for the revived era of *Doctor Who* [7], [22]. Ellams' background as a critically acclaimed literary figure, including being a Fellow of the Royal Society of Literature and recipient of an OBE for services to the arts, brought a distinct literary sensibility to the script [7]. His prior works, particularly *The Barbershop Chronicles*, directly informed the episode's central metaphor of the barbershop as a sanctuary for communal storytelling and intimate connection [7], [22]. The script also draws from Ellams' personal discoveries, most notably his 2020 finding of racist linguistic erasure in Francophone knowledge systems through the French term 'le nègre' for ghostwriter, a concept woven directly into the episode's critique of colonial power structures [2], [5]. The narrative itself was adapted from one of Ellams' poems published in *Poetry* magazine in May 2020, which was inspired by an anecdote about musician Yo-Yo Ma visiting Botswana, illustrating Ellams' thesis that stories, like music, change with their context and remain vital only when they are "a live thing" [28].

The direction of the episode was handled by Makalla McPherson, who was responsible for translating Ellams' complex and theatrical script into a compelling visual narrative [2], [4], [13]. Her work was crucial in balancing the episode's confined, dialogue-driven structure with spectacular world-building elements, such as the CGI spider and the animated sequences depicting stories told within the barbershop [29]. The production team also included executive producers Russell T Davies and Zoella (Louise Pentland), ensuring alignment with the overarching vision for the second season [34]. The filming schedule placed the episode within Block Four of Series 15, indicating its planned narrative position within the season's broader arc [[1]), [36]]. Principal photography took place at Bad Wolf Studios, while location filming was used for establishing shots of Lagos, Nigeria, reinforcing the episode's commitment to authentic setting [4], [22]. Writer Inua Ellams himself made a cameo appearance in the episode as a marketplace seller, adding a metafictional layer to the production [4], [29].

The release strategy reflected the global nature of the *Doctor Who* brand. The episode premiered on Saturday, May 10, 2025, simultaneously on BBC iPlayer in the United Kingdom and Disney+ internationally [2], [13]. A subsequent broadcast on the terrestrial channel BBC One followed later that day [2], [13]. This dual-platform approach is consistent with the season's overall distribution model [36]. Ancillary content was also produced to enhance the viewing experience. A prequel short story titled 'What I Did On My Holidays By Omo Esosa', set in Lagos and Etsako, Nigeria in August 1965, was published on the BBC website [3], [4]. This story introduces young protagonist Omo Esosa and explores themes of environmental justice and colonial-era resource exploitation related to oil extraction, providing historical context for the main plot [3]. Additional promotional materials, including a compilation of past Doctor clips and the source stories, were also made available [11].

## Plot Synopsis

The narrative of "The Story & the Engine" centers on the Fifteenth Doctor, portrayed by Ncuti Gatwa, and his new companion, Belinda Chandra, played by Varada Sethu [[1]), [2]]. Their journey takes them to Lagos, Nigeria, a city that becomes more than just a backdrop, serving as a vibrant and integral character in the episode's exploration of identity and belonging [2], [22]. Upon arrival, the Doctor expresses a profound sense of acceptance and community, stating, "here, I’m accepted," a sentiment that immediately redefines his portrayal within the series [27]. The duo visits a barbershop named 'Omo's Palace', a space identified by multiple sources as a safe and sacred place for Black men to escape racism and speak freely [22], [28]. Inside, they encounter four local men—Omo, Rashid, Tunde, and Obioma—who share stories of their lives [29]. These stories are not merely anecdotes; they are revealed to be potent forces of reality. As each man speaks, his narrative animates in the window frames of the barbershop, visually demonstrating the episode's central thesis that stories shape and sustain existence [13], [39].

The atmosphere shifts dramatically when the shop's door opens onto a different dimension, revealing a colossal, multi-legged creature [37]. The four men vanish after entering, and the Doctor realizes he has walked into a trap set by the Barber, a mysterious figure played by Ariyon Bakare [5], [13]. The Barber reveals himself to be a former human mythmaker who once chronicled the stories of various gods—including Anansi, Saga, Bastet, Dionysus, and Loki—and in doing so, gave them life and power [37], [39]. However, when these deities gained autonomy, they cast him out without credit, leaving him forgotten and erased [37]. His motivation for vengeance is therefore rooted in a demand for recognition and narrative sovereignty, a powerful critique of colonial erasure [28], [37]. He built a transdimensional nexus powered by the collective stories of humanity, intending to sever the gods' connection to this web, thereby killing them by removing them from memory [37], [39].

Trapped inside the barbershop, which exists simultaneously in Lagos and on the back of the giant spider-like structure, the Doctor and Belinda must navigate the Barber's plan [37]. The barbershop contains a sentient "story engine"—a heart-brain organ that collects narratives and powers the nexus [13], [39]. The Barber's ultimate goal is to erase all gods across cultures, a plan that would lead to their extinction [24]. During the confrontation, the Barber's daughter, Abena, introduced as Michelle Asante, confronts the Doctor with emotional dialogue about growing up "trapped with Anansi, terrified someone would win me in a bet" [20]. The episode features a pivotal cameo by Jo Martin, reprising her role as the Fugitive Doctor, who appears in a flashback scene where she tells Abena about her own fugitive status and her busy, unfinished story [20], [39]. This scene was strategically timed by showrunner Russell T Davies to address the ethnicity of the current Doctor's incarnation, creating a powerful link between different Black Doctors across the series' history [9], [39].

The Doctor, recognizing the Barber's pain but rejecting his violent solution, attempts to reason with him. He establishes a cognitive link with the story engine, triggering a montage of all previous Doctor incarnations—from William Hartnell to Jodie Whittaker—with voice and visual clips [13]. This act culminates in the line, "My body is like a barber shop – all of them inside... telling their stories, bickering – I will not fail them," before the engine implodes from the overload of the "never-ending story" of the Doctor's own timeline, enabling the group's escape [13]. The escape is facilitated by a time/space compressor linking Lagos to the otherworldly craft [13]. Before leaving, a critical piece of lore is revealed: the motif of enslaved African Americans braiding escape route maps into their hair is physically enacted when Abena braids such a map onto the Doctor’s scalp, transforming Black hair into a literal vessel for ancestral knowledge and resistance [5], [39]. The episode concludes with the Doctor reflecting on the power of narrative, declaring "The engine is connected to me, my stories," cementing the idea that stories are autonomous, world-shaping engines of reality [13].

## Thematic and Narrative Analysis

At its core, "The Story & the Engine" is a dense and ambitious exploration of narrative sovereignty, postcolonial identity, and the ontological power of stories [2], [13]. The episode's central thesis posits that stories are not passive entertainment but active, world-shaping forces—an engine of reality, resistance, and ancestral continuity [13]. This concept is embodied in the narrative device of the sentient "story engine," a brain-like organ housed within the Lagos barbershop that feeds on human narratives to power a transdimensional web connecting various pantheons [37], [39]. This elevates storytelling from a mere art form to a fundamental force of existence, a theme that aligns with Afrofuturist principles of using speculative fiction to explore alternative histories and futures [2], [26]. The narrative argues that stories are the very fabric of belief and reality, a notion reinforced when the Barber seeks to kill gods by severing their connection to the web of human memory [37].

A powerful undercurrent of postcolonial critique runs throughout the episode, primarily through the character of the Barber, a deposed human mythmaker [37]. His quest for vengeance against the gods he created is framed as a demand for recognition, highlighting the historical erasure of marginalized creators and the disposability of their contributions [28], [37]. His motivation—"all of this started because he wanted to be credited for his work"—is a direct commentary on the systemic injustice of intellectual property and authorship [37]. The episode further critiques colonial linguistic violence through the explicit mention of the French term 'le nègre,' which means ghostwriter [2], [5]. This textualizes the theme of narrative erasure, pointing to how colonial powers and linguistic systems have historically stripped Black creators of their voices and agency. The Doctor's self-identification as "expendable" due to having "no home, no family" is another instance of this theme, reframing his status as the "Last of the Time Lords" through a contemporary political lens that resonates with discourse around refugees and displaced people [28].

The setting of the barbershop is far more than a convenient location; it is a central symbol of safety, community, and cultural sanctuary [22], [28]. Multiple sources describe it as a "safe, sacred place" where Black men can escape racism and speak freely, functioning as a locus of intimacy and connection [14], [22], [28]. For the Fifteenth Doctor, this space provides a rare moment of acceptance and joy, allowing him to forget his burdensome identity as the Last of the Time Lords [22], [27]. This depiction marks a significant departure from previous incarnations of the Doctor, who have often been portrayed with melancholy and isolation [22]. By centering this culturally specific space, the episode redefines the Doctor's identity, grounding it in shared experience and belonging rather than loneliness [28]. Belinda Chandra intuitively understands the significance of this space, drawing parallels to her own trips to India with her grandmother, further emphasizing the theme of intergenerational and cross-cultural connection through shared spaces and stories [28].

The episode is a prime example of Afrofuturist storytelling, utilizing speculative elements to explore Black identity, history, and futurity [2], [26]. It integrates West African folklore, particularly the legend of Anansi the Spider God, into the *Doctor Who* canon, creating a new mythological framework that moves beyond Eurocentric influences [13], [27]. The character of Abena, Anansi's daughter, serves as a bridge between the old world of oral tradition and the new, modern context of the story, confronting the Doctor with the intergenerational consequences of narrative choices [20]. Another key Afrofuturist motif is the transformation of Black hair into a conduit for ancestral knowledge. The episode physically enacts the historical practice of enslaved African Americans braiding escape maps into their hair, a motif brought to life when Abena braids such a map onto the Doctor’s scalp [5], [39]. This turns Black hair from a cultural marker into a literal vessel for resistance and survival. The inclusion of a prequel story, 'What I Did On My Holidays By Omo Esosa', further deepens this engagement with history by exploring themes of environmental justice and colonial-era resource exploitation in Nigeria, providing a revisionist counter-narrative to mainstream historical accounts [3].

The narrative structure itself reflects the episode's themes. It employs a confined, dialogue-driven, theatrical format centered almost entirely within the barbershop, mirroring the intimate and communal nature of storytelling [29]. This structure allows for deep ensemble character development among the trapped patrons, giving them brief but impactful moments to tell their stories [29]. The climax, where the Doctor connects to the story engine and the screen fills with clips of every previous Doctor, is a metafictional tour de force [13]. It serves as both a celebration of the series' history and a profound statement on the Doctor's identity as a multilayered, storied being whose body contains the entirety of his past incarnations [13]. The Doctor's metafictional six-word story, devised as a response to the Barber's challenge—"I'm born. I die. I'm born."—explicitly references the Time Lord regeneration mechanic, embedding core *Doctor Who* mythology into the episode's exploration of narrative [32]. However, some critics noted that the decision to use animation for the initial story-pastiches was a missed opportunity to visualize the Doctor's history in this way, suggesting the final reveal could have been even more impactful if the entire sequence had been animated [29].

## Critical and Public Reception

The critical reception for "The Story & the Engine" was polarized, reflecting the episode's ambitious and challenging nature. While many reviewers lauded its thematic depth, poetic execution, and groundbreaking representation, others critiqued its narrative compression, tonal inconsistencies, and underdeveloped character arcs [2], [24]. This schism in opinion underscores the episode's status as a high-concept piece that prioritized conceptual daring over conventional storytelling structure. Several critics praised the episode as a standout achievement in the second season of Russell T Davies' revival. Newsweek's Ryan Woodrow described it as a "fresh and visually striking hour" that uses magical realism to explore how cultures are defined by the stories they tell, identifying it as "the real highlight of the year" on a list of best TV shows of 2025 [38]. Another review hailed it as "perhaps season two’s freshest take on Doctor Who," crediting writer Inua Ellams with pushing the series "to frontiers anew" and calling it "spectacular" [30], [31]. The Guardian highlighted its significance as a milestone in representation, noting its nearly all-Black cast and Lagos setting—a first for the series beyond superficial location dressing—which was seen as a major step forward for diversity [2].

The performances, particularly those of Ncuti Gatwa and Ariyon Bakare, received widespread acclaim. Gatwa's portrayal was noted for its deep emotional authenticity, especially in his reaction to betrayal by his friend Omo [19]. His performance marked a departure from traditional, detached responses to such events, instead expressing profound hurt and loss, which reinforced the episode's focus on relational vulnerability [19]. Bakare's performance as the Barber was similarly praised for its complexity, portraying a villain driven by understandable pain and a desire for recognition rather than simple malice [37]. The integration of Jo Martin's Fugitive Doctor was also seen as a successful narrative choice, with showrunner Russell T Davies confirming the cameo was intentionally timed to address the ethnicity of the current Doctor's incarnation, creating a powerful thread of narrative kinship across different Black Doctors [9], [39]. The production design and visuals were frequently commended for their "sumptuous" quality and Afrofuturist aesthetic, including the design of the story-responsive windows and the heart-brain engine [22], [39].

Despite the praise, several reviews offered sharp constructive criticism. Many pointed to the episode's narrative density as a potential drawback. Critics noted that stakes were not clearly established until mid-episode, leading to a delayed sense of urgency [24]. The pacing was described as inconsistent, with some scenes feeling rushed or overly broad in their focus [2], [24]. Secondary characters, while well-intentioned, were criticized for being underdeveloped; the four trapped men exposit their backstories quickly before fading from the plot, leaving little room for deeper engagement [24]. Some reviewers found the use of Jo Martin's pre-First Doctor timeline to establish emotional investment in Abena to be problematic, as it felt like an artificial manipulation of canon for dramatic effect [24]. The finale's exploration of story-as-power was widely appreciated, but the preceding build-up was seen by some as suffering from overly broad focus [24]. Overall, the consensus was that the episode was a "lovable mess" that succeeded in its ambition but faltered in its execution [2].

Public and fan reception mirrored this critical ambivalence, characterized by strong enthusiasm mixed with analytical debate. Social media reactions were overwhelmingly positive in the immediate aftermath of the episode's release. Viewers praised the writing of Inua Ellams, with many tweets describing the episode as "Hugo-worthy," "incredible," and "power & heart" [30]. Some fans declared it "the best Doctor Who episode of the last few seasons," signaling a high level of popular acclaim [30]. This enthusiasm suggests that the episode resonated strongly with audiences who appreciate its bold thematic risks and cultural specificity. The episode also generated significant discussion within dedicated fandom media, including podcasts like *The Doctor Who Show*, which produced a special episode analyzing its narrative and themes, confirming its lasting impact on the fan community [35].

However, the lack of formal industry awards and nominations stands as a notable point of analysis. "The Story & the Engine" received zero nominations in the 2025 BAFTA TV and Craft Awards [25]. It was deemed ineligible for the 2025 ceremony because the eligibility period covered programming aired between January 1 and December 31, 2024, meaning it would only be considered for the 2026 awards cycle [25]. Furthermore, the episode was not included among the finalists for the 2025 Hugo Awards in the Best Dramatic Presentation, Short Form category, where only two other *Doctor Who* episodes from the same season were nominated [21]. This absence from official award consideration, despite widespread fan praise suggesting it was a potential contender, may indicate a disconnect between popular acclaim and the more conservative criteria of some award bodies, or it may reflect the subjective and experimental nature of the episode itself [21], [30].

## Cultural Significance and Legacy

The cultural significance of "The Story & the Engine" extends far beyond its place within the *Doctor Who* canon. The episode is widely recognized as a landmark moment for the franchise, representing a significant step towards greater diversity and a more sophisticated engagement with postcolonial and Afrofuturist narratives [2], [31]. By placing a nearly all-Black cast in a prominent role within a major science fiction series, and by centering a story on Nigerian folklore and culture, the episode challenged the traditionally Eurocentric worldview of much of the genre [2]. It was praised for advancing Afrofuturist and postcolonial frameworks, offering a narrative that was not merely inclusive but actively reimagined the possibilities of the *Doctor Who* universe [2]. This move was seen as part of a broader effort to make the show more representative of the diverse world it inhabits, with one reviewer describing it as "Doctor Who made for me," a testament to its resonance with audiences seeking more authentic and varied storytelling [2].

The episode's legacy is intrinsically linked to the work of its writer, Inua Ellams. His contribution injected a fresh, literary, and culturally specific voice into the series, proving that *Doctor Who* could successfully adapt complex, poetry-influenced narratives for a mass audience [7], [22]. Ellams' ability to weave together themes of linguistic erasure, ancestral knowledge, and the sanctity of storytelling demonstrated a new frontier for the show's writers [5], [28]. The success of his debut script paved the way for future writers from diverse backgrounds and signaled a willingness by the production team to take risks on unconventional storytelling formats. The episode's exploration of the Fifteenth Doctor's Black identity was particularly groundbreaking. Rather than framing his race as a source of trauma or vulnerability, the narrative presented it as a source of joy, community, and belonging, a significant departure from the "Last of the Time Lords" trope that has long defined the character [22]. This portrayal, enabled by Ncuti Gatwa's nuanced performance, contributed to a more humanistic and socially conscious interpretation of the iconic hero [22].

In conclusion, "The Story & the Engine" remains a complex and influential chapter in the history of *Doctor Who*. Its legacy is defined by a tension between its visionary thematic ambition and its sometimes flawed execution. It is an episode that pushed the boundaries of the series, tackling difficult subjects like colonial erasure and narrative sovereignty with poetic intensity. While its narrative structure may have alienated some viewers, its cultural impact is undeniable. It expanded the Whoniverse by integrating West African mythology, celebrated the power of marginalized communities, and provided a platform for a new generation of storytellers. Whether viewed as a "lovable mess" or a "revolutionary" experiment, it stands as one of the most discussed and debated episodes of the modern era, a testament to its capacity to provoke thought and conversation. Its unresolved continuity questions, such as the memory discrepancy involving the Fugitive Doctor, add a layer of ongoing intrigue for fans to analyze, ensuring its place as a subject of enduring interest within the *Doctor Who* fandom [33].
