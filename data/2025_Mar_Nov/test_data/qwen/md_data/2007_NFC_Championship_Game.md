# 2007 NFC Championship Game

## Background and Pre-Game Narratives

The 2007 NFC Championship Game, played on Sunday, January 21, 2007, at Soldier Field in Chicago, Illinois, served as the National Football Conference's decisive contest to determine its representative in Super Bowl XLI [11], [12]. The matchup pitted two teams with vastly different trajectories against one another: the Chicago Bears, the top seed in the NFC and North Division champions, and the New Orleans Saints, South Division champions who were seeking their first-ever appearance in the conference's ultimate game [10], [11]. The Bears entered the game with a dominant 13-2 regular season record, having clinched home-field advantage throughout the playoffs [10]. Their path to the NFC Championship was built upon a formidable defense that led the NFL with 44 takeaways during the season, a testament to the aggressive philosophy instilled by head coach Lovie Smith [16], [17]. The team was powered by a potent running game centered on Thomas Jones and a surprisingly effective passing attack led by quarterback Rex Grossman [4], [17]. However, the Bears' journey was not without controversy; their victory over the Seattle Seahawks in the divisional round was marred by a controversial coaching decision from Smith. With just two seconds remaining in regulation, Smith called a timeout after Seahawks players had already begun to leave the field, intending to set up a potential walk-off return from Devin Hester but inadvertently allowing Seattle a chance to attempt a "Hail Mary" pass that was ultimately unsuccessful [7]. Smith later publicly acknowledged the call as "not a good play on my part," highlighting the high-stakes nature of the playoff environment [7].

In contrast, the New Orleans Saints arrived at Soldier Field as the more polarizing of the two finalists. After a remarkable turnaround under head coach Sean Payton, the Saints finished the regular season with a 10-5 record to claim the South Division title and a bye week in the playoffs [8], [10]. Their run to the NFC Championship was viewed with skepticism by some analysts, who questioned whether a young and talented team could maintain focus after such a dramatic improvement [8]. Critics pointed to a concerning trend towards the end of the season, noting that the Saints had "stunk it up three of the last four weeks," raising doubts about their ability to perform under pressure in a deep playoff run [8]. Despite these concerns, the Saints had demonstrated a "talented offense that can strike from any position," making them a dangerous opponent for any team [8]. For the Saints, the opportunity to reach the NFC Championship Game was a historic milestone in itself. It marked the franchise's first-ever appearance in the conference's final, a momentous achievement for a team that had only recently returned to the Superdome after being displaced for the entire 2005 season due to Hurricane Katrina [11], [17]. This backdrop added a layer of emotional resonance to their playoff journey, framing it as a symbol of resilience for the city and organization [11], [21].

The personal narratives surrounding the head coaches were also central to the pre-game storylines. Lovie Smith, the head coach of the Bears, was positioned to make history regardless of the outcome. His leadership had guided the Bears to the top of the NFC standings, but his most significant potential legacy was his role as a pioneer in NFL coaching. Should the Bears win, Smith would become the first African American head coach to lead a team to the Super Bowl, a monumental achievement in the league's long history [15], [17], [25]. This prospect generated significant attention, particularly given the broader context of racial barriers in professional sports leadership. The other side of this narrative was the possibility that Smith would join another coach in this exclusive club. If the Indianapolis Colts defeated the New England Patriots in the simultaneous AFC Championship Game, Tony Dungy would become the first Black head coach to win an AFC title and advance to the Super Bowl [26]. Such a development would create a historic convergence, with two Black head coaches facing each other in the NFL's championship game for the first time in its 41-year history [25], [26]. For Sean Payton, the game represented an opportunity to validate his transformative work with the Saints and silence the critics who questioned his team's consistency [8]. The stage was set for a clash between a dominant, historically-minded powerhouse and a resilient, emotionally-driven team fighting for its place in franchise lore.

## Game Summary and Play-by-Play Analysis

The 2007 NFC Championship Game lived up to its billing as a pivotal clash, delivering a decisive and dominant performance by the Chicago Bears that secured their berth in Super Bowl XLI [12], [21]. The game, played on January 21, 2007, at Soldier Field in Chicago, unfolded under severe winter conditions, with cold rain transitioning into sleet and snow, creating a slippery playing surface that impacted nearly every player on the field [16], [17], [18]. The Bears capitalized on the adverse conditions and the mistakes of their opponents, establishing a commanding lead early and never relinquishing control throughout the afternoon. The final score of 39–14 told a story of overwhelming Bears dominance and a faltering Saints offense [3], [11], [13].

The opening quarter saw the Bears establish their identity, combining their punishing running game with timely special teams play. The first major blow came on the game's second drive when the Bears marched down the field, culminating in a 19-yard field goal by kicker Robbie Gould, giving Chicago an early 3-0 lead [13], [17]. The Saints responded, but their drive ended with a missed 47-yard field goal attempt by Billy Cundiff, a costly miss that prevented them from tying the score [13]. The Bears then struck again, extending their lead with a second field goal, this one from 24 yards out, pushing the score to 6-0 [13], [17]. The tide began to turn decisively in the second quarter as the Bears' defense began to prey on the Saints' offense. A critical turnover occurred when Charles Tillman stripped the ball from Marques Colston, and the Bears recovered the fumble [19]. This forced turnover set up another Robbie Gould field goal, this time from 43 yards, increasing the Chicago lead to 9-0 [13], [17]. The Saints managed to get on the board late in the half, but a short touchdown reception by Marques Colston from Drew Brees was followed by a missed extra point, leaving the score at 9-6 in favor of the Bears at halftime [3], [13].

The Bears' defensive prowess continued unabated into the third quarter, where they established a seemingly insurmountable lead. The first major blow of the period was a safety awarded to Chicago after Drew Brees was penalized for intentional grounding in his own end zone [3], [17], [19]. This play shifted the score to 11-6, a crucial momentum swing that immediately deflated the Saints' spirits. The Bears' offense then took center stage. On their next possession, they embarked on a decisive 85-yard, five-play drive that concluded with a 33-yard touchdown pass from Rex Grossman to Bernard Berrian, further extending the lead to 18-6 [17], [18], [19]. Just as the Saints appeared to be mounting a comeback, Reggie Bush exploded on a play that momentarily changed the complexion of the game. He took a short slant pass from Brees and turned it into an electrifying 88-yard touchdown run, cutting the deficit to 18-14 and injecting new life into the Saints' effort [3], [15]. However, this moment of hope was quickly extinguished. Following the kickoff, Adrian Peterson stripped Michael Lewis of the ball on a return, and the Bears recovered at the Saints' 30-yard line [19]. The Bears capitalized on this gift, driving down the field before sealing the momentum with a 43-yard field goal by Robbie Gould, shifting the score to 23-14 and effectively putting the game away [13], [17].

The fourth quarter was a coronation for the Bears' defense and a frustrating finale for the Saints. The Bears wasted no time in adding to their lead. On their first drive of the quarter, Cedric Benson burst through the line for a 12-yard touchdown run, making the score 30-14 [13], [17], [19]. The Saints, desperate for a spark, committed another critical error. On a subsequent drive, a penalty on a punt gave the Bears excellent field position, and they needed only a few plays to capitalize. Thomas Jones punched in his second touchdown of the day from a mere 2 yards out, increasing the margin to 37-14 and all but ending the game [13], [17], [18]. The final score of the game was capped off by another field goal from Robbie Gould, this one from 24 yards, bringing the total points for the Bears to 39 and the final margin of victory to 39-14 [13], [17]. The Saints managed to add a single field goal in the final moments of the game, securing their final score of 14, but it was too little, too late [13]. The game summary by quarter reflects this decisive performance: Chicago scored 3 points in the first quarter, 13 in the second, 7 in the third, and 16 in the fourth, while New Orleans failed to register any points in the final period [3], [13].

## Statistical Breakdown and Key Performances

The statistical sheet from the 2007 NFC Championship Game provides a clear and unequivocal picture of the factors that dictated the outcome: the Chicago Bears' suffocating defense and efficient offense overwhelmed the New Orleans Saints, whose struggles with turnovers and execution proved fatal [13], [19]. The most telling statistic of the game was the stark disparity in turnovers. The Bears' defense forced four takeaways from the Saints—three fumbles and one interception—which directly translated into 17 points for Chicago [16], [19], [23]. These turnovers were not isolated incidents but rather a symptom of the Bears' relentless defensive pressure, a hallmark of a unit that led the NFL with 44 takeaways during the regular season [16], [17]. In stark contrast, the Saints themselves committed four turnovers, contributing significantly to their downfall [19], [23]. Another critical statistical advantage for the Bears was their control of the clock. Chicago held the ball for 35 minutes and 15 seconds compared to the Saints' 24 minutes and 45 seconds, a substantial difference in time of possession that limited the opportunities for New Orleans' offense and allowed the Bears to dictate the pace of the game [13], [19].

On the offensive side, the Bears showcased a balanced attack that was difficult for the Saints to contain. The ground game, spearheaded by Thomas Jones, was particularly effective. Jones rushed for 123 yards on 19 carries and scored two touchdowns, including a crucial 2-yard run that gave Chicago a 16-0 halftime lead [13], [17], [18]. His physical presence wore down the Saints' defense and opened up opportunities for the passing game. The passing attack, led by Rex Grossman, was efficient and poised. Grossman completed 11 of 26 passes for 144 yards and a touchdown, throwing without an interception [13]. His 33-yard touchdown pass to Bernard Berrian in the third quarter was a pivotal play that shifted the momentum firmly in Chicago's favor [13], [17], [19]. Special teams also played a crucial role, with kicker Robbie Gould proving to be reliable and accurate despite the terrible weather conditions. Gould made three field goals from distances of 19, 24, and 43 yards, contributing 9 of the Bears' 39 points [13], [17], [23].

The New Orleans Saints' offensive performance was a study in contrasts, highlighted by flashes of brilliance overshadowed by fundamental errors. Quarterback Drew Brees, the reigning NFL MVP, struggled mightily under pressure from a disciplined Bears defense. He completed only 27 of 49 passes for 354 yards, threw one interception, and was sacked three times for a loss of 35 yards [13]. While he found receivers for two touchdown passes—one a 13-yarder to Marques Colston and the other an electrifying 88-yarder to Reggie Bush—the damage was done elsewhere on the field [3], [13]. The running game, typically a strength for the Saints, was largely neutralized. Reggie Bush was the lone bright spot, gaining 132 yards on seven receptions and accounting for 139 of the team's 375 total net yards [13]. However, the rest of the offense was ineffective, managing only 56 rushing yards on 12 attempts and converting a dismal 1 of 10 third-down conversions [2], [13]. The team's inability to sustain drives and protect the quarterback led to the costly turnovers that defined the game.

## Pivotal Moments and Momentum Shifts

While the final score of 39-14 suggested a one-sided affair, the 2007 NFC Championship Game was shaped by several critical moments that punctuated the flow of play and determined its ultimate trajectory. The most significant of these was the series of events that unfolded in the third quarter, a period that transformed a competitive game into a rout. The sequence began with a spark from the New Orleans Saints. Early in the quarter, the Saints drove deep into Bears territory, and Drew Brees connected with Reggie Bush on a short slant pattern [15]. What happened next was a moment of pure brilliance. Bush broke free of tacklers and sprinted 88 yards untouched for a touchdown, a play that electrified the visiting crowd and cut Chicago's lead to 18-14 [3], [15], [17]. This explosive play temporarily altered the momentum, suggesting that the Saints could overcome the Bears' defensive pressure and challenge for the lead.

However, this moment of hope for New Orleans was incredibly short-lived, as the next play completely reversed the momentum. Immediately following the kickoff, the Bears' defense swarmed the New Orleans return unit. On the play, Adrian Peterson stripped the ball from Michael Lewis, and the Bears recovered the fumble at the Saints' 30-yard line [19]. This forced turnover, coupled with a penalty on a punt return, gave the Bears exceptional field position deep in enemy territory [19]. The Bears' offense capitalized on this gift, driving methodically down the field. The play was emblematic of the game's overarching theme: the Saints' inability to hold onto the ball. Head coach Sean Payton attempted to challenge the fumble recovery via video review, but the call on the field was upheld, confirming the turnover and sealing the Saints' fate [19]. This back-to-back exchange—a huge gain for the Saints followed by a catastrophic loss for them—was the definitive pivot point of the game.

Another crucial momentum shift occurred much earlier, in the second quarter, when the Bears were awarded a safety. The play happened after a series of events that showcased the chaotic nature of the game, exacerbated by the slippery turf [18], [19]. As the Saints attempted to move the ball, pressure from the Bears' defense led to a critical mistake. Drew Brees, under duress, stepped into his own end zone and was penalized for intentional grounding [3], [17], [19]. The safety awarded Chicago a single point, shifting the score from 11-6 to 13-6 in favor of the Bears [13]. This play was strategically vital because it occurred just after the Saints had finally crossed the goal line to score, preventing them from tying the game and instead providing the Bears with a lead they would never relinquish. The timing of the safety was perfectly orchestrated to kill the Saints' momentum and restore the Bears' confidence. It was a textbook example of how a small detail, a penalty, could have a disproportionately large impact on the game's outcome.

Beyond these major swings, the game was characterized by a steady stream of smaller, yet equally important, plays that chipped away at the Saints' resolve. The aforementioned fumble recoveries by Charles Tillman and Israel Idonije were instrumental in setting up scoring opportunities for the Bears [17], [19]. The defense, led by players like Brian Urlacher, Tank Johnson, and Charles Tillman, consistently disrupted the Saints' rhythm, forcing hurried throws and creating confusion in the backfield [5]. The physical toll of the harsh weather conditions also played a role in these smaller moments. Players from both teams frequently slipped on the gashed turf, leading to incomplete passes and lost yardage [18], [19]. While this adversity affected everyone, the Bears' more disciplined execution and superior conditioning allowed them to handle the conditions better than the Saints, who seemed to struggle with footing throughout the afternoon [18]. These cumulative small advantages, combined with the two massive momentum shifts, created a narrative of a team that was simply too strong, too prepared, and too opportunistic for its opponent on that particular day.

## Broader Context and Playoff Implications

The 2007 NFC Championship Game was not merely a standalone contest; it was a crucial chapter in the larger story of the 2006–2007 NFL playoffs, with profound implications for the future of both the participating teams and the league itself. For the Chicago Bears, the 39-14 victory was far more than just a win; it was the culmination of a long and arduous journey back to the pinnacle of professional football. The triumph secured their berth in Super Bowl XLI, marking their first appearance in the league's championship game in 21 years [15], [21], [23]. The last time the Bears had reached the Super Bowl was in the 1985 season, a legendary campaign immortalized by a dominant defense and a powerful offense that captured the hearts of a nation [17], [21]. By defeating the New Orleans Saints, the 2006 Bears squad, led by head coach Lovie Smith, officially returned to the grand stage, reigniting the hopes of a fanbase yearning for another taste of glory [12]. The victory was celebrated not just as a sporting achievement but as a symbolic reclamation of the franchise's storied past [17].

Simultaneously, the game's conclusion had immediate ramifications in the concurrent AFC Championship Game. As the Bears were celebrating their victory in Chicago, news spread from Indianapolis that the city's own team, the Indianapolis Colts, was engaged in a historic and dramatic battle against the New England Patriots [26]. The Colts were locked in a seesaw war, having squandered a 21-3 lead and trailing by 18 points in the fourth quarter [15], [26]. However, under the guidance of Peyton Manning, the Colts mounted an incredible comeback, ultimately winning the game 38-34 to secure their own trip to Super Bowl XLI [20], [26]. This meant that the two conference champions would meet in Miami on February 4, 2007, for the NFL title [26]. The convergence of these two outcomes was a defining moment for the 2007 postseason, setting the stage for a highly anticipated matchup between the high-powered Colts offense and the formidable Bears defense.

The fallout from the NFC Championship Game was devastating for the New Orleans Saints. Their 39-14 drubbing was a humbling end to a season that had seen them achieve a remarkable turnaround under Sean Payton [8]. The loss confirmed many of the skeptics' fears, with post-game analysis describing the Saints as being "overwhelmed by the occasion" [21]. The defeat exposed the vulnerabilities of a team that, despite its talent, lacked the experience and mental toughness required to compete against a well-oiled playoff machine in a hostile environment [8]. For the Saints, the game served as a painful lesson about the gap between a successful regular season and sustained success in the postseason. It underscored the challenges they still faced in building a dynasty and silencing the critics who doubted their ability to perform under the brightest lights [8]. The loss was particularly poignant given the context of their recent history, as the team was still operating out of the Louisiana Superdome and working to rebuild the city's spirit after the devastation of Hurricane Katrina [11], [17]. Their failure to advance to the Super Bowl was a setback for the entire organization and community that had rallied behind them.

For the league, the 2007 playoffs, and this championship game in particular, were notable for their compelling human-interest stories. The emergence of Lovie Smith as a legitimate Super Bowl contender was a significant storyline, but the truly historic narrative unfolded in the aftermath of the AFC Championship Game. When news broke that Tony Dungy had led the Colts to victory, it was revealed that he and Lovie Smith were now the first two African American head coaches to ever reach the Super Bowl [15], [25], [26]. This historic convergence was a watershed moment for the NFL, representing a tangible step forward in diversity and inclusion within the highest echelons of league leadership. The fact that two Black coaches would face each other in the Super Bowl was a powerful symbol of progress, a story that transcended the gridiron and resonated deeply across the country [15], [25]. The 2007 NFC Championship Game, therefore, was not just a contest to determine the NFC champion; it was a pivotal event that helped set the stage for one of the most culturally significant Super Bowls in history.

## Legacy and Historical Significance

The legacy of the 2007 NFC Championship Game extends far beyond the final score of 39-14. For the Chicago Bears, the victory cemented a triumphant return to the Super Bowl era after a 21-year absence, a moment that was rich with historical weight and emotional resonance [15], [21], [23]. The team's performance was a testament to the power of a balanced roster, featuring a punishing defense that forced four turnovers and a dynamic offense capable of executing in the worst of conditions [16], [19]. The win was a validation of the team's identity and a continuation of the legacy forged by the legendary 1985 championship squad [17]. Players like Brian Urlacher emphasized the immense responsibility they felt to live up to the standards set by that previous team, making the victory even more meaningful [17]. The celebration at Soldier Field was not just a party for a playoff win; it was a coronation for a franchise reclaiming its rightful place among the NFL's elite [5]. The game marked the apex of the 2006 season, a year that saw the Bears rise from a solid team to a legitimate contender, driven by the strategic acumen of their head coach and the unyielding determination of their players.

Perhaps the most indelible aspect of the game's legacy lies in its role as a catalyst for a historic moment in the NFL's social fabric. The victory propelled head coach Lovie Smith into a position of unprecedented prominence. By guiding the Bears to the Super Bowl, he became the first African American head coach in NFL history to achieve this feat [15], [17], [25]. This was a monumental achievement, shattering a glass ceiling that had existed since the league's inception and inspiring countless individuals aspiring to leadership roles in professional sports. The significance of this accomplishment was magnified hours later when it was announced that his counterpart in Super Bowl XLI, Tony Dungy of the Indianapolis Colts, had also reached the championship game, becoming the first Black head coach to win an AFC title [15], [26]. The resulting matchup between Smith and Dungy was a historic first in the NFL's 41-year history, making them the first two African American head coaches to face each other in the league's ultimate game [25], [26]. This convergence of history was celebrated widely and framed as a landmark moment for equality and representation in American sports.

For the New Orleans Saints, the game represents a bittersweet memory of a season filled with promise but ultimately defined by disappointment. Their 10-6 record and division title were a testament to the successful rebuilding efforts undertaken by general manager Mickey Loomis and head coach Sean Payton, but their loss in the NFC Championship Game highlighted the challenges of translating regular-season success into playoff dominance [8], [11]. The defeat was a sobering reminder of the mental and physical fortitude required to compete for a Lombardi Trophy. Despite the loss, the very fact that the Saints reached the NFC Championship Game was a monumental achievement in their 40-year franchise history [11], [17]. It was a statement that the team, and the city it represented, was back on the national stage. The game was played against the backdrop of Hurricane Katrina's lingering effects, and the Saints' playoff run was seen as a source of pride and healing for a community still rebuilding [11], [21]. The memory of the 39-14 loss will always be paired with the memory of the incredible journey that preceded it, a journey that laid the groundwork for future successes for the franchise.

Ultimately, the 2007 NFC Championship Game is remembered as a classic example of a team fulfilling its potential on the biggest stage. The Chicago Bears, armed with a suffocating defense, a powerful running game, and a coach making history, systematically dismantled a talented but ultimately less-resilient New Orleans Saints team. The game's legacy is multifaceted: it is the story of a franchise's return to glory, a historic milestone for diversity in sports leadership, and a cautionary tale for a team that fell just short of its ultimate goal. The images of Lovie Smith holding the George Halas Trophy and the anticipation of the historic Super Bowl matchup with Tony Dungy continue to define the place of this game in NFL lore [5], [25]. It stands as a pivotal moment in the 2006-2007 season, a game that decided a conference and helped shape the narrative of the entire postseason.
