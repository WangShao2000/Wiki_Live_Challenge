# 2025 Pennsylvania Supreme Court Retention Election

## Background and Electoral Process

The 2025 Pennsylvania Supreme Court election was not a competitive race between opposing candidates but rather a nonpartisan retention election held on November 4, 2025 [10], [19]. This electoral mechanism requires judges who have completed an initial ten-year term to stand for voter approval before serving another full term [36], [37]. In this process, voters across the state were presented with individual yes-or-no ballots for each justice up for retention, without any party labels appearing next to their names [12], [21]. The election was part of the broader 2025 municipal election cycle, which also included local races and other judicial retention contests [4], [16]. The nonpartisan format is designed to focus public evaluation on a judge's performance rather than their political affiliation, a shift from the partisan elections that began in 1850 and were replaced by the current system under the 1968 state constitution [10].

The specific justices facing retention in 2025 were all Democrats whose terms were set to expire in January 2026 [35], [36]. They had been originally elected in a historic partisan sweep in 2015, which flipped the ideological balance of the seven-member court from Republican to Democratic control for the first time in its history [18], [22]. Their successful retention in 2025 would secure them a second consecutive ten-year term, extending their service until January 2036, subject to the state's mandatory retirement age of 75 [1], [37]. The legal framework for this process is established in Article V of the Pennsylvania Constitution, specifically Sections 13(a) and 15(b) [38], [42]. Section 13(a) mandates that initial judicial terms are filled via partisan election, while Section 15(b) governs the retention process, requiring a justice to file a declaration of candidacy for retention by the first Monday of January preceding their term's expiration to appear on the ballot [37], [42]. If a majority of voters cast a "yes" vote for retention, the justice serves a new regular ten-year term; if a "no" vote prevails, the justice is removed from office [37], [42].

The stakes of the 2025 retention election were exceptionally high due to the precarious composition of the Pennsylvania Supreme Court. A 5–2 Democratic majority already existed, meaning the retention of the three incumbent Democratic justices was critical to maintaining this status quo [1], [16]. Failure to retain even one of the three would have triggered a chain of events that could have fundamentally altered the court's function. According to state law, a governor appointed temporary replacement, but this appointment required confirmation by a two-thirds supermajority of the state Senate [13], [19]. Given that the Pennsylvania Senate was controlled by Republicans, securing such a supermajority for a Democratic appointee was highly improbable [13]. Consequently, a single failed retention could have resulted in an indefinite vacancy, reducing the court to four members and creating a potential 2–2 ideological deadlock [1], [36]. Such a deadlock would have effectively stalled the court's ability to issue binding opinions on critical issues, including pending cases on election administration and abortion rights, thereby halting rulings on matters of profound state and national significance [16]. Analysts warned that this scenario could have paralyzed the judiciary's power to act on urgent legal challenges [1]. Therefore, the coordinated anti-retention campaign sought to remove all three Democratic justices simultaneously, aiming to guarantee a new partisan landscape on the court in the subsequent partisan election scheduled for 2027 [17], [20]. The retention of all three justices was thus essential to preserving the existing five-to-two Democratic majority and ensuring the court could continue its work without being bogged down by procedural paralysis [16], [36].

## The Incumbent Justices

The three incumbent Democratic justices who faced the 2025 retention election possessed diverse professional backgrounds and distinct judicial profiles, yet collectively represented the court's dominant party faction. Each had served a decade since their initial partisan election in 2015, bringing extensive legal and judicial experience to the state's highest court [22], [24]. Their careers spanned private practice, appellate courts, and administrative leadership roles within the Philadelphia and Allegheny County judicial systems, providing a deep reservoir of expertise that shaped their perspectives on the bench [25], [28], [31].

Justice Christine L. Donohue, born on December 25, 1952, in Coaldale, Pennsylvania, brought a rich background in civil litigation and community service to her role on the court [31], [32]. Before her election to the Superior Court in 2007, she practiced law for 27 years as a personal injury attorney and commercial litigator in Allegheny County, holding positions at several prominent firms [31], [35]. Her tenure on the Superior Court, where she authored or participated in over 7,000 decisions, was marked by a commitment to legal ethics and discipline; she served as chairwoman of the Pennsylvania Judicial Conduct Board and on the Disciplinary Board of the Supreme Court of Pennsylvania [31], [32]. Her ascent to the Supreme Court in 2015 marked a culmination of a distinguished legal career grounded in practical courtroom experience. Donohue's judicial legacy is defined by her landmark 2024 opinion in *Allegheny Reproductive Health v. Pennsylvania Department of Human Services*, where she authored a 219-page decision reviving Pennsylvania’s Equal Rights Amendment to strike down the state’s Medicaid abortion ban, establishing that denying coverage for abortions constitutes unconstitutional sex discrimination [21]. This ruling made her a primary target for critics of the court's stance on reproductive rights [8]. Critically, Justice Donohue is subject to the state's mandatory retirement age of 75, which she will reach in late 2027, making her seat the next point of contention in a partisan election scheduled for that year [1], [6], [41].

Justice Kevin M. Dougherty, a native of Philadelphia County, brought a career deeply rooted in the city's legal and judicial institutions to the Supreme Court [7]. He earned his B.A. from Temple University and his J.D. from Antioch School of Law before beginning his legal career as an assistant district attorney in Philadelphia [24], [25]. His judicial journey saw him appointed to the Philadelphia Court of Common Pleas in 2001, where he later won election to a full 10-year term [24]. During his tenure on the Common Pleas Court, he held significant administrative roles, serving as Administrative Judge of the Family Court from 2005 to 2014 and the Trial Division from 2014 to 2015 [24], [25]. As Administrative Judge of Family Court, he spearheaded numerous initiatives aimed at improving access to justice for vulnerable populations, including the Cross-Over Program for youth transitioning out of the juvenile system, the Adolescent Medicine Initiative, and the Expungement Initiative [25]. He also championed statewide efforts like the Autism and the Courts initiative and the Statewide Behavioral Health Initiative [26]. His concurrence in the 2024 abortion rights case demonstrated his support for the outcome—upholding abortion providers' standing—but revealed a more cautious approach to declaring a fundamental right to abortion under the state constitution, distinguishing his reasoning from that of some colleagues [26]. Dougherty's background reflects a focus on procedural fairness and administrative reform within the judicial system [7].

Justice David N. Wecht, elected to the court in 2015, possesses a distinguished academic pedigree and a unique career path that includes both medicine and law [29]. A 1984 graduate of Yale University summa cum laude, he earned his J.D. from Yale Law School, where he served as Notes Editor of The Yale Law Journal [28]. Prior to his judicial appointments, he worked as a clerk for U.S. Circuit Judge George MacKinnon on the D.C. Circuit and practiced law at prestigious firms in Washington, D.C., and Pittsburgh [28], [30]. His judicial career began on the Fifth Judicial District's Court of Common Pleas in 2003, where he later served as Administrative Judge of the Family Division from 2009 to 2011, implementing reforms like the Unified Family Court [30]. He then moved to the Superior Court in 2012 before his election to the Supreme Court in 2015 [28]. Wecht's participation in events hosted by the Federalist Society, an organization known for promoting conservative and libertarian principles, has been noted, though the society clarifies that such listings do not imply endorsement of any specific legal views [27]. Nonetheless, his elite education and experience reflect a traditionalist approach to jurisprudence. On the Supreme Court, he joined the majority in pivotal cases, including the 2018 ruling against partisan gerrymandering and the 2020 decision upholding expanded mail-in ballot access during the COVID-19 pandemic [9], [26].

## The Campaign and Its Financial Scale

The 2025 Pennsylvania Supreme Court retention election rapidly transformed from a routine administrative process into a highly politicized and financially intensive battle for the soul of the state's judiciary, attracting unprecedented national attention and spending [1]. It became the most expensive judicial retention race in Pennsylvania's history, shattering previous records with total expenditures exceeding $14 million by late October 2025 [8], [15]. Another analysis noted that as of late October, spending surpassed $15 million, with Democrats outspending Republicans by a ratio of approximately four-to-one [2], [5]. By early November, outside groups alone had spent over $8 million, with projections suggesting the final tally could reach $10 million before Election Day [11], [18]. This massive influx of capital underscored the perception among national political actors that state supreme courts had become decisive battlegrounds for determining the outcomes of major social and political issues [8].

The campaign was characterized by starkly contrasting narratives and a clear division in funding sources. The pro-retention forces, led by Democratic-aligned groups and liberal organizations, framed the vote as a defense of judicial independence against external political pressure [2], [5]. Their messaging emphasized the protection of fundamental rights, particularly abortion access, and the integrity of the democratic process [8]. Central to their argument was the court's 2024 ruling in *Allegheny Reproductive Health v. Pennsylvania Department of Human Services*, which Justice Donohue authored to strike down restrictions on Medicaid-funded abortions, a decision that was widely celebrated by reproductive rights advocates [8], [21]. Pro-retention ads highlighted this ruling alongside the court's repeated upholds of voting rights protections, such as allowing mail-in ballot drop boxes and counting ballots postmarked by Election Day but received up to three days later during the COVID-19 pandemic [9], [14]. A powerful theme running through their messaging was a warning against the undue influence of wealthy interests, encapsulated in slogans like "billionaires trying to buy our independent and fair courts" [6]. Major funders of this side included the Democratic National Committee (DNC), the American Civil Liberties Union (ACLU), the Democratic Legislative Campaign Committee, labor unions, and Planned Parenthood’s super PAC, which collectively invested heavily in defending the incumbents [6], [13], [18].

Conversely, the anti-retention campaign was aggressively funded and strategically orchestrated by conservative billionaires and national Republican entities, primarily targeting the three Democratic justices for their controversial rulings [8], [12]. The principal financial backer was conservative billionaire Jeff Yass, whose network of groups, including Commonwealth Partners, funneled millions into the race [12], [13]. These efforts were often channeled through the Republican State Leadership Committee (RSLC), which committed to spending seven figures in 2025 to influence these specific races, anticipating the court's crucial role in the next redistricting cycle [17]. The campaign's messaging strategy relied heavily on discrediting the justices by selectively interpreting or outright misrepresenting their past decisions [11], [41]. One of the most prominent tactics involved sending misleading mailers that falsely claimed the justices had enabled a "stolen election" for Joe Biden in 2020 by upholding a three-day extension for receiving mail-in ballots—a ruling that the U.S. Supreme Court itself upheld in a 4-4 split [12], [14]. The campaign also distorted the court's 2018 decision striking down a GOP-drawn congressional map as having supported partisan gerrymandering, when in fact the ruling was a condemnation of it [11], [41]. Furthermore, they misrepresented the 2021 reversal of Bill Cosby's conviction, which was based on due process grounds, as evidence of hostility toward sexual assault survivors, using it as justification to "protect women and children" [12], [41]. These aggressive tactics, combined with explicit calls from Republican activists like Scott Presler to vote "no" on all three justices to create a partisan stalemate, fueled a highly charged and contentious campaign environment [14], [20].

## Key Contentious Issues and Judicial Philosophy

The 2025 retention election was a referendum on the judiciary's role in adjudicating some of the most divisive political and social issues of the era. The campaign discourse was dominated by debates over abortion rights, election integrity, and judicial accountability, with the justices' past rulings serving as the primary evidence for both sides' arguments. While the three incumbent justices consistently affirmed their commitment to judicial independence and impartiality, their collective record on the bench provided a clear basis for their respective supporters and detractors [22]. The core of the controversy lay in how their judicial philosophy, as manifested in their opinions, aligned with the contemporary political battles raging across the nation and within Pennsylvania.

A central pillar of the pro-retention campaign was the defense of reproductive rights, anchored by Justice Donohue's groundbreaking 2024 opinion in *Allegheny Reproductive Health v. Pennsylvania Department of Human Services* [21]. In this case, Donohue authored a sweeping 219-page opinion that revived the dormant Equal Rights Amendment and Non-Discrimination Clause of the Pennsylvania Constitution to invalidate the state's Medicaid abortion ban [21]. The ruling established that denying Medicaid coverage for abortions constituted unconstitutional sex-based discrimination, effectively safeguarding access to a portion of reproductive healthcare for low-income Pennsylvanians [21]. This decision was celebrated by abortion rights advocates as a landmark affirmation of a state constitutional right to abortion [8]. For the pro-retention forces, this opinion was the ultimate justification for keeping the justices on the bench, framing the vote as a direct defense of reproductive freedom against a concerted political attack [11]. Conversely, the anti-retention campaign weaponized this very ruling, using it as a primary example of judicial activism and an overreach of judicial power [8]. Opponents argued that the court was legislating from the bench by imposing its policy preferences on the electorate, a sentiment echoed by critics who pointed to the dissenting opinion from Justice Dougherty, who agreed with the outcome but cautioned against the majority's expansive interpretation of the constitution [26].

Another flashpoint that galvanized both sides of the debate was the court's handling of election administration during the COVID-19 pandemic. The justices played a pivotal role in upholding measures that expanded ballot access, most notably in the 2020 case *Pennsylvania Democratic Party v. Boockvar* [26]. In that decision, the court, in which all three justices participated, ruled to uphold the use of county-run mail-in ballot drop boxes and to count ballots that were postmarked by Election Day but received up to three days late [9], [26]. This ruling was instrumental in ensuring the safe and orderly conduct of the 2020 presidential election in Pennsylvania and was later affirmed by the U.S. Supreme Court [14]. For proponents of retention, this was a clear example of the court protecting the integrity of the democratic process and ensuring that valid votes were counted [16]. However, Republican operatives and activists seized upon this ruling to fuel false claims of fraud. Scott Presler, a prominent activist, publicly claimed that the ruling "engineered the stolen election" for Joe Biden, despite state officials confirming that only about 10,000 of the affected ballots were impacted, a fraction of Biden's 80,000-vote margin in the state [14]. This narrative, amplified by anti-retention mailers, sought to paint the justices as politically motivated actors who undermined public trust in elections [41].

Beyond these specific issues, the broader philosophical divide centered on the principle of judicial restraint versus judicial activism. The anti-retention campaign, backed by figures like Jeff Yass and the RSLC, argued that the justices had consistently favored activist interpretations of the law that aligned with a progressive political agenda [8], [12]. They pointed to a string of decisions that struck down GOP-drawn legislative and congressional maps as unconstitutional partisan gerrymanders, a move that prompted Republican legislators to file impeachment articles against the justices [16]. They also criticized rulings on school funding, malpractice lawsuits, and gig worker classification as examples of the court interfering in areas traditionally left to the legislature [17]. The anti-retention messaging often framed the justices as beholden to special interest groups, with Commonwealth Partners board member Rob Ecklin alleging that trial lawyers and union interests had contributed millions to their prior campaigns to advance their financial agendas [23]. In response, the pro-retention camp, citing the recommendations of the Pennsylvania Bar Association, portrayed the justices as fair and logical jurists dedicated to interpreting the law neutrally [6]. The justices themselves repeatedly asserted their nonpartisan conduct, emphasizing that their personal political views had "absolutely nothing to do" with their rulings and that they had "hung up that partisan title" upon taking the bench [22]. This clash of philosophies, played out through the lens of specific, high-stakes rulings, defined the contours of the 2025 retention election.

## Controversies and Ethical Debates

The 2025 Pennsylvania Supreme Court retention election was not only a battle over judicial ideology but also a period marked by significant controversies and ethical questions that tested the boundaries of judicial conduct and political engagement. The entire campaign unfolded under the watchful eye of the Pennsylvania Judicial Conduct Board, which enforces the state's Code of Judicial Conduct, a set of rules designed to maintain the integrity and impartiality of the judiciary [33], [34]. Despite these rules, which prohibit judges from engaging in certain types of political activity, the campaign featured actions and statements that drew sharp criticism and led to formal complaints, raising concerns about the appearance of impropriety and the potential for judicial influence [23], [34].

The most prominent ethical controversy arose from a joint campaign advertisement featuring Governor Josh Shapiro and the three incumbent justices: Christine Donohue, Kevin Dougherty, and David Wecht [23], [33]. The ad, which aired in the final weeks of the campaign, showed the governor and the justices walking together, with the governor stating, "I'm proud to stand with these three judges who have protected our democracy and stood up for our families." The ad went on to highlight the justices' role in "holding big corporations accountable," referencing the Regional Greenhouse Gas Initiative (RGGI) appeal, a case involving major corporate stakeholders that was still pending before the Supreme Court at the time [33]. This collaboration prompted the Pennsylvania Manufacturers’ Association (PMA) to file a formal ethics complaint with the Judicial Conduct Board on or before October 29, 2025 [23], [33]. The PMA alleged that the joint campaigning violated Chapter 39 of the Pennsylvania Code of Judicial Conduct by indicating potential rulings and referencing ongoing cases, thereby compromising judicial impartiality [33]. PMA President David Taylor stated that the arrangement "looks as though [Shapiro is] attempting to influence jurists who have a case before them," directly challenging the principle of judicial neutrality [33]. The justices' offices declined to comment on the complaint, which placed the judiciary's commitment to impartiality under intense scrutiny [33]. This incident exemplified the delicate balance judges must strike between participating in civic life and avoiding actions that could be perceived as endorsing political candidates or influencing pending litigation.

Beyond the joint campaign ad, the broader campaign atmosphere was fraught with allegations of political interference and bias. Anti-retention activists, including Republican operative Scott Presler and Commonwealth Partners CEO Matt Brouillette, openly advocated for a "no" vote on the retention of all three justices, framing the election as a direct "vote of confidence or no confidence" in the court [14], [23]. Their rhetoric, which linked the justices' rulings to political outcomes like the 2020 presidential election, blurred the lines between judicial review and partisan politics [14]. Furthermore, the widespread dissemination of misleading information by anti-retention groups, such as mischaracterizing the court's 2018 redistricting decision and distorting the reasons behind the Bill Cosby conviction reversal, raised serious concerns about the manipulation of public perception [12], [41]. These tactics not only inflamed political passions but also challenged the public's understanding of the judiciary's proper role. The ethical debate thus extended beyond the actions of the sitting justices to encompass the behavior of outside actors seeking to shape the outcome of the election. The controversy surrounding the campaign activities highlights the increasing difficulty for judges to remain insulated from the intensely partisan climate in which modern judicial elections are conducted, even within a nonpartisan retention framework [10]. The formal ethics complaint served as a stark reminder that the standards of judicial conduct are constantly being tested in an era of hyper-partisanship and deep-pocketed political advocacy.

## Election Outcome and Aftermath

On November 4, 2025, Pennsylvania voters delivered a decisive verdict in the nonpartisan retention election, casting "yes" votes to retain all three incumbent Democratic justices: Christine Donohue, Kevin Dougherty, and David Wecht [1], [2], [5]. The outcome preserved the court's 5-to-2 Democratic majority, preventing the potential for a 2-to-2 deadlock that could have crippled the court's ability to function [16], [36]. Official results, after all counties completed mandatory audits, confirmed the success of the pro-retention campaign [4]. Preliminary tallies indicated that each justice received approximately 60% of the "yes" votes, with some reports citing margins of around 27 percentage points [2], [6]. More specific data indicated retention percentages ranging from the "roughly 60% range" to slightly higher figures, such as 61.4% for Donohue and 63% overall in one unofficial report, though raw vote counts were not officially published, only percentages across different reporting tiers [3], [5], [41]. The Secretary of the Commonwealth Al Schmidt officially certified the results of the 2025 municipal election, including the Supreme Court retention vote, on December 19, 2025, following two pre-certification audits that confirmed the accuracy of the reported outcomes [4].

The victory for the three justices was hailed by supporters as a triumph for judicial independence and the rule of law, validating their stances on abortion rights and election integrity against a well-funded and aggressive opposition campaign [2], [5]. South Philly voter Deborah Dalton articulated a common motivation for retaining the justices, citing national political instability and the need for consistency and stability on the state's highest court [1]. The successful retention ensured that the court would continue to operate with its existing ideological balance, allowing it to proceed with its caseload, which includes critical issues related to election administration and reproductive health [16]. The high level of voter engagement, reflected in a significant increase in turnout to 42.45% of registered voters compared to 36.77% in 2023, underscored the public's keen interest in the judiciary's role in shaping state policy [4].

However, the aftermath of the election immediately shifted focus to the court's future and the looming vacancy created by Justice Donohue's impending mandatory retirement. All three justices secured new ten-year terms, extending their service until January 2036 [37]. Yet, Justice Donohue, who was 72 years old at the time of the election, will reach the state's mandatory retirement age of 75 in late 2027 [1], [41]. When her term expires in 2027, she will be required to step down, triggering a partisan election for her seat [6], [41]. This upcoming election is expected to be fiercely contested, as the seat represents a prime opportunity for Republicans to reclaim a majority on the court and potentially overturn the precedents set by the current 5-2 majority. The 2025 retention election, therefore, can be seen as a temporary reprieve for the Democratic-leaning court. While it successfully defended the status quo in 2025, it also illuminated the intense pressures on the judiciary and foreshadowed the inevitable return of the court to the center of Pennsylvania's political struggles in the near future. The transformation of the retention process into a national spectacle highlighted the vulnerability of the nonpartisan model and set a precedent for future judicial races in the state, likely ensuring that such contests will continue to attract substantial outside funding and fierce political polarization.
