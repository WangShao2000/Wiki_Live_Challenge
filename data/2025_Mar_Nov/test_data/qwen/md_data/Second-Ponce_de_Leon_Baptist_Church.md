# Second-Ponce de Leon Baptist Church

## History and Origins

The Second-Ponce de Leon Baptist Church, situated in Atlanta, Georgia, possesses a complex and deeply rooted history that intertwines the narrative of a major metropolitan church merger with a remarkable act of interfaith preservation during the American Civil War [1], [13]. Its identity is forged from two foundational pillars: the strategic consolidation of established congregations in the early 20th century and its survival against the widespread destruction of Atlanta in 1864 [1], [23]. The church was formally constituted on November 18, 1932, through the merger of three distinct Buckhead-area churches: Second Baptist Church, Ponce de Leon Avenue Baptist Church, and Buckhead Baptist Church [1]. However, the most consequential aspect of this formation was the union between Second Baptist Church, founded in 1854, and Ponce de Leon Avenue Baptist Church, which together created the new entity that would become Second-Ponce de Leon [1], [22]. This act of consolidation marked a pivotal moment, bringing together two influential congregations to establish a new center of worship in the rapidly developing Buckhead neighborhood [1], [3]. The historical marker associated with the church notes September 1, 1854, as a significant date, correctly identifying the founding year of its original component, Second Baptist Church, thereby establishing an unbroken lineage dating back to the mid-19th century [22].

The church’s most celebrated historical event occurred during the Atlanta Campaign of the Civil War in November 1864. At that time, the predecessor congregation, Second Baptist Church, was located downtown and was one of several city churches targeted for destruction by Union forces under General William T. Sherman [13], [30]. In a documented act of profound interfaith cooperation, Father Thomas O’Reilly, the pastor of Immaculate Conception Church, the city's first Catholic church, intervened directly with Union General Henry Slocum [30]. According to historical accounts, Father O’Reilly argued that burning the churches would be "an affront to heaven" and risked mutiny among the predominantly Catholic and Irish soldiers in his command, who held deep reverence for the sacred buildings [30]. His successful plea resulted in Second Baptist Church being spared from the flames that consumed much of the surrounding area [1], [13]. This event is cited as a powerful example of wartime preservation and interfaith dialogue, a legacy that continues to hold significance for the congregation today [1]. Dr. Charles Qualls, the church's associate pastor for pastoral care, confirmed in 2013 that church leaders planned to intentionally discuss Father O’Reilly’s wartime actions and legacy in the months ahead, underscoring the enduring importance of this historical episode to the church's self-understanding [13].

Following its formal establishment in 1932, the church embarked on a period of growth and relocation. It moved to its present location in the Buckhead section of Atlanta, specifically to the Garden Hills neighborhood plan, where it constructed a new home [13], [23]. The cornerstone of the church's physical presence was laid in 1937 with the opening of a new building designed by architect George H. Bond [23]. This structure, located at the intersection of Peachtree Road and East Wesley Road, became the permanent campus for the congregation [4], [23]. The development of this site was part of the broader Garden Hills Company neighborhood plan, a community characterized by curvilinear streets and Tudor and Colonial Revival architecture, which itself was recognized for its historical significance [23]. The church's building, along with other structures in the immediate vicinity, was later included in the Garden Hills Historic District, which was officially listed in the National Register of Historic Places in 1987 [23]. This designation provides external validation of the church's architectural and historical importance within the cityscape of Atlanta. The move to Buckhead solidified the church's position as a central institution in one of Atlanta's most affluent neighborhoods, a status it would maintain and leverage throughout the latter half of the 20th century [15], [19].

## Physical Plant and Cultural Heritage

The physical plant of Second-Ponce de Leon Baptist Church is not merely functional but represents a significant cultural and architectural landmark within the Buckhead/Garden Hills community of Atlanta [2], [23]. The church is consistently identified by its address at 2715 Peachtree Road NE, Atlanta, GA 30305, a location that serves as its primary point of access and a recognizable feature of the neighborhood skyline [6], [15], [34]. The main sanctuary building, a four-story structure completed in phases, houses the worship center, music suite, classrooms, and youth department, forming the heart of the church's daily operations [2]. A multi-phase renovation and expansion project, managed by architect TURNERBATSON, transformed this space into a modern and versatile facility [2]. The campus also includes a Family Life Center and a Children’s Education Building, connected by a new entry structure featuring a large lobby, check-in area, offices, and indoor play areas, reflecting the church's focus on family ministry and community engagement [2]. The entire campus spans a total of 80,000 square feet, demonstrating the scale of the church's infrastructure and its capacity to host a wide range of programs and events [2]. This substantial footprint underscores its long-standing institutional presence and commitment to serving a large and diverse congregation.

A defining feature of the church's architectural heritage is its connection to the Garden Hills Historic District, which was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1987 [23]. The district, developed between 1923 and 1941, encompasses the original Garden Hills Company neighborhood plan and is noted for its cohesive design, including curvilinear streets and prominent civic structures like schools and churches [23]. The inclusion of the Second-Ponce de Leon Baptist Church building within this designated district highlights its contribution to the area's unique character and historical integrity [23]. The church's own building, designed by architect George H. Bond and opened in 1937, exemplifies the architectural styles prevalent in the neighborhood at the time, contributing to the district's collective significance [23]. This recognition provides an external, authoritative assessment of the church's value beyond its religious function, situating it within the broader context of American architectural and urban history.

Beyond its architecture, the church maintains a rich musical heritage centered around its pipe organ. The facility houses a M. P. Moller Pipe Organ, built in 1959 with the opus number 9185 [6]. This instrument is a significant piece of musical equipment, meticulously documented in both the Organ Historical Society Database and the church's official website [6]. The organ features four manuals—Choir, Great, Swell, and Bombarde—as well as a pedal division, providing a vast palette of tonal colors for worship and performance [6]. Notable components include 21-bell cloches and a combination of digital and traditional piped stops, showcasing a blend of classic craftsmanship and modern technology [6]. The expressive enclosures allow for dynamic control over the sound, enhancing the organ's versatility [6]. This instrument is central to the church's worship experience, supporting a tradition of high-quality musical programming. Historical descriptions of the church's services note a sophisticated worship style that contrasted sharply with more traditional rural Baptist practices; rather than relying on an upright piano playing hymns like 'The Old Rugged Cross,' Second-Ponce de Leon featured pipe organ performances of classical composers such as Bach and Tchaikovsky [19]. This emphasis on liturgical music, facilitated by its impressive organ, reflects the church's historical orientation towards a more formal and culturally refined expression of worship, aligning with its status as a prominent institution in an affluent community [19].

## Denominational Affiliation and Institutional Profile

Second-Ponce de Leon Baptist Church is formally affiliated with the Cooperative Baptist Fellowship (CBF) and its corresponding state-level organization, the Cooperative Baptist Fellowship of Georgia [7]. This affiliation is a cornerstone of its theological and organizational identity, positioning it firmly within the more progressive and ecumenically-minded wing of Southern Baptist life. The church operates under its own set of formal bylaws and personnel policies, yet it actively participates in the collaborative networks fostered by the CBF, engaging in global and domestic missions, including disaster response efforts [5], [7]. This partnership model emphasizes mutual support, information sharing, and collective action to strengthen and transform contexts both locally and internationally [5]. The church’s leadership structure includes a Leadership Council and various standing boards, committees, and councils, with the Senior Pastor serving ex-officio, ensuring a system of governance that balances centralized authority with shared responsibility [7].

Throughout its history, the church has cultivated a formidable institutional profile characterized by significant financial power, social influence, and a strong commitment to mission work. As of May 1976, the church reported having approximately 5,000 members and 3,000 weekly worshipers, cementing its status as a large and active congregation [19]. It was frequently described as 'perhaps the most affluent Baptist church in the Southeast,' a characterization supported by the composition of its congregation, which included prominent and wealthy individuals such as Georgia Governor George Busbee, two Federal judges, and a host of influential businessmen [19]. This demographic profile endowed the church with considerable social capital and financial resources. During that same period, its annual budget was estimated at $1.2 million, with a remarkable half of that sum—$600,000—allocated directly to mission work, both within Atlanta and globally [19]. This consistent and substantial investment in missions appears to be a core element of the church's identity and purpose.

This pattern of generous financial support continued and even intensified under the leadership of subsequent pastors. Under the tenure of Rev. Dr. C. Robert Marsh from 1978 to 1993, the church became the Georgia Baptist Convention’s most reliable supporter of the Cooperative Program and consistently led the state in missionary giving [25], [35]. The church’s general budget grew substantially during his pastorate, reaching $2 million in 1982 and escalating to $3 million by 1987 [25], [35]. Furthermore, the church raised an additional $2 million toward the construction of a $6.5 million Family Life Center, which was completed in 1990, demonstrating its capacity for large-scale capital projects [25], [35]. While specific financial figures for the 21st century are not provided in the sources, the church's ongoing participation in denominational funding and its extensive community outreach programs suggest that its commitment to mission remains robust [5], [7]. The church's influence extends beyond its financial contributions; it has been a hub for nurturing future Baptist leaders, such as Monroe Swilley Jr., who served as its senior pastor before becoming president of Atlanta Baptist College (now Mercer University Atlanta) [12]. This legacy of leadership development further cements its status as a vital and influential institution within the Cooperative Baptist tradition. More recently, the church has demonstrated its continued relevance by hosting the 2025 Annual Gathering of Baptist Women in Ministry (BWIM), an event that will also feature the release of the 2025 State of Women in Baptist Life Report, signaling its ongoing engagement in contemporary conversations about gender and leadership within the denomination [28].

## Pastoral Leadership and Governance

The leadership of Second-Ponce de Leon Baptist Church has been shaped by a succession of senior pastors whose tenures reflect the church's evolution and its engagement with broader trends in American Christianity. Each leader has contributed to the church's institutional strength, theological direction, and community impact. The records provide detailed insights into the careers of several key pastors, illustrating a trajectory of growth, innovation, and adaptation. One of the most significant figures in the church's modern history was Russell H. Dilday Jr., who served as senior pastor immediately prior to his election as the sixth president of Southwestern Baptist Theological Seminary in 1978 [38]. During his time at the church, Dilday was already a prominent figure in denominational leadership, serving as a trustee of the Home Mission Board, a member of the executive committee of the Georgia Baptist Convention, and second vice president of the Southern Baptist Convention [38]. His profile exemplifies the church's elite status; he was described as "well-spoken, easygoing," and "at home in any level of society" [19]. His leadership was characterized by a sophisticated approach to ministry, employing advanced technology such as computerized member tracking, closed-circuit television for instruction, and statewide cable broadcasts of his Sunday sermons [19]. He was succeeded by Rev. Dr. James 'Dock' Hollingsworth, who served as senior pastor from 2013 until his appointment at Big Canoe Chapel [24], [26]. Hollingsworth's tenure saw the church navigate the economic challenges of the COVID-19 pandemic, a period during which he publicly addressed the church's financial resilience, stating that prior reserves had been sufficient to weather a 30% drop in giving in March 2020 [29]. His decade-long leadership was marked by an active pulpit ministry, with numerous sermons published online, indicating a focus on contemporary ethical and spiritual issues [24].

Another transformative leader was Rev. Dr. C. Robert Marsh, who served as senior pastor from July 1978 to March 1993, a tenure spanning 15 years [25], [35]. Known affectionately by longtime members as "Brother Bob," Marsh's leadership was defined by a renewed passion for missions, which he prioritized as his "first love" [35]. Under his guidance, the church solidified its reputation as a leading force in the Cooperative Baptist Fellowship, becoming the Georgia Baptist Convention’s most reliable supporter of the Cooperative Program and consistently ranking first in missionary giving [25], [35]. His administration oversaw a significant increase in the church's general budget, which rose from $2 million in 1982 to $3 million in 1987 [25], [35]. Marsh was also a visionary administrator, overseeing the fundraising and construction of the $6.5 million Family Life Center, a major addition to the campus completed in 1990 [25], [35]. Beyond finances and infrastructure, Marsh was a leader committed to social justice and theological diversity. In 1992, he presided over the ordination of nine women deacons, a groundbreaking step toward greater gender inclusion within the denomination [25], [35]. He also expanded the church's Vietnamese Ministry by hiring a full-time pastor and launching ESL and job-coaching programs, while simultaneously providing office space in its educational wing for Baptist scholars displaced by fundamentalist leadership shifts in other denominational institutions [25], [35]. His resignation in 1993 was attributed to pastoral burnout and exhaustion after a demanding 15-year term [35].

Other notable pastors have also left their mark on the church. Monroe Swilley Jr. served as senior pastor for many years before moving on to become president of Atlanta Baptist College in 1969 [12]. The church was a major financial supporter of the college during its planning stages, highlighting its role in nurturing denominational institutions [12]. After the college merged with Mercer University in 1972, Swilley became vice president of Mercer's Atlanta campus, where the library was eventually named in his honor, a testament to his lasting impact [12]. Another former pastor, Dr. James Cecil Bryant, Jr., was a respected academic and author who served as a professor at Mercer University and was memorialized with a funeral service at the church in 2008 [37]. Robert Willis Hutcherson also served as pastor and distinguished himself with a career in public service, also serving as Chaplain of the Atlanta Police Department and Counselor at Baptist Village of Waycross [36]. ## Ministry, Missions, and Community Engagement

Second-Ponce de Leon Baptist Church engages in a wide array of ministries and missions that extend far beyond its walls, demonstrating a deep commitment to both local and global community service. The church's mission focus is a core tenet of its identity, reflected in its substantial financial allocations and the active involvement of its members [19], [25]. Through its affiliation with the Cooperative Baptist Fellowship (CBF), the church supports a network of global and domestic missions, including disaster response initiatives [5]. This collaborative approach allows the church to amplify its impact, working in partnership with other congregations to inform, equip, and transform contexts worldwide [5]. The church's ministry portfolio is multifaceted, encompassing direct financial support, volunteerism, and the provision of physical space for community-based organizations, all of which underscore its role as a vital civic institution in Atlanta.

The church's engagement with the local Buckhead community is particularly robust. It provides significant support to GHES, an elementary school located within the neighborhood, through volunteer efforts at school events, backing for teachers via the Parent-Teacher Association (PTA), participation in reading programs, and financial assistance for special projects [5]. This partnership illustrates a hands-on approach to education and community enrichment. Similarly, the church is a key supporter of the Buckhead Christian Ministry (BCM), an organization dedicated to preventing homelessness and advancing economic empowerment in Atlanta [5]. Second-Ponce de Leon contributes to BCM through supply drives for emergency assistance and by sponsoring financial education programs [5]. The church also partners with Atlanta Habitat for Humanity, periodically sending teams to participate in local home-building projects aimed at creating affordable housing and revitalizing neighborhoods [5]. Further extending its reach, the church works with the Toco Hills Community Alliance (THCA) by hosting and assisting its mobile food pantry, collecting clothing and shelf-stable food, and participating in the Atlanta Community Food Bank’s annual Hunger Walk/Run to raise funds for hunger relief [5]. These diverse local partnerships highlight a comprehensive strategy for addressing pressing social needs within its immediate geographic area.

One of the most innovative and impactful examples of the church's community engagement is its long-standing partnership with Respite Care Atlanta (RCA), a faith-based Alzheimer’s respite care program [4], [15]. Second-Ponce de Leon Baptist Church serves as the primary campus for RCA, providing its facilities for Club RCA Day Program, which supports individuals living with dementia and offers crucial caregiver support services [15], [16]. The program typically operates on Tuesdays, Wednesdays, and/or Thursdays, offering a safe and enriching environment for its participants [16]. This collaboration is supported by eight other Buckhead congregations, each of which appoints a representative to RCA's Board of Directors and contributes financially and collaboratively to the initiative [4], [33]. The partnership goes beyond mere facility rental; it is a shared mission of compassion and service. The church has also integrated a therapeutic, publicly visible urban botanical garden named Sarahs’ Garden onto its grounds, designed by Atlanta architect Edward L. Daugherty to support RCA programming and named in honor of the co-founders of the Atlanta Alzheimer’s Auxiliary [4]. This demonstrates a creative and thoughtful integration of the church's physical space to serve a specific and important community need.

The church's commitment to missions also includes a history of church planting. In 1946, driven by a vision to expand its reach, the church initiated a mission to plant a new church in North Buckhead [17]. It purchased property on Wieuca Road in 1947 and held its first service on May 9, 1954, giving rise to Wieuca Road Baptist Church [17]. The early growth of this daughter church was attributed to residential expansion in the North Buckhead area and was significantly amplified by the regional television broadcast of its worship services, showcasing the church's willingness to adopt new technologies to spread its message [17]. This act of church planting reflects a core missional imperative to multiply its witness and establish new centers of worship and community. The church also maintains a long-term partnership with The Miami Lighthouse (TML), collaborating for nearly thirty years by sending a team annually to lead a week-long summer camp for approximately 400 students in Overtown and West Homestead, Florida, and by providing financial support for facility repairs [5]. This sustained international mission effort, focused on children in a different metropolitan area, further exemplifies the breadth and depth of the church's global and domestic mission priorities.

## Contemporary Context and Recent Developments

In recent years, Second-Ponce de Leon Baptist Church has navigated the complexities of contemporary religious life, demonstrating both resilience in the face of economic challenges and continued relevance within its denominational and community spheres. The onset of the COVID-19 pandemic presented a significant test of the church's financial stability and operational adaptability. As reported in an Atlanta Journal-Constitution article covering the pandemic's impact on Georgia churches, the church experienced a notable 30% drop in financial giving in March 2020 compared to March 2019 [29]. In addressing this challenge, Senior Pastor Dock Hollingsworth acknowledged the short-term impact but emphasized the church's preparedness, noting that it had built up sufficient reserves to weather the crisis [29]. He explained that the long-term financial outlook would depend heavily on the pace of economic recovery, particularly concerning major donors and their ability to make appreciated asset gifts, such as stocks, which are often timed with year-end giving strategies [29]. This transparent communication from leadership highlighted the church's capacity for prudent financial management and its awareness of the delicate balance between immediate needs and long-term sustainability during a period of unprecedented disruption.

Despite the challenges posed by the pandemic, the church has maintained its active role in the Cooperative Baptist Fellowship (CBF) and broader Atlanta community. The confirmation of the Baptist Women in Ministry (BWIM) 2025 Annual Gathering, scheduled for November 6–8, 2025, and set to take place at the church, signals its continued prominence as a venue for significant gatherings within the denomination [28]. Hosting such an event, especially one that will coincide with the release of the 2025 State of Women in Baptist Life Report, reaffirms the church's commitment to contemporary discussions on leadership, gender, and the evolving role of women in ministry [28]. This engagement ensures that the church remains a participant in and contributor to the ongoing conversation about the future of Baptist life in America. The church's long history of mission work, which dates back decades, continues to be a defining characteristic. For instance, Louise Propst Adamson's ministry, formally sponsored by the church from 1959 until her death in 2025, spanned 50 years and involved providing logistical and financial support for inner-city slum work, distributing household goods, and co-sponsoring a weekly lunch program [39]. This enduring commitment to long-term, grassroots mission work speaks to a deep-seated institutional ethos of service and compassion.

Looking ahead, the church is currently led by a new senior pastor following the departure of Rev. Dr. James Neil "Dock" Hollingsworth in 2023, who accepted a call to lead Big Canoe Chapel [26]. The provided sources do not contain information regarding the identity of the successor pastor, which represents a gap in the available data. However, the church's infrastructure and established programs, such as its partnership with Respite Care Atlanta and its active participation in the CBF, provide a stable foundation for continued ministry. The church's physical campus, with its 80,000-square-foot footprint, its National Register-listed architecture, and its M. P. Moller pipe organ, remains a tangible symbol of its history and cultural significance [2], [6], [23]. These assets, combined with a legacy of influential leadership and a steadfast commitment to mission, position Second-Ponce de Leon Baptist Church to continue its impactful work in the years to come. The church stands as a testament to a century of growth, adaptation, and service, maintaining its role as a central pillar of religious and civic life in the Buckhead community and a significant voice within the Cooperative Baptist tradition.
