# 1982 Los Angeles Rams–Green Bay Packers Game

## Context of the 1982 NFL Season

The 1982 National Football League (NFL) season stands as one of the most tumultuous and unconventional in the history of American professional football, defined almost entirely by an unprecedented labor dispute that fundamentally reshaped the league's structure and operations [4], [6]. The central event of the season was the 1982 NFL players' strike, a work stoppage initiated by the NFL Players Association (NFLPA) that lasted for 57 days, from September 20, 1982, to November 16, 1982 [2], [5]. This industrial action was not a minor disruption; it was the longest players' strike in sports history and had profound consequences, forcing the cancellation of the first seven weeks of the regular season and compelling the league to implement a radical, abbreviated nine-game schedule [2], [3], [4], [5]. The strike was triggered by unresolved collective bargaining issues between the NFLPA, led by executive director Ed Garvey and president Gene Upshaw, and the NFL Management Council, led by Paul Tagliabue (acting for Jack Donlan) [4]. The core disputes centered on player compensation, specifically a proposed $1.6 billion player compensation fund, with the union demanding a five-year payout period and the owners offering a six-year period, along with disagreements over mandatory wage scales and performance bonuses [4]. The impasse led the union to file eight unfair-labor-practice charges, two of which were already upheld by the National Labor Relations Board (NLRB) before the strike began [4].

The cessation of play on September 20, 1982, immediately following a Monday Night Football game between the New York Giants and the Green Bay Packers, sent shockwaves through the league [3], [4]. All training facilities were closed effective that night, and the first regular-season games affected by the strike were scheduled for Thursday, September 23, 1982 [4]. The resulting logistical chaos created a cascade of problems for every team in the league. In response to the absence of their star players, some NFL owners staged non-union games using replacement players [6]. Furthermore, the league was forced to lift reserve list restrictions, allowing injured players who were on the injured reserve list to participate in these replacement games, thereby further complicating roster management [6]. The return of the players was also fraught with tension. By Wednesday, just days before the resumption of play, dozens of striking players had crossed picket lines, including notable names like Danny White of the Dallas Cowboys, Mike Webster and Earnest Jackson of the Pittsburgh Steelers, and Roy Green of the Arizona Cardinals [6]. While those returning by 9 a.m. on Friday would only miss a fraction of their salary due to the cancellation of the final week's games, the atmosphere was one of uncertainty and financial penalty for those who returned early [6].

The tentative agreement that ended the strike on November 16, 1982, allowed for the resumption of regular-season play, but the damage to the original schedule was irreparable [3], [5]. The revised season consisted of nine games for each team, structured in a unique way: two games were played before the strike began, six games were rescheduled to be played on their original dates from the league's pre-strike schedule, and a single final game was played on January 2, 1983, against the team they had played in Week 1 of the cancelled portion of the season [5]. The first full slate of games after the settlement occurred on Sunday, November 21, 1982, creating a new normal where teams were dealing with roster turnover, replacement players, and the psychological effects of the long hiatus [3], [6]. It is within this highly unusual context that all games of the 1982 season must be understood. While the specific game between the Los Angeles Rams and the Green Bay Packers was played on September 12, 1982, making it part of the shortened season, the entire competitive landscape of the year was altered by the strike. The condensed schedule meant that every game carried immense weight, and team dynamics were constantly shifting. For instance, the Rams' loss to the Packers on September 12th was their only loss in a nine-game season, a fact that would have had significant implications for their playoff aspirations under the compressed schedule. Therefore, while the strike did not directly cause the Packers' comeback in this particular game, it provides the essential backdrop for understanding the challenges and pressures faced by both franchises throughout the 1982 campaign.

## Game Summary and Final Score

The game between the Los Angeles Rams and the Green Bay Packers on September 12, 1982, stands as a remarkable chapter in NFL history, primarily noted for the extraordinary second-half rally executed by the Packers [1], [13]. The contest took place at Milwaukee County Stadium in Milwaukee, Wisconsin, serving as the season opener for both teams [7], [8]. The final score was a decisive victory for the Green Bay Packers, who defeated the Los Angeles Rams by a score of 35 to 23 [1], [8], [11], [12]. However, this final tally fails to capture the true narrative of the game, which was a dramatic reversal of fortune. The Packers entered the locker room at halftime trailing 23-0, facing what appeared to be a monumental challenge [1], [13]. Their ability to overcome this 23-point deficit is recognized as the largest comeback in franchise history at the time of the game [13], and it remains a significant achievement in the annals of the organization [14].

The game's story is one of resilience and offensive firepower. After a dominant first half by the Rams, the Packers emerged from the intermission with a renewed sense of purpose and proceeded to dominate the remainder of the contest. They orchestrated five unanswered touchdowns in the second half, effectively dismantling the Rams' defense and seizing control of the game [13]. This remarkable turnaround showcased the leadership of quarterback Lynn Dickey and the dynamic playmaking abilities of running back Eddie Lee Ivery, whose contributions were instrumental in securing the win [7], [13]. The Packers' defense also played a crucial role, holding the Rams scoreless in the second half and limiting them to just four first downs over the final 30 minutes of regulation [13]. This combination of opportunistic offense and tenacious defense transformed a potential season-opening disaster into a memorable and inspirational victory.

The attendance for the game was recorded at 53,694 fans, who witnessed a thrilling display of football [8]. The officiating crew for the match is not specified in the provided sources. The result of this game was particularly significant for the Los Angeles Rams, as it marked their only loss of the abbreviated nine-game season [10]. For the Green Bay Packers, this win served as a powerful statement of intent, demonstrating their capacity to overcome adversity and setting a tone of determination for the rest of the campaign. The victory was not merely a matter of points; it was a testament to coaching strategy, player perseverance, and the unpredictable nature of professional football. The comeback was so complete that it has been cited as a defining moment for the Packers in the 1982 season, a turning point that likely influenced their ultimate success that year [13]. The game's final score of 35-23, therefore, represents the culmination of a historic and unforgettable athletic performance, one that continues to be remembered for its sheer improbability and execution.

## Player Performances and Statistics

The 1982 matchup between the Green Bay Packers and the Los Angeles Rams was a showcase of individual brilliance, particularly on the offensive side of the ball, as key players from both teams stepped up to deliver performances that defined the outcome of the game. For the Packers, the heroics of quarterback Lynn Dickey, the receiving prowess of Paul Coffman, and the bruising power of running back Eddie Lee Ivery were the cornerstones of their historic comeback [7], [13]. For the Rams, the debut of quarterback Bert Jones as a starter was a significant storyline, though he was ultimately outmatched by the surge from Green Bay [7].

Lynn Dickey, the Packers' quarterback, was the undisputed leader of the second-half charge. After a shaky first half in which he struggled with turnovers, Dickey rebounded magnificently, completing 17 of his 27 passes for a total of 237 yards [8], [11], [12]. His passer rating for the game was 88.6, a strong indicator of his effectiveness once he found his rhythm [11], [12]. Dickey's most significant contribution was throwing three touchdown passes, two of which went to his tight end, Paul Coffman [7], [13]. The other touchdown pass was caught by wide receiver James Lofton, demonstrating Dickey's ability to attack the field vertically and horizontally [7], [13]. Despite his heroic performance, Dickey's stat line was marred by three interceptions, reflecting the volatility of his first half [8], [11], [12]. One source suggests he threw two picks and lost two fumbles in the first half alone, indicating a difficult start to the game [7].

Paul Coffman, the Packers' tight end, was the most consistent and impactful offensive weapon in the second half. He was targeted frequently by Dickey and delivered in clutch moments, catching four receptions for a total of 66 receiving yards and scoring two crucial touchdowns [8], [11], [12]. His first touchdown was a 4-yard reception in the third quarter, which ignited the comeback [1], [13]. His second touchdown was a 10-yard reception in the fourth quarter, which put the Packers ahead for the first time and shifted the momentum decisively in their favor [1], [11]. Coffman's reliability as a red-zone target was a critical factor in the Packers' ability to convert their opportunities into points.

Eddie Lee Ivery, the Packers' running back, complemented Dickey's passing game with a powerful and productive ground attack. He carried the ball 17 times for a total of 109 rushing yards, averaging an impressive 6.4 yards per carry [8], [11], [12]. More importantly, he scored two rushing touchdowns, providing the Packers with crucial short-yardage scoring capabilities [8], [12]. His first touchdown was a 3-yard rush in the third quarter, which followed a long Dickey-to-Coffman pass and demonstrated his ability to finish drives on the ground [1], [13]. His second and perhaps most important touchdown was a 27-yard run late in the fourth quarter, which sealed the victory and capped off the historic comeback [1], [11]. Ivery also contributed as a receiver, catching two passes for 32 yards [7].

On the other side of the ball, Bert Jones made his first start for the Los Angeles Rams after being traded from the Baltimore Colts [7]. He showed flashes of promise but was ultimately unable to contain the Packers' offensive onslaught. Jones completed 17 of 31 passes for 202 yards, throwing for one touchdown but also committing two interceptions [8], [12]. One interception was returned by George Cumby to the Green Bay 30-yard line, setting up a scoring opportunity for the Packers [8], [13]. The other interception was thrown near the end of the game, preventing the Rams from mounting a serious comeback attempt [8]. Jones was also sacked four times for a total loss of 36 yards, indicating pressure from the Packers' defense [11]. His performance highlights the transition period the Rams were undergoing with a new quarterback at the helm.

Other notable Rams contributors included running back Eric Dickerson, who scored two rushing touchdowns in the first half to build the initial 23-0 lead, and kicker Mike Lansford, who was perfect on the day, converting three field goals (from 32, 29, and 28 yards) and both extra point attempts for a total of 11 points [11], [12]. On the defensive side for the Rams, linebacker Lawrence Taylor recorded four sacks, showcasing his disruptive ability even against the eventual comeback team [7]. The Rams' defense was dominant in the first half, but their inability to generate any pressure or stops in the second half proved to be their undoing. The contrast between the individual performances, particularly the offensive explosion from the Packers' trio of Dickey, Coffman, and Ivery, perfectly encapsulates why the game is remembered as one of the greatest comebacks in NFL history.

## Legacy and Aftermath

The 35-23 victory by the Green Bay Packers over the Los Angeles Rams on September 12, 1982, transcends the final score, leaving a lasting legacy as one of the most significant comebacks in the franchise's history [1], [13]. At the time, overcoming a 23-point deficit at halftime was the largest comeback in the Packers' storied history, a record that underscored the team's resilience and fighting spirit [13]. This game served as a powerful catalyst for the Packers during the abbreviated 1982 season. After starting the year with a stunning loss, the team regrouped and used the lessons learned from this comeback as a springboard for the rest of the campaign. The victory provided a much-needed boost to team morale and demonstrated that the roster possessed the talent and determination to compete at a high level, even after a disastrous start. This win was a foundational moment that likely contributed to the Packers' success later in the season, reinforcing the belief that no deficit was too large to overcome.

For the Los Angeles Rams, the game represented a challenging season opener. As the defending Super Bowl champions, they entered the game with high expectations, but falling behind 23-0 highlighted areas of vulnerability in their roster, particularly with the introduction of Bert Jones as their new starting quarterback [7]. The loss, while significant, became the lone blemish on their otherwise stellar nine-game season, in which they compiled an 8-1 record [10]. This record was enough to secure them a spot in the playoffs, and they ultimately advanced to Super Bowl XVII, where they would defeat the Washington Redskins. The Packers' comeback, therefore, did not derail the Rams' championship aspirations for that season, but it did serve as an early-season reality check and a reminder of the competitive balance within the NFC West division.

In the broader context of NFL history, the game is often cited as a classic example of a team overcoming a massive deficit. The dramatic nature of the comeback, featuring multiple long touchdown passes and a stifling second-half defensive effort, has cemented its place in the annals of football lore. Over time, however, its status as the franchise's biggest comeback has been challenged. The Packers' historical records show that this 23-point comeback is tied for the second-largest deficit overcome in team history [14]. The tie is with a more recent game, a thrilling 37-36 victory against the Dallas Cowboys on December 15, 2013, which actually overcame a larger deficit. In the ranking of comeback wins, the 2013 game is listed as #1, with the 1982 Rams game ranked as #2 [14]. This evolution in historical context demonstrates that while the 1982 game remains a celebrated milestone, its position among the franchise's greatest victories has been surpassed by more modern miracles.

To conclude, the legacy of the 1982 Rams-Packers game is multifaceted. For the Green Bay Packers, it is a story of redemption and a testament to the power of perseverance, a game that galvanized a team and set a positive trajectory for their season. For the Los Angeles Rams, it was a sobering but ultimately inconsequential setback in their path to another Super Bowl appearance. For the sport of American football, it is a textbook case study in resilience, a reminder that in a game defined by momentum swings, the team that refuses to quit may yet find a way to triumph. The echoes of that comeback continue to resonate within the Packers' organization, serving as a benchmark for what is possible when a team rallies together in the face of adversity.
