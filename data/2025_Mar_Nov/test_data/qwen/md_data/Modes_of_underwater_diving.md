# Modes of Underwater Diving

Underwater diving encompasses a diverse array of techniques and technologies that enable humans to operate beneath the surface of water. These modes are fundamentally distinguished by the diver's relationship to their source of breathing gas, a factor that dictates mobility, operational duration, physiological risk, training requirements, and intended application. The spectrum ranges from ancient breath-hold diving, which relies solely on human physiology, to highly sophisticated technological systems that place the human operator within a pressurized environment separate from the ambient aquatic conditions. Historically, early activities were limited to short periods dictated by lung capacity, primarily for subsistence purposes like harvesting shellfish and pearls [27], [39]. The advent of mechanical aids, beginning with simple air-trapping devices like the diving bell, marked the first step toward extending time underwater [36], [38]. However, the true transformation occurred with the invention of reliable methods for supplying fresh breathing gas directly to the diver, giving rise to professional surface-supplied diving and, later, the revolutionary mobility of self-contained scuba equipment [13], [83]. Today, the field continues to evolve, pushing the boundaries of depth and duration through advanced saturation systems and exploring previously inaccessible realms via manned submersibles and atmospheric diving suits [46], [54]. Each mode represents a distinct balance between freedom and safety, depth and time, complexity and capability, shaped by the relentless interplay of physics, physiology, and engineering.

## Freediving and Surface-Supplied Diving: The Ancestral and Foundational Modes

The earliest forms of underwater activity were not reliant on technology but on the innate physiological capabilities of the human body. Freediving, also known as skin diving or breath-hold diving, is the original mode of underwater exploration, predating any mechanical apparatus by thousands of years [28]. Evidence of its practice dates back at least 8,000 years, with archaeological findings of exostosis, or "surfer's ear," in the Chinchorro mummies of coastal Chile indicating repeated deep-water immersion for subsistence purposes [29], [30]. Ancient Mesopotamian pearl divers were active as early as 2500 BCE, while Greek sponge fishermen around 1000 BCE were documented using weighted rocks to descend to depths of 30 meters [37]. Practical applications extended beyond sustenance to include military sabotage, such as the actions of Scyllis, who used a hollow reed snorkel to cut enemy ship moorings during the Battle of Salamis in 480 BCE [39], [112], and the use of diving bells by Alexander the Great during the Siege of Tyre in 332 BCE [37], [40]. Over millennia, several cultures developed specialized traditions centered on freediving. The Japanese *Ama*, whose practice dates back approximately 2,000 years, and the Korean *Haenyeo* of Jeju Island represent multi-generational, matrilineal professions dedicated to harvesting seafood [27], [31], [35]. More recently, genetic studies have revealed remarkable adaptations in the Bajau people of Southeast Asia, who have lived nomadically at sea for over 1,000 years; they possess a larger spleen, which enhances oxygen storage, and genes associated with enhanced peripheral vasoconstriction and blood CO₂ regulation, all traits shaped by natural selection [30], [33]. Physiologically, freediving is governed by the Mammalian Dive Reflex, a suite of involuntary responses including bradycardia (a significant slowing of the heart rate), peripheral vasoconstriction (shunting blood to vital organs), and splenic contraction (releasing a reserve of oxygen-rich red blood cells) [106], [107]. Despite these adaptations, freediving carries inherent risks, most notably barotrauma from failure to equalize pressure in air-filled cavities like the ears and sinuses, and shallow water blackout, a form of hypoxic convulsion that can occur near the surface when low carbon dioxide levels suppress the urge to breathe, leading to loss of consciousness [42], [107].

While freediving represents the baseline of human capability, the ability to remain submerged for extended periods required technological intervention. The diving bell, described by Aristotle in the 4th century BC, was the first such innovation, providing a trapped pocket of air that allowed a diver to breathe for a limited time before needing to resurface [23], [36]. Edmond Halley improved upon this design in the late 17th century by adding a mechanism to replenish the air supply, enabling dives lasting up to an hour [17], [36]. However, the foundational technology that truly launched professional diving was the development of reliable surface-supplied equipment. In 1823, John Deane patented a 'Smoke Helmet' for firefighters, which was later adapted for underwater use in 1828 [24], [83]. Augustus Siebe significantly improved the design in the 1830s by integrating the helmet with a full waterproofed canvas suit sealed at the waist, creating the Standard Diving Dress [108], [110]. This system, supplied with compressed air from the surface via a hose, revolutionized maritime salvage, civil engineering, and naval operations for over a century [83]. The evolution of this technology continued with the invention of demand regulators by Rouquayrol and Denayrouze in the 1860s, which delivered gas only when the diver inhaled, improving efficiency [16], [115]. Modern surface-supplied diving (SSD) retains the fundamental principle of a physical link to the surface, typically through a reinforced multi-line umbilical containing hoses for breathing gas, communications, and sometimes hot water [84]. The diver wears either a full-face mask or a helmet, and the gas is supplied from compressors or high-pressure cylinder banks on the surface [84]. The primary advantage of SSD is safety; the diver has access to a virtually unlimited gas supply and can communicate with the surface team at all times, drastically reducing the risk of an out-of-air emergency compared to self-contained systems [83], [85]. This makes it the standard for demanding commercial operations in industries like oil and gas, where tasks such as pipeline inspection, hull cleaning, and platform maintenance require long, uninterrupted working periods [79], [81]. Military clearance divers also utilize rebreathers connected by an umbilical for covert mine clearance and sabotage missions [86], [87]. The trade-off for this security is reduced mobility, as the diver is tethered to the umbilical and cannot move far from the support vessel or structure [83]. Specialized systems, such as reclaim systems that scrub and recycle expensive helium-based mixed gases, have been developed to make deep diving more economically feasible [85].

| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Breathing Gas Source** | Lungs (pre-dive breath hold) [28] | Surface-based compressor or high-pressure cylinders [84] |
| **Mobility** | Unrestricted, untethered [28] | Limited by length and entanglement potential of umbilical [83] |
| **Bottom Time** | Constrained by physiological limits of oxygen stores and CO₂ buildup [28] | Virtually unlimited, determined by task requirements and gas supply [83] |

| **Typical Applications** | Sport, spearfishing, military reconnaissance, scientific observation [27], [42] | Commercial salvage, construction, inspection, military clearance, scientific research [46], [49] |

## Self-Contained Underwater Breathing Apparatus (SCUBA): A Revolution in Mobility

The invention of the Self-Contained Underwater Breathing Apparatus (SCUBA) in the mid-20th century represented a paradigm shift in underwater activity, granting divers unprecedented freedom and autonomy by eliminating the umbilical connection to the surface [14]. Co-invented by Jacques-Yves Cousteau and Émile Gagnan in 1943, the Aqua-Lung was the first practical open-circuit scuba regulator, adapting Gagnan's automotive gas regulator to deliver compressed air from a back-mounted cylinder at ambient pressure on demand [13], [18], [19]. This innovation catalyzed the transition from brief breath-hold dives to sustained exploration, laying the foundation for modern recreational and technical diving [18]. SCUBA is broadly divided into two principal types: open-circuit and closed-circuit systems, each with distinct characteristics, advantages, and risks.

Open-circuit SCUBA is the most common form of diving, particularly in the recreational sector. Its operation is straightforward: the diver inhales breathing gas from a high-pressure cylinder through a two-stage regulator, and the exhaled gas is vented directly into the water as bubbles [1]. This single-use gas pathway means that every breath consumes a volume of gas proportional to the ambient pressure, a principle governed by Boyle's Law [7]. Consequently, gas consumption increases linearly with depth, limiting bottom time and requiring careful planning, especially on deeper dives [1]. Key components of an open-circuit system include the cylinder, first and second stage regulators, a buoyancy compensator device (BCD) to manage neutral buoyancy, and gauges or dive computers to monitor depth and gas supply [1]. The simplicity, reliability, low purchase price, and global accessibility of open-circuit equipment make it the ideal entry point for new divers [2]. Buoyancy control can be managed through lung volume adjustments, as the volume of air in the lungs changes with pressure, affecting overall buoyancy [2]. However, this mode is not without its drawbacks. The production of large volumes of bubbles can disturb marine life, obscure visibility, and compromise stealth, making it unsuitable for certain scientific and military applications [2]. Furthermore, the constant flow of expanding gas into the water requires the diver to actively add or release air from the BCD during ascent and descent to maintain neutral buoyancy, a skill that requires practice to master [7]. Decompression theory, rooted in Henry's Law which describes the dissolution of gases in liquids under pressure, remains a critical consideration for all open-circuit diving, as rapid ascents can lead to the formation of nitrogen bubbles in the bloodstream, causing decompression sickness [8], [10].

Closed-circuit rebreathers (CCRs) represent a significant technological advancement over open-circuit systems, offering superior gas efficiency and other operational benefits [1]. Instead of venting exhaled gas, a rebreather recirculates it through a loop. In this loop, carbon dioxide (CO₂) is chemically absorbed by a scrubber canister filled with a substance like Sofnolime, and oxygen that has been metabolized by the diver is replenished to maintain a controlled partial pressure [1], [6]. This closed-loop system results in minimal gas waste, dramatically increasing endurance on a given gas volume, which is particularly advantageous for deep technical diving where carrying multiple heavy cylinders is impractical [1], [4]. By maintaining a constant partial pressure of oxygen (PO₂), CCRs allow divers to optimize their gas mixtures throughout the dive, minimizing the absorption of inert gases like nitrogen and thereby reducing decompression obligations and enabling shorter decompression stops compared to open-circuit diving with fixed mixtures [4], [6]. Other benefits include warm, humidified breathing gas that reduces thermal stress and dry mouth, and exceptionally quiet, bubble-free operation, which is invaluable for observing wildlife and conducting covert military operations [2], [6]. The core components of a CCR are more complex than those of an open-circuit system and include counterlungs, a CO₂ scrubber, one or more oxygen sensors with a finite lifespan, and electronics to monitor PO₂ and manage gas injection, or manual controls in simpler models [1]. However, this complexity introduces unique and potentially catastrophic risks. Unlike open-circuit failures, which are often obvious (e.g., regulator free-flow), rebreather failures can be silent and insidious [1]. A sensor or electronic failure could lead to undetected hypoxia (low oxygen), while a scrubber failure or bypass can cause dangerous hypercapnia (high CO₂), both of which can incapacitate a diver without warning [2]. Similarly, an electronics fault could cause the unit to inject too much oxygen, leading to hyperoxia and oxygen toxicity [2]. Because of these risks, CCR diving demands rigorous pre-dive checks, strict adherence to discipline, and extensive training focused on recognizing and responding to emergencies, often involving a mandatory bailout system of open-circuit gas [1], [2]. The history of rebreathers dates back to Henry Fleuss's commercially practical oxygen rebreather in 1878, but modern electronic CCRs are a product of post-WWII engineering advancements, including the development of electro-galvanic oxygen sensors [5], [6].

## Advanced Professional Diving: Saturation Systems and Remotely Operated Platforms

As diving operations moved into deeper and more hazardous environments, particularly within the offshore oil and gas industry, the physiological and logistical limitations of conventional diving became increasingly apparent. The primary challenge at great depths is decompression sickness (DCS), a condition caused by dissolved inert gases forming bubbles in the body upon a rapid reduction in ambient pressure [118]. For deep and prolonged work, the time required for decompression after each dive becomes prohibitive, severely limiting productivity. The solution to this problem was saturation diving, an operational technique developed and refined after World War II [51], [90]. The fundamental principle of saturation diving is to allow the divers' bodies to become fully saturated with inert gas at the work pressure, a state achieved after approximately 72 hours of continuous exposure [90]. Once saturated, further time spent at that pressure does not increase the total amount of inert gas in the tissues. Therefore, the total decompression obligation becomes fixed and is performed only once, at the very end of the entire project, regardless of how many days or weeks the divers spend living and working at depth [91], [92]. This technique effectively decouples the length of the work period from the decompression time, enabling extended missions that would otherwise be impossible.

A modern saturation diving system is a complex, integrated hyperbaric facility consisting of three main pressurized components: a living chamber, a transfer lock, and a submersible diving bell [91]. The living chamber serves as the habitat for the divers, who reside there for the duration of the operation, sleeping, eating, and resting at the equivalent pressure of the worksite [91]. The transfer lock provides a safe interface between the living chamber and the diving bell [91]. The diving bell is a pressurized personnel transport vehicle that carries the divers down to the worksite and back to the surface, maintaining the same internal pressure as the living chamber throughout the journey [91]. All three modules are interconnected and maintained at the same ambient pressure. To mitigate the effects of nitrogen narcosis and oxygen toxicity at extreme depths, divers breathe helium-oxygen (heliox) mixtures, which are non-narcotic and less dense than air [92]. The system is equipped with sophisticated life support, including CO₂ scrubbers, temperature and humidity control, and real-time health monitoring [46]. While incredibly effective for deep-sea operations, typically conducted between 100 and 300 meters, saturation diving is a highly complex and costly endeavor, requiring specialized vessels, extensive medical support, and strict adherence to safety protocols, such as the International Marine Contractors Association's D022 Code of Practice [46], [122]. The tragic death of aquanaut Berry Cannon during the SEALAB III program in 1969, attributed to electrocution due to systemic electrical faults in the habitat, underscores the immense risks associated with this technology and the critical importance of engineering integrity and safety culture [93]. Beyond commercial applications, saturation diving has also been used for scientific missions, such as restoring deep-sea habitats injured by the Deepwater Horizon oil spill, where divers worked for up to a week at depths of 53–100 meters [52].

In parallel with the development of human-occupied systems, remotely operated platforms have become indispensable tools for underwater operations, particularly in commercial and industrial sectors. Remotely Operated Vehicles (ROVs) are unmanned, tethered robots that are piloted from the surface and equipped with cameras, manipulator arms, and various sensors [77]. They offer several advantages over human divers, especially in challenging environments. ROVs can operate in hazardous or contaminated waters, at depths or in conditions (such as strong currents or poor visibility) that are unsafe or impossible for humans, and for unlimited durations since they are not subject to physiological limitations [76], [77]. Their payloads can include high-definition cameras, sonar systems for mapping, laser scanners for precise measurements, and manipulators for light construction, sampling, and rigging tasks [77]. The global market for observation and inspection class ROVs is substantial, valued at USD 148 million in 2024 and projected to grow steadily, driven largely by the offshore oil & gas industry's need for routine subsea inspections [78]. Companies like Oceaneering and Subsea Global Solutions deploy fleets of ROVs for tasks ranging from pipeline surveys and riser inspections to supporting emergency repairs and pollution control [79], [81]. While ROVs excel at inspection and data collection, they lack the dexterity and cognitive flexibility of a human diver. As a result, a common strategy in modern operations is to integrate ROVs with human divers, using the ROV to perform initial surveys and prepare sites, followed by divers for more complex or delicate tasks [79]. This synergistic approach leverages the strengths of both platforms, enhancing safety, efficiency, and the quality of the work accomplished. The global commercial diving market, which includes services utilizing both divers and ROVs, was valued at USD 1.36 billion in 2024 and is forecast to reach USD 2.33 billion by 2033, highlighting the ongoing economic importance of these advanced professional diving modes [47].

## Occupied Vehicles: Redefining Human Presence in the Deep Ocean

Beyond the realm of traditional diving, where the human body interfaces directly with the ambient aquatic environment, lies a category of technology that fundamentally redefines the role of the operator. Occupied vehicles, encompassing Atmospheric Diving Suits (ADS) and Deep Submergence Vehicles (DSVs), place the human entirely within a protected, pressurized habitat, allowing them to interact with the deep ocean without being subject to its crushing pressures, toxic gases, or freezing temperatures [25], [54]. These platforms represent the pinnacle of engineering designed to extend human presence into the most extreme and inaccessible regions of the planet.

Atmospheric Diving Suits (ADS) are small, one-person, articulated submersibles that maintain an internal pressure equivalent to one atmosphere, regardless of the external water pressure [54], [56]. This design eliminates the need for the pilot to undergo decompression and removes the risks of decompression sickness and nitrogen narcosis, allowing for prolonged operations at significant depths [25], [56]. ADS units are typically constructed from lightweight, high-strength materials like forged aluminum alloys or glass-reinforced plastic, and feature a series of pressure joints that allow for articulation of limbs, granting the pilot exceptional dexterity [54], [55]. Life support systems provide breathable gas, remove CO₂, and maintain a comfortable microclimate, with some systems capable of sustaining the pilot for up to 50 hours [55]. Prominent examples of ADS include the JIM suit, developed in the 1970s and widely used in North Sea oil operations; the Newtsuit, a lighter and more agile successor also rated for 1000 feet; and the Exosuit, which features advanced thrusters and rotary joints for unprecedented mobility [55], [123], [126]. HARDSUIT™ is another ADS rated for 1,200 feet, used for commercial inspections and repairs [57]. The primary advantage of ADS is safety and operational flexibility. By removing the physiological constraints of deep diving, ADS can replace the complex logistics of saturation diving for many tasks, reducing costs and turnaround times [57]. They are employed in a variety of commercial and government missions, including dam inspection, outfall work, and non-destructive testing [57]. Training for an ADS pilot is rigorous, requiring a minimum of 20 hours of bottom dive time and proficiency in operating the suit's life support and emergency jettison systems [56].

Deep Submergence Vehicles (DSVs), also known as manned submersibles, are larger, untethered, human-occupied craft designed primarily for scientific exploration and observation [66]. Unlike ADS, which are designed for manipulation at moderate depths, DSVs are engineered for long-duration missions in the abyssal zones of the ocean. Their defining characteristic is a spherical pressure hull, typically made of thick titanium, which houses the crew and withstands immense external hydrostatic pressure [59], [64]. This sphere is located within a larger, streamlined hull that contains ballast systems, thrusters for propulsion, and external laboratories [59]. Mission times are typically limited to 8-16 hours, though some emergency life support systems can sustain the crew for longer periods [66]. The maximum operating depth is the key metric for DSVs, with different classes capable of reaching progressively greater depths. The current generation of U.S. Navy-owned Alvin is certified for 6,500 meters (4 miles) [100]. China's Jiaolong has reached 7,000 meters, and Russia's Mir-class vehicles can dive to 6,000 meters [[58]), [60]]. The deepest-diving manned submersible ever built is the Triton 36000/2 (Limiting Factor), which holds the record for a round trip to the bottom of Challenger Deep in the Mariana Trench at 11,000 meters [64]. DSVs serve as mobile science labs, enabling direct observation, sample collection, and in-situ experimentation that cannot be replicated by remotely operated vehicles [66]. They have been instrumental in discovering deep-sea ecosystems like hydrothermal vents and in exploring shipwrecks like the Titanic [100], [101]. The operation of these vehicles requires a mother ship for logistical support and a skilled crew of pilots and scientists [66]. The synergy between DSVs and ROVs is becoming increasingly common, with cooperative missions now standard practice. For example, the Fendouzhe HOV and a lander simultaneously explored the Mariana Trench in 2020, demonstrating new methods for live data transmission from the hadal zone [68].

## Comparative Analysis of Diving Modes

The various modes of underwater diving present a series of distinct trade-offs, balancing factors such as mobility, operational duration, depth capability, risk, and cost. The choice of a particular mode is dictated by the specific goals of the activity, whether it be recreational exploration, commercial construction, military operations, or scientific discovery. A comparative analysis reveals the unique strengths and weaknesses of each approach, highlighting the technological and physiological solutions developed to overcome the challenges of the underwater environment.

Freediving stands apart as the most primitive mode, relying exclusively on human physiology. Its greatest strength is complete freedom of movement, unencumbered by any equipment except perhaps a weight belt or fins [28]. However, this freedom comes at the cost of extremely limited bottom time, constrained by the diver's lung capacity and the onset of fatigue and CO₂ buildup [28]. Freediving is therefore best suited for shallow-water activities like spearfishing, sport, and military reconnaissance where agility and stealth are paramount [27]. In contrast, surface-supplied diving (SSD) offers unparalleled safety and endurance. The physical umbilical ensures a continuous supply of breathing gas and enables real-time communication with the surface, making it the gold standard for high-risk commercial operations like offshore oil and gas infrastructure work [79], [83]. The trade-off is severe restriction of mobility; the diver is tethered and cannot venture far from the support vessel or structure [83]. SCUBA represents a middle ground, offering the mobility of freediving with the extended bottom time of SSD. Open-circuit SCUBA is the most accessible and affordable option, suitable for a vast range of recreational activities [2]. Its limitations are predictable gas consumption and environmental disturbance from bubbles [1], [2]. Closed-circuit rebreathers (CCRs) push the boundaries of endurance and stealth, making them ideal for deep technical diving, covert military missions, and sensitive scientific observations where noise and bubbles must be avoided [1], [6]. However, this capability is purchased at the price of immense complexity and a unique set of risks, including silent, catastrophic gas management failures that demand a higher level of training and discipline [2].

For operations requiring sustained work at extreme depths, saturation diving is the only viable option. It solves the critical problem of decompression by allowing divers to live and work at pressure for days or weeks, with decompression occurring only once at the end of the mission [90], [91]. This maximizes productivity in deep-water environments, particularly in the oil and gas industry [49]. The downside is the enormous logistical footprint, including a specialized support vessel, a large hyperbaric system, and a significant medical and safety team, making it one of the most expensive forms of diving [46]. Finally, occupied vehicles—both ADS and DSVs—offer a different paradigm altogether. ADS provides the dexterity of a human operator with the protection of a suit, enabling intricate tasks at depths up to 700 meters without the need for decompression [54], [57]. DSVs, meanwhile, grant humanity direct access to the deepest parts of the ocean, functioning as mobile laboratories for scientific discovery [66]. Both platforms are limited by mission duration (typically 8-16 hours) and are far more expensive and logistically complex than human-operated diving systems [66]. This comparison underscores a central theme: there is no single "best" mode of diving. Instead, the optimal choice depends on a careful evaluation of the task at hand. Freediving excels in agility, SCUBA in versatility, SSD in security, saturation diving in endurance at extreme depth, and vehicles in access to the deepest and most hostile environments.

## Conclusion: Evolution and Future Trajectories

The history of underwater diving is a compelling narrative of human ingenuity overcoming the formidable barriers of the aquatic environment. From the primal breath-hold dives of our ancestors to the technologically advanced, pressurized habitats of today, the evolution of diving modes reflects a continuous drive to extend the boundaries of what is possible beneath the waves. The progression from freediving, limited by biology, to surface-supplied diving, enabled by the harnessing of compressed air, laid the groundwork for the modern era of exploration [27], [83]. The invention of SCUBA was a watershed moment, democratizing underwater access and launching the age of recreational diving, while the refinement of rebreather technology pushed the frontiers of endurance and stealth for specialized applications [1], [13]. In response to the escalating demands of deep-sea industry and science, saturation diving emerged as a powerful solution to the physiological curse of decompression sickness, enabling sustained operations at previously unimaginable depths [90], [91]. Most recently, the development of atmospheric diving suits and deep submergence vehicles has granted humanity a direct, albeit protected, presence in the most extreme reaches of the ocean, transforming remote locations into accessible scientific laboratories [54], [66].

Throughout this evolution, the fundamental challenges posed by the physics of the underwater world—namely, the inverse relationship between pressure and volume described by Boyle's Law, and the dissolution of gases in tissue governed by Henry's Law—have remained constant drivers of innovation [7], [8]. Each new mode of diving, from the simplest snorkel to the most complex saturation habitat, represents a novel solution to these enduring problems. The trade-offs between mobility and safety, depth and time, simplicity and complexity, have defined the trajectory of the field, leading to a diverse toolkit of technologies tailored to specific needs. Looking forward, the future of diving will likely be shaped by further integration of robotics and automation. The trend towards synergistic operations combining the precision of human divers with the endurance of ROVs is already well-established, and this partnership is expected to deepen [68], [79]. Advancements in materials science, life-support systems, and artificial intelligence may lead to even more capable and safer ADS and DSVs, potentially reducing operational costs and expanding their roles in commercial, scientific, and military domains. The pursuit of knowledge about our planet's final frontier, the deep ocean, will continue to demand robust and versatile ways for humans to explore, investigate, and understand it, ensuring that the evolution of diving modes remains a vibrant and essential field of human endeavor.
