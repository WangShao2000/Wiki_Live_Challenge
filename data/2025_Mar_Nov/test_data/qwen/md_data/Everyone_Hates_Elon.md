# Everyone Hates Elon

## Origins and Ideology

The phrase "Everyone Hates Elon" refers to a UK-based activist collective that emerged in early 2025 as a coordinated response to the perceived influence and political conduct of billionaire entrepreneur Elon Musk [3], [4]. Unlike a passive social media hashtag, the collective is characterized by its organized structure, strategic use of guerrilla tactics, and a clearly defined ideological opposition to what it describes as Musk's role in fueling far-right extremism both in the United States and Europe [1], [23]. The group's origins are traced back to an anonymous "ranty group chat" among three co-founders, one of whom identified as Emma, a resident of South London in her early thirties, and another as Jason [3]. These individuals had previously collaborated on various campaigns and were prompted to found the group after Musk began interfering in UK politics through his social media activity [3], [4]. The collective officially launched its first initiative, a crowdfund titled 'People Versus Elon', in January 2025, which pledged to donate one pence for every post Musk made on the social media platform X [4]. This initial act of digital protest was projected to raise £150,000 by the end of 2025, demonstrating a nascent but ambitious financial strategy aimed at countering Musk's immense wealth [3], [4].

The core ideological pillar of the 'Everyone Hates Elon' (EHE) movement is the belief that Musk is actively promoting and amplifying far-right political movements [1], [23]. The group explicitly states its objective is to hold Musk accountable for "fuelling the far-right in the USA" and repeating that pattern "in Europe" [1]. This ideology is not presented as a vague dislike of Musk but as a direct reaction to a series of specific events and statements he made in late 2024 and throughout 2025. A primary catalyst for the group's formation was Musk's appearance at Donald Trump's presidential inauguration on January 20, 2025, where he reportedly gave a Nazi-style salute [4], [7]. This single gesture became a focal point for the activists, who interpreted it as a clear endorsement of fascist ideologies and a symbolic link between Musk's personal conduct and the rise of right-wing populism [23], [29]. The group's messaging directly connects this gesture to Musk's subsequent political endorsements and online behavior, framing him as a dangerous figure capable of poisoning democratic politics [23]. Their GoFundMe campaign statement, for instance, juxtaposes Musk's alleged political machinations with the struggles of ordinary citizens, citing his reported $200 billion wealth increase in 2024 while nurses in the UK were forced to use food banks [6], [23].

This ideological stance is further solidified by Musk's documented engagement with far-right political actors and parties. The EHE narrative specifically highlights his January 2025 tweets demanding the release of British far-right activist Tommy Robinson and promoting the slogan "Make Europe Great Again" [4], [29]. The group also cites his February 2025 actions, including an allegedly congratulatory phone call to Alice Weidel, the chancellor candidate for Germany's far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) party, following the party's success in securing 20.8% of the vote in federal elections [1], [9]. By linking Musk's personal brand and his control over major technology platforms to the electoral gains of these movements, the EHE collective positions itself as a defender of social justice against what it perceives as a coordinated assault on progressive values [9]. Their partnership with organizations like 'Everyday Racism' and their stated goal of funding causes Musk "hates"—such as Rainbow Migration, Women for Refugee Women, and Hope Not Hate—reinforces this defensive posture [4], [9]. The group's mission, therefore, extends beyond mere protest; it is framed as a direct intervention in the political sphere, aiming to delegitimize Musk's influence and counter the policies he supports [23].

The collective’s emergence reflects a broader trend of digital-age activism, leveraging social media and grassroots organizing to mount a sustained challenge against a high-profile public figure. By creating a distinct brand identity around the name "Everyone Hates Elon," the group effectively weaponizes a common sentiment into a structured movement. Their actions are designed not only to criticize Musk personally but to attack the very concept of unchecked billionaire power, a theme that becomes more pronounced in their later international campaigns [26], [27]. The group’s professional backing from a PR firm named People vs Profiteers, led by consultant Jack Steadman, adds a layer of sophistication to their operations, suggesting a calculated approach to media relations and public messaging [1]. This blend of grassroots energy and professional strategy defines the EHE movement's unique position within the contemporary political landscape.

## Key Campaigns and Tactics

The 'Everyone Hates Elon' (EHE) collective has distinguished itself through a series of bold, unauthorized, and highly visible public interventions designed to disrupt the image of Elon Musk and his associated brands [3]. These tactics range from satirical advertising and sticker campaigns to direct action events, all orchestrated to maximize media attention and public discourse. The centerpiece of their strategy has been the creation and dissemination of provocative anti-Musk posters placed across London's public transport network, including bus stops and Tube stations [3], [22]. One of the most infamous examples, which sparked widespread debate, depicted a Tesla branded as the "Swasticar" [6], [22]. The poster featured an image of Musk performing a straight-armed salute beside a red Tesla, accompanied by the slogan "Goes from 0 to 1939 in 3 seconds" [2], [9], [22]. This imagery explicitly referenced Musk's controversial arm-salute at Donald Trump's inauguration and directly invoked the historical symbolism of the swastika and the year 1939, when Germany invaded Poland, marking the beginning of World War II [9], [23]. The group released installation videos of these posters on Instagram and TikTok, framing the work as "parody" and encouraging viral sharing under hashtags like #Swasticar and #BoycottTesla [9], [22]. Transport for London (TfL) confirmed these ads were unauthorized and instructed contractors to remove them from the network [22].

Beyond posters, the EHE collective has engaged in physical interventions targeting Tesla vehicles and dealerships. They plastered hundreds of Teslas parked in central London with stickers bearing the text "don’t buy a swasticar" [6], [20]. This tactic sought to transform private property into a canvas for protest, forcing drivers and passersby to confront the activists' message directly. The group also took a life-size cardboard cutout of Musk to a North London Tesla showroom, installed a "Musk B-Gone" air freshener product at another dealership, and conducted a "rage room" event where they smashed a donated Tesla, with the recycled battery components subsequently auctioned off for charity [3]. These actions demonstrate a shift from purely informational protests to more visceral forms of disruption, designed to shock and provoke a strong reaction. Another satirical ad styled as a perfume commercial read "Elon's Musk" with the tagline "Pour W*nkers," showcasing the group's use of dark humor and wordplay to critique Musk [22]. A third poster declared simply "ELON MUSK IS A BELLEND. Signed, the UK," using a British slang term for a fool to express public disdain [3].

Financially, the collective has established a crowdfunding model to sustain its operations and support allied causes. The 'People Versus Elon' crowdfunder, which pledges one pence per Musk X post, garnered 1,000 pledgers and raised £12,000 ($15,275) in its first month alone, with projections reaching £150,000 ($190,000) by the end of 2026 [4]. The funds are channeled to organizations that the group identifies as being opposed by Musk, including LGBTQI+ refugee support group Rainbow Migration, Women for Refugee Women, the anti-racism organization Everyday Racism, and the anti-fascist group Hope Not Hate [4]. This creates a tangible link between public participation in the protest and direct financial support for social justice causes, thereby framing the campaign as a form of resistance with concrete outcomes. The group's official website, peoplevselon.co.uk, serves as a central hub for soliciting donations and disseminating information, reinforcing their status as a formalized activist entity rather than a loose collection of individuals [9].

## International Expansion and Coalition-Building

While initially focused on London, the 'Everyone Hates Elon' (EHE) collective demonstrated a capacity for international expansion and strategic coalition-building in late 2025, moving its activism from local streets to global stages. The group's first documented international action occurred in Venice, Italy, in coordination with the environmental organization Greenpeace and a coalition of about a dozen Venetian advocacy groups [26], [27]. On Monday, December 16, 2025, the activists unfurled a giant banner in St. Mark’s Square with the slogan "IF YOU CAN RENT VENICE FOR YOUR WEDDING YOU CAN PAY MORE TAX" [26], [28]. This action was timed to coincide with protests against Jeff Bezos's wedding festivities in the city and was part of a broader movement known as "No Space for Bezos" [27]. The collaboration explicitly drew a connection between Musk's growing influence and the unchecked power of other tech billionaires, framing their protest not merely as a criticism of Musk but as a critique of systemic wealth inequality and tax avoidance among the ultra-rich [26], [27].

This move to Venice marked a significant evolution in the EHE collective's strategy. It shifted their focus from direct attacks on Musk's person or brands (like Tesla) to engaging with broader structural issues of capitalism and wealth concentration. The group's participation in the Venice protest was framed around their stated mission "Make Billionaires Losers Again," indicating a conscious effort to align themselves with other anti-capitalist and anti-corporate movements [28]. By joining forces with Greenpeace and local Venetian groups concerned with housing, tourism, and environmental degradation, EHE demonstrated an ability to build bridges across different political and social movements, transforming their localized anti-Musk campaign into a component of a larger, more inclusive struggle against billionaire power [27]. The joint action highlighted how the perceived influence of figures like Musk and Bezos exacerbates pressing societal problems, from climate change to the commodification of public spaces, thereby expanding the scope of the EHE narrative beyond a single individual to encompass a systemic critique [26].

The choice of venue and timing for the Venice protest was strategically significant. St. Mark’s Square is one of the world's most iconic public spaces, and staging a protest there ensured maximum visibility. The backdrop of Jeff Bezos's lavish wedding provided a stark contrast to the activists' message about wealth disparity, making the visual impact of their banner even more potent [28]. Furthermore, the timing allowed EHE to leverage the existing momentum of the "No Space for Bezos" campaign, integrating their own grievances seamlessly into a pre-existing coalition. This demonstrates a level of political sophistication, as the group did not operate in isolation but instead positioned itself as a valuable partner within a broader network of dissent. The fact that EHE was described as having "smashed Teslas to protest Elon Musk" in the context of this international action underscores the continuity of their aggressive tactics, even as their ideological framing broadened to include critiques of other oligarchs like Bezos [26], [27]. This dual-pronged approach—combining disruptive local action with strategic global alliances—represents a maturation of the movement, allowing it to project its message onto the international stage and engage with a wider audience concerned with issues of economic justice and democratic accountability.

## Broader Anti-Musk Movement

The 'Everyone Hates Elon' (EHE) collective does not operate in isolation; it is a prominent member of a wider ecosystem of anti-Musk sentiment and activism that has gained significant traction in the United Kingdom and the United States since Elon Musk acquired Twitter in October 2022 [17]. This broader movement encompasses various groups, each with slightly different focuses but a shared underlying concern about Musk's influence on technology, politics, and public discourse. For example, the US-based advocacy group Indivisible launched the "Musk or Us" campaign, which mobilized protests in both the US and UK to defend democracy against what it described as Trump's authoritarian agenda, heavily criticizing Musk's leadership of the Department of Government Efficiency (DOGE) for advancing billionaire-friendly policies [25]. Similarly, the 'Tesla Takedown UK' protest group, organized by software engineer John Gorenfeld, held demonstrations outside Tesla dealerships in West London, focusing their efforts on the company's products and brand reputation [25]. These groups often share a common ideological ground, viewing Musk as a threat to democratic institutions and social progress, though their specific targets and tactics may differ.

The concerns animating this multi-front opposition are deeply rooted in the consequences of Musk's acquisition of Twitter. Following the takeover, the platform experienced a documented surge in hate speech, with a peer-reviewed study finding weekly rates rose by approximately 50% in the months after the acquisition [12], [14]. Experts expressed alarm that loosening moderation policies would embolden hate speech actors and accelerate the mainstreaming of false narratives from fringe platforms, potentially increasing the risk of real-world violence [12]. This was exacerbated by Musk's decision to reinstate high-profile banned accounts, including those of Donald Trump, Kanye West, and Andrew Tate, an action he framed under the banner of "Freedom Fridays" but which critics viewed as a fundamental abdication of responsibility [13]. The implementation of a pay-to-verify blue check system, which allows anyone paying a monthly subscription to obtain verification, was formally criticized by the European Commission for undermining users' ability to assess account authenticity and enabling malicious actors to deceive users [13]. These governance failures have provided a powerful rationale for the perception of Musk as a destabilizing force in the information ecosystem.

Compounding these platform governance issues is a sustained campaign of systematic disinformation attributed to Musk on X. Multiple analyses have documented the vast scale of false or misleading claims he has posted. A Center for Countering Digital Hate (CCDH) report found that Musk generated nearly 1.2 billion views with 50 false or misleading posts about the 2025 U.S. elections alone [8]. A separate analysis by DW Fact Check in 2024 documented three verified instances of him spreading falsehoods ahead of the November 5, 2024 election, including retweeting a claim about "illegal aliens becoming voters" that received over 45 million views and posting an AI-generated fake image of Kamala Harris in a communist outfit [11]. More recently, a CNN fact-check published on December 19, 2025, detailed eight additional instances of fabricated information, such as inventing a White House claim about thwarting a "$50M 'condoms for Gaza' expenditure" and misrepresenting Pentagon contracts [10]. Critics argue that Musk, as the owner of a platform with over 200 million followers, has used his position to manipulate the algorithm to boost his own posts and spread conspiracy theories, thereby undermining electoral integrity and public trust [8], [11]. The combination of increased hate speech, lax moderation, and a prolific output of misinformation has created a fertile ground for organized opposition, providing a clear and compelling case for why a diverse array of activists, advocacy groups, and concerned citizens have united under the banner of resisting Musk's influence.

## Public Perception and Impact

The concerted efforts of the 'Everyone Hates Elon' (EHE) collective and the broader anti-Musk movement appear to have had a measurable impact on public perception and corporate performance in the United Kingdom. According to a YouGov survey conducted for *The Times*, 71% of UK respondents held an unfavorable view of Elon Musk, representing a seven-point increase from a previous poll in November 2024 [20]. This data provides quantitative evidence that the negative campaigning and public discourse surrounding Musk have successfully translated into a decline in his public standing within the country. The survey results suggest that the activists' messaging, which links Musk's political conduct to tangible societal harms, resonates with a significant portion of the population. His status as a "deeply unpopular figure in Britain," as described by some outlets, is thus supported by polling data, indicating that the grassroots campaigns have contributed to shaping a hostile national environment for him [19].

Beyond public opinion, the anti-Musk sentiment appears to have affected Tesla's business interests in Europe. The source materials note that the "Swasticar" campaign coincided with a near-halving of Tesla's European sales at the start of 2025 [22]. While a direct causal link cannot be definitively established from the provided sources, the temporal correlation suggests that the negative publicity and boycott calls generated by activists like EHE may have contributed to this sharp downturn. The campaign's slogans, such as "Autopilot for your car. Autocrat for your country," cleverly exploit the duality of the word "autopilot" to associate Tesla's technology with Musk's perceived political autocracy, potentially influencing consumer purchasing decisions [1], [3]. The distribution of thousands of "don’t buy a swasticar" stickers on Teslas in London would have served as a constant, on-the-ground reminder of the controversy to potential buyers and the general public, likely contributing to a chilling effect on the brand's appeal [6], [20].

The overall impact of these campaigns is reflected in the broader cultural and political discourse. The viral nature of the "Swasticar" posters and related hashtags (#Swasticar, #BoycottTesla) indicates that the movement succeeded in capturing mainstream attention and embedding its narrative into the public conversation [22]. The involvement of celebrities like comedian Bill Burr, who publicly labeled Musk an "idiot" and "evidently a Nazi" on NPR's Fresh Air, citing the same inauguration salute that inspired the activists, lent further credibility and reach to the movement's core accusations [24]. This convergence of grassroots activism, celebrity endorsement, and critical media coverage created a powerful feedback loop that amplified the message far beyond the confines of the original "ranty group chat" that birthed the movement [3], [24]. By relentlessly connecting Musk's public persona to accusations of fascism and his corporate holdings to boycott-worthy associations, the activists have managed to shift public sentiment, negatively affect a major corporation's bottom line, and embed their cause within the broader political consciousness of the UK. The movement's effectiveness lies in its ability to translate abstract ideological opposition into concrete, observable consequences, demonstrating the power of targeted, creative activism in the modern era.

## Controversies and Source Analysis

An examination of the provided sources reveals several areas of nuance, ambiguity, and potential bias that are crucial for a balanced understanding of the "Everyone Hates Elon" phenomenon. A significant point of contention arose from a reporting error by *The New York Times*, which published a correction on April 7, 2025, clarifying that a fake movie billboard titled 'The Fast and the Führer' was claimed by the group 'Overthrow Musk', not 'Everyone Hates Elon' as originally reported [2]. This incident highlights the complexity of the anti-Musk activist landscape, where multiple groups may share similar ideologies and employ comparable tactics, leading to potential confusion in media attribution. While the sources do not confirm whether 'Everyone Hates Elon' was involved in that specific instance, the existence of overlapping groups like 'Tesla Takedown UK' alongside 'Everyone Hates Elon' and 'Overthrow Musk' suggests a loosely affiliated ecosystem of resistance rather than a monolithic front [1], [2], [25]. This analytical distinction is important, as it complicates a simple narrative of a single, unified movement and points to a more fragmented but ideologically aligned field of activism.

Another area requiring careful analysis is the precise interpretation of Elon Musk's controversial January 20, 2025, inauguration salute. While many sources, including journalists like Erin Burnett of CNN and activists like Bill Burr, describe the gesture as a "Nazi salute" or a "Sieg Heil" gesture, the most detailed description characterizes it as a Roman/Fascist-style salute [7], [24]. This technical distinction, while subtle, is significant. The ambiguity surrounding Musk's intent—the possibility that he was mimicking a historical or political figure rather than consciously endorsing a specific ideology—is not fully explored in the provided materials. However, the activists' framing of the gesture as unequivocally Nazi in connotation appears to be a deliberate rhetorical strategy. By bypassing nuanced debate and presenting the salute as a clear-cut act of sympathy for Nazism, the 'Everyone Hates Elon' collective simplifies a complex issue into an unambiguous moral condemnation, thereby maximizing its emotional impact and mobilizing public outrage [23], [29].

Finally, a critical assessment of the sources reveals a significant and unavoidable bias. The vast majority of the provided context blocks originate from left-leaning or mainstream press outlets, including *The Guardian*, *The New York Times*, Novara Media, DW Fact Check, and *Newsweek* [2], [5], [11], [15], [20]. There is no representation of reliable sources that offer a defense of Musk's actions, present a neutral perspective, or provide a counter-narrative to the allegations of far-right sympathies. This presents a one-sided view of the controversy, where Musk is almost universally portrayed as a provocateur and a threat to democratic norms. While the facts of his actions—such as the number of false posts he made or the specific accounts he reinstated—are verifiable and sourced, the interpretations of those actions are overwhelmingly critical. Therefore, any report based on this information must rely heavily on attributing claims to their respective sources to maintain neutrality. For instance, stating that "activists have labeled Musk a 'Nazi'" is factual, whereas stating "Musk is a Nazi" would be an editorial judgment. The report must consistently use phrases like "the group alleges," "critics claim," or "sources describe" to ensure that the bias is attributed to the source material, not introduced by the reporting itself. This methodological rigor is essential for fulfilling the encyclopedic requirement of impartiality, even when the available evidence strongly leans in one direction.
