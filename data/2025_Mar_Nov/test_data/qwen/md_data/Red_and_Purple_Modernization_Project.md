# Red and Purple Modernization Project

The Red and Purple Modernization Project (RPM) refers to a major capital improvement initiative undertaken by the Chicago Transit Authority (CTA) to upgrade one of the busiest and oldest sections of its rail network [4], [13]. Primarily, the term denotes a $2.1 billion reconstruction effort focused on the North Main Line of the Red and Purple Lines, stretching from Belmont Avenue to Howard station [3], [25]. This project, which began construction in 2019 and saw its first phase conclude in July 2025, represents the largest capital improvement ever executed by the CTA [11], [17]. It encompasses the complete rebuilding of four century-old Red Line stations, the replacement of over six miles of aging elevated track structure, and the construction of a critical bypass to alleviate a historic bottleneck at the Clark Junction where the Brown Line intersects with the Red and Purple Lines [36], [37], [39]. The RPM is also a component of a larger, long-term strategic plan known as the All Stations Accessibility Program (ASAP), which aims to make all 145 CTA train stations compliant with the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) by 2038 [15]. Under this broader umbrella, the RPM program extends beyond the initial North Side corridor to include the rehabilitation of 42 inaccessible stations across the entire Red and Purple Lines, most notably the non-accessible stations on the Evanston Branch of the Purple Line [15]. While the initial, high-profile phase has reached completion, subsequent phases focusing on other segments of the line remain in an early planning stage and are contingent upon securing additional funding [3], [5]. The project's implementation has been marked by significant engineering feats, extensive community engagement, and recent federal scrutiny over its funding mechanisms, highlighting its central role in Chicago's ongoing efforts to modernize its transit infrastructure [20], [23].

## Project Scope and Phasing

The Red and Purple Modernization Project (RPM) is defined by two primary scopes: a large-scale, multi-billion-dollar reconstruction of a specific corridor on Chicago's North Side, and a broader, long-term program aimed at making the entire Red and Purple Line system fully accessible. The more commonly referenced scope is the $2.1 billion Phase One project, which is widely recognized as the largest capital improvement initiative in the history of the Chicago Transit Authority (CTA) [11], [17], [45]. Announced in 2014 as part of Mayor Rahm Emanuel’s 'Red Ahead' program, this phase was designed to address decades of deferred maintenance and improve service reliability on the CTA’s busiest rail line, which carries over 75 million riders annually [13]. The project's physical footprint covers a 9.6-mile stretch of track built over a century ago, running from Belmont Avenue to Howard station [1], [18]. This segment traverses dense urban areas, including Lakeview and the 44th Ward, serving over 240,000 average weekday riders entering at stations within its corridor [1], [4]. The core objective of Phase One was to replace obsolete infrastructure with modern facilities capable of supporting increased capacity and improved passenger experience for the next 60 to 80 years [41].

Phase One itself was a complex undertaking divided into several major components. The most visible element was the complete reconstruction of four Red Line stations: Lawrence, Argyle, Berwyn, and Bryn Mawr [3], [7], [31]. These stations, some of which dated back to the early 20th century, were transformed into fully accessible, modern facilities featuring elevators, escalators, wider platforms, and translucent overhead canopies [3], [7]. The second major component was the replacement of six miles of century-old track structure and support structures between Lawrence and Bryn Mawr stations [3], [36]. This included new track, bridges, viaducts, and a new signal system covering a 23-mile stretch of track between Howard and Belmont stations [7], [9]. A third critical component was the construction of the Red-Purple Bypass, also known as the Belmont Flyover, a grade-separated rail bridge located directly north of Belmont Station [1], [37]. This bypass was engineered to eliminate a historic bottleneck at the Clark Junction, a level crossing where northbound Brown Line trains previously had to cross over the four tracks of the Red and Purple Lines, causing frequent delays and limiting overall system capacity [1], [39]. By providing a dedicated route for the Brown Line, the bypass allows for uninterrupted service on all three lines, thereby increasing capacity and reducing overcrowding [1], [11]. The final component of Phase One was the installation of a new signal system based on Communications-Based Train Control (CBTC) technology, which enables more precise train location tracking and allows for shorter, more frequent headways, resulting in smoother and more reliable service [6], [7].

While Phase One concluded in July 2025, the broader RPM program continues through subsequent planning and potential future construction phases [2], [36]. The planning for these future phases is currently underway under the name "RPM Next Phases Planning Study," which commenced in late 2025 [3], [5]. This study focuses on three distinct segments of the Red and Purple Lines: the Red Line section from Addison to Sheridan, the Red Line section from Thorndale to Howard, and the entire Purple Line Evanston Branch from Howard to Linden [3], [5]. As of December 2025, these future phases are unfunded and unannounced, meaning no specific projects, budgets, or timelines have been established [9]. The progression of the Evanston Branch portion of the RPM is particularly uncertain; it remains in an early "ideation and discovery phase" with no committed funding or set timeline, pending the acquisition of necessary capital from federal, state, and local sources [15]. Securing future funding will likely depend on obtaining a Federal Transit Administration (FTA) Core Capacity Grant, which requires demonstrating a projected increase in passenger capacity of at least 10 percent [32]. For example, the planned reconstruction of Sheridan station, which is over 120 years old and currently non-accessible, faces significant spatial constraints due to adjacent developments, prompting planners to evaluate potential station relocation alongside full reconstruction [32]. The successful transition from the planning phase to the construction phase for these future projects hinges entirely on the ability to secure substantial financial commitments.

## Infrastructure and Technical Upgrades

The Red and Purple Modernization Project entailed comprehensive upgrades to nearly every aspect of the rail infrastructure between Belmont and Howard stations, transforming century-old assets into modern, efficient, and accessible facilities. The centerpiece of the project was the complete reconstruction of four Red Line stations—Lawrence, Argyle, Berwyn, and Bryn Mawr—which were originally built in the early 20th century [3], [11]. Each station was redesigned to be 100% accessible, incorporating elevators and escalators to meet Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) standards [3], [7]. The new designs featured wider platforms to accommodate growing passenger demand and reduce crowding, improved lighting, modern clearance-compliant viaducts, and platform benches [3], [7]. A distinctive feature of the new stations is the use of large, translucent overhead canopies that provide weather protection while allowing natural light to penetrate, creating a brighter and more welcoming environment [3], [7]. Ventra fare machines were installed, and bike parking facilities were expanded to support multimodal transportation [7], [11]. Furthermore, the CTA conducted stakeholder engagement led by a community relations liaison to ensure the architectural character of each new station reflected the unique identity of its surrounding neighborhood [11]. The project also included the development of open spaces beneath the new tracks between Lawrence and Ardmore avenues, which are scheduled to begin in 2026 and include a pedestrian trail, dog parks, playgrounds, and a flexible-use plaza to benefit the local community [7].

The structural backbone of the project was the replacement of approximately six miles of track built over a century ago, along with their associated support structures [3], [36]. The old trackbed and embankments were dismantled and replaced with a new elevated structure constructed higher than the previous one to meet modern vertical clearance standards for vehicles and pedestrians below [7], [36]. This elevation change eliminated mid-street support columns, significantly improving sightlines for drivers and enhancing the aesthetic integration of the elevated line into the urban fabric [7], [36]. The construction utilized advanced materials and techniques, including precast segmental box girders for the main viaduct between Lawrence and Bryn Mawr, assembled using a custom-designed gantry system to minimize community impact, and steel plate girders for the 45-foot-high Brown Line flyover at Clark Junction [2]. The new track structure was designed for longevity and durability, ensuring a smoother ride for passengers and reducing future maintenance needs [17]. To manage the immense complexity of replacing active track while maintaining continuous service, the project employed a "stop-go-stop-go" approach, conducting much of the work at night to avoid disrupting commuter traffic [42].

Perhaps the most transformative technical upgrade was the implementation of a new Communications-Based Train Control (CBTC) signal system across 23 miles of track between Howard and Belmont stations [7], [9]. This modernizes the system away from legacy fixed-block signaling, which has been in place since the subway's opening, and replaces it with an international gold standard moving-block system [6], [10]. CBTC technology provides operators with real-time, precise information on the exact location, speed, and direction of every train on the line [6]. This capability allows for dynamic adjustment of clearances between trains, enabling them to operate much closer together safely. The result is a significant increase in operational efficiency, allowing for faster, more frequent, and more reliable service [6]. On lines where CBTC has already been implemented, such as the L and 7 lines, on-time performance has consistently exceeded 90%, and signal-related delays have been drastically reduced [6]. The goal for the RPM corridor is to achieve similar results, ultimately increasing corridor capacity by 15 percent, which exceeds the federal 10-percent minimum threshold required for Core Capacity projects [25], [34]. The installation of the new signal system was a massive undertaking overseen by consultants like VHB, who provided signal design review and construction oversight to ensure quality and compliance throughout the $2.1 billion project [10]. In addition to the signaling overhaul, the project also involved upgrades to the power systems, including substations that convert high-voltage AC power from Commonwealth Edison into the 600V DC required by the third rail system, ensuring a stable and robust power supply for the modernized fleet [33], [35].

## Funding, Governance, and Stakeholders

The Red and Purple Modernization Project was financed through a combination of substantial federal, state, and local funding sources, reflecting its status as a critical piece of regional infrastructure. The cornerstone of its financing was a legally binding commitment from the U.S. government. On January 9, 2017, the Federal Transit Administration (FTA) awarded the CTA a Full Funding Grant Agreement (FFGA) for the project [22], [27], [40]. An FFGA is a formal instrument used for major capital investment projects receiving $25 million or more, establishing the project scope, maximum federal funding amount, and contractual obligations [40]. This particular agreement represented the "full faith and credit of the United States" and committed $1.07 billion in federal funds toward the project's total estimated cost of $2.1 billion [22], [40]. This federal allocation was broken down into $956.61 million from the Capital Investment Grant (CIG) Program, specifically designated for Core Capacity projects, and approximately $116 million from the Congestion Mitigation and Air Quality (CMAQ) Program [22], [27]. The CIG funds were disbursed over nine years, subject to annual Congressional appropriations [27]. Over the years, Senator Dick Durbin played a significant role in securing more than $1 billion in federal funds for the RPM, including the $956 million allocated through the MAP-21 transportation bill's Core Capacity Program established in 2012 [23]. Local funding came from various sources, including $622 million in tax increment financing (TIF) funds from the City of Chicago and contributions from the Regional Transportation Authority (RTA) [14], [37].

The governance and management of the project involved a complex web of stakeholders, each playing a distinct role in its execution. The ultimate responsibility for the project lies with the **Chicago Transit Authority (CTA)**, an independent governmental agency created in 1947 to oversee public transit in the city [12]. The CTA initiated the project as part of its 'Red Ahead' program and serves as the grantee for all federal funds [13], [31]. Oversight of the project is provided by the **Regional Transportation Authority (RTA)**, Illinois's transit oversight agency, through its Project Management Oversight (PMO) program, which monitors all state-funded transit projects with budgets over $10 million [14]. The day-to-day operations and leadership of the CTA are directed by its President, Nora Leerhsen, who assumed the role in February 2025 [12]. The executive body of the CTA, the seven-member Chicago Transit Board, appointed by the Mayor of Chicago and the Governor of Illinois, provides policy guidance and approves major contracts and expenditures [12].

The actual construction of Phase One was contracted out through a design-build approach to a team led by **Walsh Construction Co. II LLC and Fluor Enterprises Inc.**, collectively known as the Walsh-Fluor Design Build Team [13], [38]. This team was selected after a rigorous 18-month procurement process that began with a Request for Qualifications in 2017 [13]. Their proposal was chosen because it delivered $297 million in cost savings and over 200 days of schedule acceleration compared to other bids [13]. The contract, valued at $1.2 billion, covered the engineering, design, and construction of all Phase One components [13], [38]. To ensure the highest standards of quality and compliance throughout this massive project, the CTA hired external firms to serve as owner's representatives. **Jacobs**, working with joint venture partners Arcadis and Ardmore-Roderick, was awarded a six-year contract to provide professional owner's representative services for Phase One [17]. Additionally, **VHB** served as part of the CTA's Owner's Representative team, providing specialized oversight for signal design review and construction supervision [10]. These firms played a crucial role in monitoring progress, verifying that construction adhered to specifications, and mitigating risks, underscoring the project's complexity and the importance placed on quality control [10], [17]. ## Community and Economic Impact

The Red and Purple Modernization Project has generated significant economic activity and reshaped the communities along the North Side corridor, bringing both tangible benefits and raising important social questions. One of the most immediate impacts was the creation of thousands of jobs. As of January 31, 2025, the RPM Phase One project had created more than 2,600 construction trade labor jobs, providing employment opportunities throughout the duration of the multi-year effort [23], [28]. This injection of economic activity supported local businesses and skilled workers. Beyond direct employment, the project has catalyzed transit-oriented development (TOD) along the corridor, aligning with Chicago's TOD Ordinance [11]. The construction of new, modern stations has spurred redevelopment and increased property values in surrounding areas. Observations made near the Wilson and Lawrence stations noted rent increases following the introduction of new housing units and commercial spaces, indicating the project's role in attracting investment and revitalizing the neighborhoods [11]. The new stations themselves, with their enhanced amenities and accessibility, are expected to further encourage residential and commercial development by improving connectivity and convenience for residents and commuters [11].

In recognition of the project's profound impact on the communities it serves, the CTA engaged in extensive stakeholder outreach and community relations efforts. These initiatives were led by a dedicated community relations liaison, Latrice Phillips Brown, whose role was to ensure that the new station designs incorporated elements reflecting the unique character of each neighborhood [11]. This participatory approach aimed to foster a sense of ownership and pride among residents and business owners. Public meetings and forums were held to gather input and maintain transparency throughout the planning and construction process [11]. Interpretation services were provided in Spanish and American Sign Language (ASL), and captioning was available at virtual meetings for the upcoming RPM Next Phases Planning Study, demonstrating a commitment to inclusive communication [5]. The opening of the four new stations in July 2025 was celebrated as a major milestone, marking the culmination of years of collaboration and hard work [31], [45].

Despite these positive outcomes, the project has also highlighted persistent social and economic challenges, particularly concerning equity and displacement. Academic and community concerns have been raised about the risk of gentrification and displacement driven by rising rents in the wake of major infrastructure investments [11]. A teacher at Goudy Public School expressed worry that the benefits of the new stations would not be equitably shared, potentially pushing out long-term residents [11]. Research conducted by Professor Kate Lowe of DePaul University further pointed to racial equity gaps in transit investment, suggesting that the distribution of resources and benefits may not adequately address the needs of historically marginalized communities [11]. Another point of contention relates to the prioritization of certain stations for reconstruction over others. During the initial scoping and environmental review phase of the RPM between 2009 and 2012, Loyola station was considered for comprehensive reconstruction [44]. However, it was ultimately excluded from the final preferred alternative for the project, partly due to public comment and technical analysis related to its constrained location between existing buildings [44]. While Loyola did undergo a separate $11 million renovation in 2012–2013, it did not receive the full-scale reconstruction characteristic of the RPM stations, leading to questions about how priorities are set and whether all accessible stations are being treated equitably in the broader modernization effort [44]. These concerns underscore the complex relationship between large-scale infrastructure projects, urban development, and social equity.

## Controversies and Future Outlook

The Red and Purple Modernization Project, while largely characterized by successful execution and significant infrastructure improvements, has not been without controversy, particularly concerning its funding and the intersection of civil rights enforcement with federal infrastructure policy. The most significant recent challenge emerged in December 2025 when the Trump administration placed a hold on $2.1 billion in previously approved federal funding for both the RPM and the related Red Line Extension (RLE) project [20], [24], [28]. This action, announced by White House Budget Director Russell Vought, occurred concurrently with a review of the RLE and was justified by the U.S. Department of Transportation (USDOT) as a measure to assess whether unconstitutional, race- and sex-based contracting requirements were being applied to these federally funded projects [20], [30]. The review specifically targeted the CTA's use of Disadvantaged Business Enterprise (DBE) programs, which mandate that a percentage of project dollars be spent with certified minority-, women-, and veteran-owned businesses. As of the end of September 2025, 21% of spending on the RPM had been allocated to DBE firms, a practice the CTA has publicly promoted as central to its equity mission [20], [29]. The hold on funds created significant uncertainty for the future of the RPM, especially for the unfunded next phases, which rely heavily on securing new federal grants. The CTA had previously affirmed that an FFGA represents the "full faith and credit of the United States" and had never been reneged upon by a federal government [30]. This event highlights the vulnerability of large-scale infrastructure projects to shifts in federal political priorities and the ongoing legal and political debates surrounding affirmative action and equitable contracting in public works.

Another minor but notable issue arose during the construction of the Red-Purple Bypass (Belmont Flyover). In March 2022, concrete spalling—a form of surface degradation—was reported on the walls of the flyover structure, attributed to frost weathering [2]. The CTA and its contractors responded promptly, and the repairs were completed by December 2022, indicating that the issue was addressed without compromising the structural integrity or safety of the new infrastructure [2]. This incident serves as a reminder of the technical challenges inherent in constructing complex infrastructure in a major metropolitan area and the importance of ongoing maintenance and inspection even after a project's completion.

Looking ahead, the future of the Red and Purple Modernization Project is intrinsically linked to the resolution of the federal funding pause and the successful navigation of the upcoming planning and funding stages for the future phases. With the conclusion of Phase One, the focus has shifted to the RPM Next Phases Planning Study, which is actively evaluating alternatives for upgrading the Red Line from Addison to Sheridan and Thorndale to Howard, as well as the entire Purple Line Evanston Branch [3], [5]. These future endeavors are explicitly contingent on securing a Federal Transit Administration (FTA) Core Capacity Grant, which requires demonstrating a projected capacity increase of at least 10 percent to justify the investment [32]. The success of this planning phase will depend on effectively communicating the need for continued investment to policymakers and securing the necessary financial commitments from federal, state, and local sources. If the funding controversy is resolved favorably, the next generation of RPM projects could bring similar modernization benefits to a much larger portion of the Red and Purple Lines, addressing accessibility gaps in Evanston and upgrading decades-old infrastructure across the system. However, if funding remains constrained, the progress made in Phase One may stand as a singular achievement, leaving many of the remaining modernization goals unrealized for the foreseeable future. The ultimate legacy of the RPM will therefore hinge not only on the successful completion of its first phase but also on its ability to build momentum and secure the resources needed for its next chapter.
