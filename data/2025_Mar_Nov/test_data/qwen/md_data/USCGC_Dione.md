# USCGC Dione (WPC-107)

The United States Coast Guard Cutter *Dione* (WPC-107) was a 165-foot Thetis-class patrol boat that served with distinction during World War II [1], [3]. As one of eighteen sister ships built between 1931 and 1934, she represented a critical component of America's coastal defense efforts during the height of the Battle of the Atlantic [5], [8]. Named for a figure from Greek mythology, the *Dione* was a versatile and adaptable vessel, evolving from a standard coastal patrol craft into a formidable anti-submarine warfare platform [8]. Her career is defined by heroic acts of rescue, direct engagement with enemy forces, and a pivotal role in the post-war surrender of German U-boats, cementing her place as a symbol of the Coast Guard's vital contribution to national security [4], [8], [9]. This report details the technical specifications, operational history, and enduring legacy of the *USCGC Dione*, based on archival records and historical accounts.

## Technical Specifications and Design

The *USCGC Dione* (WPC-107) was a product of the early 20th-century American shipbuilding industry, designed to be a durable and versatile patrol vessel capable of operating in challenging coastal environments. She belonged to the "B"-class, also known as the 165-foot patrol boat or Thetis-class cutter, a group of vessels specifically designed for long-range patrols and law enforcement duties along the nation's coasts [1], [10]. These cutters were constructed at small commercial yards, reflecting a pragmatic approach to building a large number of high-quality vessels quickly [8]. The *Dione* herself was built by the Manitowoc Shipbuilding Company and launched between 1931 and 1934, entering an era of increasing international tension that would soon define her entire career [3]. Her design balanced firepower, endurance, and crew capacity, making her well-suited for the diverse missions she would undertake. The *Dione*'s propulsion system was central to her endurance and operational effectiveness. Powered by two Winton Model 158 six-cylinder diesel engines, each producing 670 brake horsepower, the cutter could achieve a top speed of 16 knots, a respectable velocity for a vessel of its size intended for patrol rather than high-speed chase [3], [8]. However, its true strength lay in its range. With a capacity for 7,700 gallons of diesel fuel, the *Dione* could steam for 3,000 nautical miles at a more economical cruising speed of 11 knots, or an incredible 6,400 nautical miles at just 6 knots while running on a single engine [8]. This long-endurance capability was crucial for fulfilling its primary mission of maintaining a persistent presence along the vast coastline of the United States, particularly in the heavily contested waters of the Eastern Sea Frontier during World War II. The ability to remain on station for extended periods without refueling allowed the cutter and its crew to effectively monitor maritime traffic, enforce blockades, and respond to emergencies far from home port.

The vessel's armament reflects the dramatic evolution of its role from a peacetime law enforcement patrol boat to a frontline combatant in the Battle of the Atlantic. Initially, the *Dione* was armed with a main battery of one 3-inch/23 caliber Mark 7 gun and two secondary 37mm Mark 4 one-pounder guns [8]. This configuration was typical for a patrol cutter of the era, providing sufficient firepower for signaling, shore bombardment, and engaging lightly armed surface vessels. However, as the threat from German U-boats intensified, the *Dione* underwent significant modernization. By 1945, her armament had been completely overhauled to meet the demands of anti-submarine warfare. The single 3-inch gun was replaced by two more powerful 3-inch/50 caliber guns, providing greater range and lethality against surfaced submarines [3], [8]. For close-in defense against aircraft and smaller surface threats, she carried two rapid-firing 20mm Oerlikon cannons [3], [8]. To combat submerged U-boats, the cutter was equipped with two Y-guns, which launched depth charges in a pattern to attack a submarine's flank, and two depth charge tracks for deploying heavier charges directly onto a target [3], [8]. Further enhancing her ASW capabilities were two Mousetrap rocket projectors, designed to fire rockets carrying homing torpedoes to attack a submarine's conning tower [3], [8]. This formidable arsenal transformed the *Dione* into a lethal hunter-killer, capable of detecting, tracking, and destroying enemy submarines.

In addition to its advanced weaponry, the *Dione* was equipped with the latest technology available for naval warfare in 1945. It was fitted with SF-1 radar, which provided the crew with the ability to detect surface vessels and aircraft at long ranges, even in darkness or poor weather conditions [8]. This sensor was a game-changer for coastal defense, allowing the cutter to maintain situational awareness and intercept targets long before they came within visual range. Furthermore, five of the sixteen B-class cutters, including the *Dione*, were equipped with QCN-1 sonar [8]. Sonar allowed the crew to detect and track submerged submarines by listening for their acoustic signatures, providing critical data needed to conduct successful depth charge attacks. The combination of these sensors—radar for surface and air detection and sonar for underwater detection—placed the *Dione* firmly within the modernized naval framework of the U.S. Navy and Coast Guard, enabling it to function as an effective component of a coordinated antisubmarine warfare task force. The integration of these technologies, alongside the massive increase in crew complement to 75 personnel, underscores the profound transformation the vessel underwent, adapting its design and capabilities to meet the existential threat posed by Germany's U-boat campaign [3], [8].

## Commissioning and Early Career

The commissioning of the *USCGC Dione* occurred in the early 1930s, placing her entry into service during a period of global uncertainty that preceded the outbreak of World War II [10]. Built at the Manitowoc Shipbuilding Company between 1931 and 1934, she was part of a larger class of 18 identical 165-foot "B"-class patrol cutters [3], [8]. These vessels were named after figures from Greek mythology, a tradition that imbued them with a sense of classical authority and purpose [8]. The names of her sister ships included *Argo*, *Ariadne*, *Atalanta*, *Aurora*, *Cyane*, *Daphne*, *Galatea*, *Hermes*, *Icarus*, *Nemesis*, *Nike*, *Pandora*, *Perseus*, *Thetis*, and *Triton*, creating a cohesive and symbolic fleet dedicated to patrolling America's shores [5], [13]. The *Dione* was assigned the hull number WPC-107, identifying her uniquely within the Coast Guard's vessel classification system [1], [13]. Her initial deployment was focused on the routine but vital duties of the peacetime Coast Guard, which included enforcing maritime laws, conducting search and rescue operations, and maintaining aids to navigation. While specific details of her pre-war patrols and assignments are not available in the provided sources, her service during this period established the foundation for the highly skilled crews who would later serve aboard her during the crucible of World War II.

As a member of the Thetis-class, the *Dione* was designed for long-range patrols, a core mission of the Coast Guard's Surface Fleet. These cutters were the workhorses of the service, tasked with maintaining a constant presence across the vast expanses of the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans. Their endurance, enabled by a range of up to 6,400 nautical miles, allowed them to operate far from base, ensuring that the United States' maritime interests were protected and its laws enforced coast-to-coast [8]. During the late 1930s, as tensions escalated in Europe and Asia, the Coast Guard's role began to shift from purely domestic law enforcement to a more militarized posture. Cutters like the *Dione* were increasingly called upon to support national defense initiatives, preparing for a potential conflict that seemed increasingly inevitable. This transition involved extensive training exercises, heightened readiness levels, and the gradual modernization of their armaments and equipment to prepare for the realities of naval warfare. The crews of these vessels, drawn from the ranks of experienced Coast Guardsmen, honed their skills in seamanship, gunnery, and navigation, transforming them into a professional and disciplined force ready to answer the call to arms.

The onset of World War II marked a dramatic and immediate transformation in the role of the *Dione* and her fellow B-class cutters. On July 1, 1941, six of the sixteen 165-foot "B"-class cutters, including the *Dione*, were placed on duty with the United States Navy [5]. This transfer signified the formal integration of the Coast Guard into the broader naval war effort, a partnership that would prove invaluable throughout the conflict. While serving with the Navy, the cutters continued to fly the Coast Guard ensign and were commanded by Coast Guard officers, preserving their unique identity and traditions within the combined command structure. The *Dione*'s assignment to the Navy placed her at the forefront of the battle for the Atlantic, where she would spend the remainder of the war. Her new responsibilities were far removed from the simple enforcement of fishing regulations or immigration laws. She was now a frontline combatant, tasked with hunting and destroying German U-boats, escorting convoys of merchant ships through perilous waters, and rescuing survivors from torpedoed vessels. This transition was seamless for the well-trained crews, who applied their peacetime skills to the deadly new challenges of modern naval warfare. The time spent in pre-war service had prepared them perfectly for the trials ahead, forging a resilient and capable fighting force ready to defend the nation's sea lanes against the greatest naval threat the country had ever faced.

## World War II Service

During World War II, the *USCGC Dione* (WPC-107) played a vital and multifaceted role in the U.S. Coast Guard's campaign to protect the nation's coastline from the German U-boat offensive, known as Operation Drumbeat [9]. Her service was concentrated in the Virginia Capes region and the surrounding waters of the Eastern Sea Frontier, a critical theater of the Battle of the Atlantic where Allied shipping faced relentless attacks from Nazi submarines [9]. Operating in direct coordination with the U.S. Navy, the *Dione* and her sister cutters formed a crucial layer of defense, hunting enemy submarines, rescuing survivors, and escorting convoys through treacherous waters. Her actions during this period were characterized by remarkable bravery, efficiency, and tactical proficiency, earning her a distinguished record in the annals of Coast Guard history. The cutter's contributions can be understood through three primary aspects of her wartime service: coastal defense and rescue operations, active anti-submarine warfare, and participation in the historic surrender of German U-boats.

One of the most celebrated episodes of the *Dione*'s wartime service was her heroic rescue of survivors from the torpedoed tanker SS *Acme*. On the night of March 17-18, 1942, off Diamond Shoals Light Buoy, North Carolina, the *Acme* was struck by a torpedo fired from the German submarine U-124 [4]. The *Dione*, operating in proximity to Allied shipping near the destroyer USS *Dickerson* and several merchantmen, responded immediately to the distress signal [4]. Within minutes, the cutter arrived at the scene and began picking up survivors from the water. The rescue was executed with exceptional speed and efficiency; all 20 survivors, including Captain Sigismund Schulz, were recovered in "a little over 10 minutes" [4]. The cutter then transported the exhausted and traumatized men to Norfolk, Virginia, for medical attention and debriefing [4]. This swift and decisive action exemplified the professionalism and dedication of the Coast Guard crew. The *Dione*'s role in this incident highlights her dual function as both a combatant and a lifeline for those in distress. Her presence at the scene ensured that no life was lost needlessly, a testament to the cutter's operational readiness and the skill of its crew. Further documentation indicates that on the same day, the *Dione* also rescued 35 survivors from another vessel, the Greek freighter Kassandra Louloudi, further underscoring her commitment to saving lives amidst the chaos of war [6]. These rescue missions were not isolated incidents but a recurring theme of her service, demonstrating the cutter's unwavering humanitarian mission alongside its primary combat role.

Beyond her role as a rescuer, the *Dione* was an active participant in the hunt for German U-boats. A clear example of this aggressive stance occurred on June 15, 1942, when a series of German magnetic mines exploded in the Thimble Shoal Channel, a key navigational passage leading to Hampton Roads, Virginia [9]. The explosions created panic among the local shipping and military fleets. The *Dione* responded with alacrity, racing out from her position within 15 minutes of the first blast to investigate and assist the convoy escorting nearby [9]. As she searched the area, the cutter's sonar detected a contact. Acting on this information, the *Dione* dropped a spread of eight depth charges on the presumed location of the submarine [9]. Shortly after the charges detonated, a ninth explosion was triggered, confirming the success of the attack and likely destroying the U-boat responsible for laying the mines [9]. This incident moved the *Dione* from a defensive posture to an offensive one, directly engaging and neutralizing an enemy threat. It showcased her advanced sensor suite and the tactical acumen of her crew, who were able to identify a threat and execute a counterattack with precision. The cutter operated as part of a larger, integrated defense network under the command of Vice Admiral Adolphus Andrews, coordinating with U.S. Navy patrol aircraft and other naval escorts to secure the vital approaches to Hampton Roads [9]. This level of coordination was essential for the success of the Atlantic defense effort, and the *Dione* was a key player in this complex web of military activity.

The operational environment in which the *Dione* served was incredibly hazardous. The Eastern Sea Frontier was a killing ground for Allied shipping, and every patrol carried the risk of encountering a hostile U-boat. The cutter's logbooks from the WWII period, which are preserved in the National Archives, contain numerous entries documenting her patrols, maintenance records, and crew assignments during this intense period of service [11], [12]. These records provide a glimpse into the daily life of the crew, detailing their movements along the East Coast and their constant vigilance against the unseen submarine threat lurking beneath the waves. The *Dione* was part of the U.S. Coast Guard's coastal defense response during Operation Drumbeat, a period of unprecedented losses for Allied shipping due to the sudden and devastating effectiveness of German wolf packs [9]. The cutter's service was not limited to heroic rescues and successful attacks; it also involved the grim duty of recovering bodies and wreckage from sunken vessels, a somber reminder of the war's brutal cost. Despite the dangers, the crew of the *Dione* maintained a high state of readiness, driven by a sense of duty and the knowledge that their efforts were crucial to protecting the flow of troops and supplies across the Atlantic. The collective service of the B-class cutters, including the *Dione*, was instrumental in turning the tide of the Battle of the Atlantic and ensuring the eventual defeat of the German submarine force.

## Post-War Service and Decommissioning

Following the conclusion of hostilities in Europe, the *USCGC Dione* (WPC-107) participated in one of the most historically significant events of the war's final days: the surrender of German U-boats. In May and June of 1945, the cutter was assigned to the Navy's "Surrender Group" within the 1st Naval District [8]. Alongside her sister cutters, USCGCs *Nemesis* and *Argo*, the *Dione* was tasked with processing the formal surrender of German submarines in American waters [8]. This was a monumental logistical and diplomatic undertaking, requiring meticulous organization and coordination. The group was responsible for receiving at least five surrendered U-boats, including the famous *U-234*, *U-805*, *U-873*, *U-1228*, and *U-858* [8]. The *U-234* held particular historical significance, as it was carrying a cargo of senior German officers, technicians, and 1,200 pounds of uranium oxide, materials of immense strategic value to the Allied powers [8]. The *Dione*'s role in this operation was to ensure the safe arrival, control, and decontamination of these vessels and their crews, marking the end of the submarine campaign that had terrorized the Atlantic for so long. This duty provided a fitting finale to her wartime service, transitioning her from a frontline combatant to a witness and participant in the peaceful resolution of the conflict.

After the surrender ceremonies were complete, the *Dione* remained in active Coast Guard service, continuing her duties in the postwar era [8]. The Coast Guard, having proven its mettle in combat, retained a significant portion of its wartime fleet. Eight of the original sixteen B-class cutters, including the *Dione*, were still active as of 1953, a testament to their robust design and enduring utility [8]. These vessels were re-designated as High Endurance Cutters (WHEC), reflecting their expanded roles in peacetime missions such as fisheries enforcement, environmental protection, search and rescue, and drug interdiction. The experience gained during the war had made the Coast Guard a more versatile and powerful maritime service, and the B-class cutters were central to this transformation. They served as the backbone of the Coast Guard's fleet for decades, undergoing numerous upgrades and modifications to keep pace with changing technologies and operational requirements. The *Dione* would have been one of these stalwarts, contributing to the service's mission of protecting America's maritime interests long after the guns of World War II fell silent.

The ultimate fate of the *Dione* was her disposal. After decades of faithful service, the cutter was eventually decommissioned and discarded from the Coast Guard fleet [3]. The exact date of her decommissioning is not specified in the available sources, but it is known to have occurred sometime in the 1960s or 1970s [10]. The final B-class cutter, USCGC *Ariadne* (WPC-101), was not retired until December 23, 1968, indicating that the *Dione* was likely among the last of her class to leave active service [8]. The process of disposal for naval vessels of this era typically involved either being sold for scrap, used for target practice, or broken down for parts. While the precise circumstances of the *Dione*'s final disposition are unknown, her retirement marked the end of an era for the Coast Guard's B-class fleet. These vessels, which had fought in the Battle of the Atlantic and witnessed the surrender of the Third Reich, were gradually replaced by newer, more technologically advanced cutters designed for the challenges of the Cold War and beyond. The story of the *Dione* and her sisters is a complete narrative arc, from their construction in the 1930s to their final retirement decades later, representing a significant chapter in the history of the United States Coast Guard and its indispensable role in national defense.

## Legacy

The legacy of the *USCGC Dione* (WPC-107) extends far beyond her individual service record. As a representative of the 165-foot "B"-class patrol cutters, she embodies the critical role that the United States Coast Guard played in World War II, a role that often went unrecognized compared to the exploits of the Army and Navy. These eighteen cutters, though modest in size, were instrumental in the Allied victory in the Battle of the Atlantic. They were the unsung heroes of the coastal defense, tirelessly patrolling the vulnerable coastline and confronting the German U-boat threat head-on. The *Dione*'s own actions—rescuing dozens of lives from sinking ships, successfully attacking enemy submarines, and participating in the surrender of U-boats—stand as powerful testaments to the courage, skill, and dedication of the Coast Guard's enlisted men and officers [4], [8], [9]. Her story helps to illuminate the full scope of the Coast Guard's contribution, showcasing its transformation from a domestic law enforcement agency into a formidable naval force integral to the nation's survival.

The impact of the B-class cutters on the outcome of the war cannot be overstated. Before the United States entered the war, the Coast Guard was the only armed maritime service with significant experience in anti-submarine warfare. When the U-boat campaign began in earnest, these cutters were among the first American forces deployed to confront the threat [5]. Their presence, coupled with their specialized expertise, helped to blunt the initial German onslaught and buy valuable time for the Allies to develop more effective countermeasures, such as improved radar, sonar, and code-breaking capabilities. The *Dione* and her sisters were the vanguard of this effort, operating in some of the most dangerous waters in the world. Their persistence and resilience helped to protect vital supply lines, ensuring that troops and materiel could cross the Atlantic safely to fight in Europe and the Pacific. Without the steadfast service of these vessels, the course of the war might have been very different. The *Dione*'s legacy is therefore intertwined with the broader narrative of the Battle of the Atlantic, a hard-fought struggle that remains one of the most important campaigns in modern history.

In conclusion, the *USCGC Dione* serves as a powerful symbol of the adaptability and enduring spirit of the Coast Guard. Her journey from a peacetime patrol boat to a hardened combat vessel and back to a postwar service icon encapsulates the service's evolution during a century of profound change. The technological upgrades she received—the installation of powerful guns, depth charges, radar, and sonar—reflect the rapid innovation that characterized the war. The expansion of her crew complement from 44 to 75 personnel demonstrates the human dimension of this transformation, highlighting the thousands of Coast Guardsmen who manned these ships and gave their lives in defense of freedom [3], [8]. The preservation of her story, documented in logs at the National Archives and recounted in historical analyses, ensures that her legacy and that of her class will not be forgotten [2], [11], [12]. The *Dione* and her sisters proved that a small, dedicated force, operating with professionalism and courage, could make a monumental difference on the world stage.
