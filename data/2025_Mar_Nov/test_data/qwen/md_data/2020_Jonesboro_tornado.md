# The March 28, 2020 Jonesboro Tornado

On Saturday, March 28, 2020, a powerful tornado struck Jonesboro, Arkansas, causing extensive destruction across the city [1], [3]. Officially rated an EF3 on the Enhanced Fujita Scale, the storm formed in southeast Jonesboro and tracked northeast through populated areas before dissipating just sixteen minutes later [1], [2]. Despite the tornado's intensity and its path directly through homes and businesses, it resulted in zero fatalities, a remarkable outcome attributed in part to the concurrent COVID-19 pandemic which reduced occupancy in public spaces [6], [13], [15]. The event caused catastrophic damage to key commercial properties, most notably The Mall at Turtle Creek, and inflicted an estimated $300 million to $500 million in economic losses [2], [5]. The aftermath prompted significant emergency response efforts, a protracted recovery spanning years, and a fundamental transformation of the city's commercial landscape following the eventual demolition and sale of the damaged mall property [10], [21]. The event serves as a case study in meteorological phenomena, disaster response, and the complex interplay between natural hazards and societal context.

## Meteorological Conditions and Event Characteristics

The tornado that impacted Jonesboro on March 28, 2020, developed within a highly unstable atmospheric environment conducive to severe thunderstorms and tornadogenesis [7]. It occurred during Arkansas's peak tornado season and within the typical daily window for such events, just before 5:00 p.m. Central Daylight Time (CDT) [6]. The National Weather Service Storm Prediction Center had issued a "Particularly Dangerous Situation" tornado watch for parts of the region earlier that day, highlighting the elevated risk [17]. The meteorological setup featured several critical ingredients for a damaging tornado. A rapidly intensifying low-pressure system brought unseasonably warm and moist air into the area, while a strong low-level jet increased wind speeds to between 30 and 45 knots at approximately 5,000 feet above mean sea level over northeast Arkansas during the afternoon hours [7], [17]. At 2100 UTC (4:00 p.m. CDT), localized parameters near Jonesboro indicated a mixed-layer convective available potential energy (MLCAPE) greater than 75 J/kg and storm-relative helicity (SRH) in the 0–1 km layer ranging from 150 to 190 m²/s², both favorable for rotating updrafts necessary for tornado formation [7]. A crucial factor was the presence of a subtle eastward-drifting boundary, analyzed by the NWS as a cold front, which stalled near Jonesboro and provided critical low-level convergence and shear that likely contributed to the tornado's development [7].

The tornado itself touched down around 4:57 or 4:58 p.m. CDT on March 28, 2020 [2], [6]. The National Weather Service issued a tornado warning at 4:47 p.m. CDT, providing residents with a ten-minute lead time before the tornado was visually confirmed moving across the city [6]. The storm was short-lived but intensely destructive, lasting only 16 minutes before dissipating at approximately 5:14 p.m. CDT [2]. Its path was relatively narrow but concentrated its power along a 20.20 km (12.55 miles) track [1], [2]. The maximum width of the tornado was recorded at 550 meters (approximately 600 yards or one-third of a mile) [2], [6]. The storm originated in southeast Jonesboro and moved in a northeast direction, impacting a densely populated corridor of the city [1]. The event was documented by multiple sources, including the NOAA Storm Events Database, which serves as the authoritative record for official storm data compiled by the National Weather Service [8]. The National Weather Service office in Memphis, Tennessee, was responsible for the official rating and assessment of the storm [13].

The primary measure of the tornado's intensity is the Enhanced Fujita (EF) Scale, which was adopted by the National Weather Service in 2007 [1]. An independent structural engineering firm, Haag Engineering Company, also conducted a detailed damage survey in November 2022, using advanced methods like Google Street View and historical imagery to assess damage that had been obscured by rapid post-event cleanup [2]. Their survey, titled 'Damage survey of the Jonesboro, Arkansas tornado: March 28, 2020', provided specific wind speed estimates for individual structures, revealing that damage varied significantly depending on construction type and location [2]. For instance, they estimated winds of 110 mph (equivalent to an EF1 rating) at Cheddar's Scratch Kitchen, while other structures sustained far more severe damage [2]. This highlights how the overall rating represents a composite of the most intense impacts along the path. The official rating of EF3 indicates estimated peak winds of 136 to 140 mph (61 to 62 m/s) [1], [2]. This rating was determined during a rapid damage survey conducted by NWS personnel on March 29, 2020, the day after the event [1]. The survey utilized the Damage Assessment Toolkit (DAT) and involved over 80 scientists working to standardize the application of the EF scale [1]. The immediate post-event cleanup complicated documentation, making damage details "perishable" and underscoring the challenges faced by surveyors [1]. The event also exposed gaps in the EF scale's coverage for large shopping malls, prompting future updates to damage indicators [1].

## Damage Assessment and Structural Impact

The EF3 tornado unleashed catastrophic damage as it traversed Jonesboro, leaving a trail of destruction across hundreds of buildings [1]. The most severely affected structure was The Mall at Turtle Creek, which suffered catastrophic damage that rendered a significant portion of the building uninhabitable and ultimately led to its demolition [10], [14]. Employees who sheltered in an interior break room inside the mall confirmed that the room remained intact while the surrounding structure collapsed, illustrating the extreme forces at play [10]. The damage included the complete loss of the roof in the mall's center section, collapsed exterior walls, widespread ceiling collapse, shattered storefront windows, and the destruction of numerous shops stretching from Dillard’s to Target [10]. City code enforcement officers later declared the damaged eastern portion of the mall, extending from Victoria’s Secret to Dillard’s, a public hazard against public health and safety, effectively marking it for demolition [12], [14]. The mall, which opened in 2006, was the largest enclosed mall in northeast Arkansas and had been the only new enclosed mall constructed in the United States that year [19].

In addition to the mall, the tornado inflicted severe damage at Jonesboro Municipal Airport, where hangars and airplanes were destroyed [1], [6], [13]. Associated gusts from the tornado were powerful enough to derail a train [13]. Other retail establishments also suffered extensive damage, with some buildings, including Ulta and Kirkland stores, collapsing completely [1]. The storm's path through residential neighborhoods resulted in the destruction or severe damage of over 140 homes and more than 100 businesses overall [11], [12]. One source noted EF-2 damage at the Turtle Creek Mall prior to it strengthening to EF-3 as the tornado continued northeast [6]. The damage was so severe that the Dean B. Ellis Library closed early to allow staff to comply with a city-wide curfew implemented after the storm [11]. The campus of nearby Arkansas State University experienced only minor damage, but at least three employees lost their homes, and many students residing in the area suffered similar housing damage [11].

The independent damage survey conducted by Haag Engineering in November 2022 provided a granular analysis of the structural failures observed [2]. By utilizing Google Street View and historical imagery to circumvent the challenges of post-storm cleanup, the survey team was able to document specific wind speed estimates and patterns of failure across various types of structures [2]. Their work highlighted the complexities of assessing tornado damage, particularly for large, modern commercial buildings like shopping malls, which have different construction characteristics compared to single-family homes [2]. The survey found that most of the most severe damage occurred at the airport and the aforementioned retail stores, confirming the NWS's high-end EF3 rating for those locations [1]. The report meticulously documented structural failures across residential, industrial, and commercial sites, contributing to a more nuanced understanding of the tornado's impact than a single, generalized EF rating could provide [2]. This detailed engineering perspective revealed that while the official rating was based on the worst-case scenario observed, the actual wind field was likely variable, with different structures experiencing different levels of force along the tornado's path [2]. This variability underscores the difficulty in assigning a precise and uniform wind speed to the entire storm, reinforcing the value of comprehensive, multi-point damage surveys for accurately characterizing such events.

## Human Toll and Emergency Response

Despite the tornado's EF3 intensity and its direct path through populated parts of Jonesboro, the most striking outcome was the absence of fatalities [1], [2], [3]. The number of injuries is reported inconsistently across various sources, with figures cited as 18, 22, and at least 22 people injured [2], [6], [15]. The higher figure of 22 injuries is supported by multiple reports and will be considered the most accurate count [2], [15]. Multiple sources attribute this remarkably positive casualty outcome directly to the ongoing COVID-19 pandemic and the social distancing measures in place at the time [6], [13], [15]. With many residents staying home due to lockdown orders and public businesses operating under reduced capacity, the population density in vulnerable indoor spaces like malls and offices was significantly lower than it would have been on a normal day [6], [13]. This reduction in occupancy likely prevented a much higher death toll, turning a potentially deadly event into one with zero lives lost [6], [15]. The effectiveness of the tornado warning dissemination via NOAA Weather Radio, cell alerts, and local media also played a critical role in allowing residents to seek shelter in time [6].

The human stories from the event illustrate both the tragedy and resilience of the community. Lucas Luster, a driver for the Jonesboro Fire Department, called his family moments before the tornado hit his house, which was subsequently destroyed [3]. Fortunately, all his family members survived [3]. Another compelling account comes from Sharon Gore, who was at her home near the mall with her adult son, Preston, who has nonverbal autism. During the tornado warning, she used Oreo mini cookies, a known behavioral intervention for him, to coax him from his fixed "dinner chair" so they could move to a safer back bedroom [18]. Her experience provides a vivid example of adaptive behavior under extreme stress and the importance of personalized safety plans for individuals with special needs [18]. After the storm passed, her son's missing dinner chair was found intact but blown out onto the yard, offering a poignant illustration of the localized and chaotic nature of wind forces during a tornado [18].

The emergency response in Jonesboro was swift and continuous. First responders were activated immediately upon the tornado warning [3]. Jonesboro Fire Chief Marty Hamrick described responding to the event while watching movies with his own family, expecting a high number of casualties given the tornado's path through heavily populated areas [3]. All four personnel units of the fire department were deployed during the incident, and firefighters worked continuously throughout the night and into the next day to recover people and manage the aftermath [3]. Search and rescue operations were underway at The Mall at Turtle Creek, according to E-911 Director Jeff Presley [17]. By the morning of March 29, 2020, the day after the tornado, the fire department reported that "everybody was accounted for," a testament to the effective warning systems and public response [3]. In the immediate aftermath, the city implemented a city-wide curfew from 9 p.m. to daylight to maintain order and ensure public safety amidst widespread power outages and debris [11], [13]. The Craighead County Office of Emergency Management did not pre-activate its emergency plans because the criteria for activation were not met prior to the storm; however, it did activate its emergency operations center five times for subsequent storm warnings in the period following the tornado, indicating a reactive learning process [15]. Following the event, the Jonesboro Fire Department specifically refined its severe weather response plan, emphasizing the need for pre-identified staging areas for incoming mutual aid to improve coordination during future disasters [3].

## Economic Consequences and Federal Assistance

The economic impact of the March 28, 2020 tornado was profound, inflicting hundreds of millions of dollars in damage on the city of Jonesboro and Craighead County [5], [20]. Estimates of the total damage varied between official state figures and private assessments. State officials, in their request for federal assistance, estimated the total damages from the tornado and associated severe storms at nearly $500 million [5], [20]. Governor Asa Hutchinson stated the figure was "almost half a billion dollars in damage to the City of Jonesboro," a sum deemed sufficient to qualify for a major federal disaster declaration [20]. A separate, independent estimate placed the economic losses at $300 million (in 2020 USD) [2]. These figures encompass the extensive damage to residential properties, commercial buildings like The Mall at Turtle Creek, the municipal airport, and other infrastructure [1], [12]. The financial burden of such a disaster created significant compounding stress for the community, as the economic impact from the tornado coincided with the economic downturn caused by the COVID-19 pandemic [5].

The severity of the damage and the scale of the recovery effort justified a major federal disaster declaration. President Donald Trump approved a declaration for Jonesboro and Craighead County in late April 2020, enabling FEMA public assistance programs to aid individuals, households, government agencies, and utilities [5]. The formal request for federal aid was submitted by Arkansas in early April 2020, citing both the direct impacts of the tornadoes and the broader economic stress resulting from the concurrent pandemic [5]. This federal assistance was crucial for funding the extensive cleanup and rebuilding processes. Key city infrastructure and services, including Jonesboro Municipal Airport, City Water & Light, and Craighead Electric, became eligible for federal support [5]. The approval of the declaration underscored the systemic nature of the damage, which extended beyond individual homes and businesses to critical public services essential for the community's functioning and recovery [5]. The preliminary damage assessment (PDA) process, coordinated by FEMA with state and local partners, was initiated to formally document the extent of the damage and validate the need for this federal aid [4]. This process involves phases of local and state verification of damage to determine the magnitude of the disaster and inform decisions about major disaster declarations [4].

The long-term economic repercussions were significant, particularly for the local commercial sector. The prolonged closure and eventual demolition of The Mall at Turtle Creek represented a substantial blow to the regional economy, affecting employment and consumer activity [10], [21]. The city's efforts to manage the aftermath of the mall's destruction were a central focus of its economic recovery strategy. Mayor Harold Copenhaver, who took office in January 2021, prioritized restoring full mall operations, especially from the west side outward, citing concerns about the unrepaired site being an "eyesore" and a danger to the community [10]. The city's proactive management of the blighted property, from issuing notices to the owner to planning for demolition, demonstrated a strategic approach to mitigating the long-term negative effects on public safety and economic development [10], [12]. The eventual sale of the land in September 2023 for redevelopment signaled a new chapter for the property, with plans for a 96,000-square-foot open-air power center projected to begin construction in 2026 [21]. This multi-year transition from devastation to redevelopment highlights the enduring economic legacy of the tornado on the city's commercial landscape.

## Aftermath, Recovery, and Long-Term Repercussions

The aftermath of the March 28, 2020 tornado was characterized by a slow and methodical recovery process that spanned several years, fundamentally altering the physical and economic landscape of Jonesboro [10], [21]. The immediate priority was clearing debris and ensuring public safety, which led to the implementation of a city-wide curfew [11], [13]. However, the most visible and impactful aspect of the recovery was the fate of The Mall at Turtle Creek. Recognizing the structural compromise of the damaged sections, city officials took decisive action. In January 2021, approximately ten months after the tornado, Mayor Harold Copenhaver issued a 30-day notice to Brookfield Properties, the Chicago-based owner of the mall, requiring them to submit a remediation plan [12], [14]. Failure to comply would trigger city council action, potentially leading to mandatory demolition under a 60-day demolition permit requirement [12]. This enforcement action was driven by the city's desire to address the unrepaired site, which was designated a public hazard and nuisance against public health [12], [14].

Demolition of the most severely damaged central section of the mall began on March 15, 2021 [10], [14]. The city's engineering assessment concluded that while the internal structure was sound, the extensive roof damage, water intrusion, and missing drywall made the section unsalvageable [10]. The removal of the blighted section was a critical step in revitalizing the property. The remaining anchor stores—Dillard’s, JCPenney, and Target—remained operational on perimeter outparcels, serving as a base for future redevelopment [19], [21]. The property itself eventually changed hands. On September 10, 2023, the land was sold at an online auction for $4.85 million to Turtle Creek Jonesboro LLC, an entity potentially led by real estate investor Keith Bayird of Paragould [21]. This sale marked the beginning of a new era for the site, with a redevelopment plan announced for a 96,000-square-foot open-air power center [21]. Construction on this new development is estimated to begin in 2026, representing a projected timeline of over six years from the initial disaster to the start of reconstruction [21].

This protracted recovery process illustrates the long shadow cast by a major disaster. The event not only caused immediate destruction but also necessitated years of planning, legal action, and investment to restore the area to a functional and safe condition. The story of The Mall at Turtle Creek's transformation from a catastrophic damage site to a redeveloped commercial hub is emblematic of the city's resilience. The redevelopment plan aims to create a modern retail space that replaces the outdated enclosed mall, reflecting a shift in commercial real estate trends even before the tornado struck [21]. Furthermore, the tornado prompted tangible improvements in local emergency management protocols. The Jonesboro Fire Department, having witnessed the chaos firsthand, refined its severe weather response plan, placing a stronger emphasis on the identification of pre-designated staging areas for incoming mutual aid to streamline future disaster response efforts [3]. In summary, the March 28, 2020 tornado was not merely a single event but a catalyst for a multi-faceted recovery that reshaped the city's infrastructure, economy, and preparedness strategies for years to come.
