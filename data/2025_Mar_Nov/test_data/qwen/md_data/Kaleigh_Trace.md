# Kaleigh Trace

Kaleigh Trace (born 1986) is a Canadian non-fiction writer, sex educator, and disability activist known for her candid and unapologetic exploration of sexuality, queerness, and disability [14]. Her most prominent work, the memoir *Hot, Wet and Shaking: How I Learned to Talk About Sex*, centers her lived experience as a disabled person to challenge societal taboos and normative depictions of sexuality [7], [10]. Through her writing, public speaking, and therapeutic practice, Trace has become a significant voice in advocating for the desirability, autonomy, and inclusion of disabled bodies within sexual health frameworks [1], [12]. Her advocacy is imbued with a profound sense of urgency, stemming from a terminal cancer diagnosis she received in October 2022, which has framed her life's work as a testament to "radical aliveness" in the face of mortality [6], [9]. This dual focus on social critique and personal affirmation defines her impact on contemporary discourse surrounding disability, feminism, and sex positivity.

## Early Life and Formative Experiences

Kaleigh Trace’s identity and subsequent career trajectory are deeply rooted in the formative experiences of her early life, particularly a catastrophic accident that resulted in a permanent physical disability [2], [3]. Born in 1986, her life took a defining turn at a young age when she sustained severe injuries in a car accident [6], [9]. The sources provide slightly different ages for the incident, with one indicating she was eight years old and another nine [3], [4], [6]. Regardless of the precise year, the timing was crucial, occurring during childhood at a period when bodily awareness, self-perception, and the internalization of societal norms are being established [2]. This accident resulted in a spinal cord injury that led to a visible physical disability, fundamentally shaping her relationship with her own body and her place in the world [3], [7].

The physical manifestations of her disability have been consistent across accounts. She grew up ambulating with an unsteady gait, described as swaying, scissoring legs, and experiencing frequent falls [6]. During her childhood, she also used a wheelchair [7]. This physical reality became a source of both hyperawareness and alienation. As she aged, she experienced increasing feelings of estrangement due to the visible differences between her body and the narrow cultural beauty standards prevalent in the mid-2000s, which excluded disabled and queer identities [2], [6]. These early experiences of navigating a world not built for her body laid the groundwork for her later work as an activist who challenges systemic devaluation of disabled people through inaccessible infrastructure, public staring, and media underrepresentation [5]. Her identity as a disabled person is not viewed as a limitation but as one facet of a complex, multifaceted identity that informs every aspect of her life and work [7].

Trace identifies as a queer woman, a key component of her intersecting identities [2], [4]. This aspect of her identity intersects with her disability in her work, where she challenges rigid societal norms around sexuality, gender, and representation [10], [11]. Her feminist perspective further frames her analysis of power, privilege, and systemic injustice, particularly within sexual health contexts [2], [12]. This tripartite identity—disabled, queer, and feminist—is foundational to her worldview and the lens through which she engages with the world as a writer and educator [2], [7]. Growing up, she had to learn her body and how to be sexual within it in a way that was different from most people, a journey she chronicles extensively in her memoir [4]. This process of self-discovery, forged in the crucible of her unique experiences, became the raw material for her groundbreaking advocacy and writing.

Her educational background reflects a path toward understanding human relationships and well-being. She graduated from Dalhousie University in Halifax with a Bachelor of Arts in 2009 [6]. Later, she pursued advanced studies in therapy, beginning a Master of Science program in Couples and Family Therapy at the University of Guelph in 2017 while commuting from Toronto [6]. This academic pursuit represents a significant evolution in her career, moving from direct public education and activism into clinical practice. As a student, she already identified as a Registered Psychotherapist with the College of Registered Psychotherapists of Ontario, framing her therapeutic work as "justice-doing," where she integrates a critical analysis of systems of power and privilege into her sessions [12]. This formal training provides a structured methodology to complement the experiential knowledge she gained through her earlier work, allowing her to contribute to healing shame and creating inclusive spaces in a professional therapeutic context [12]. Her educational journey underscores her commitment to both theoretical understanding and practical application in her mission to foster healthier, more equitable conversations about sex and identity.

## Career and Major Works

Kaleigh Trace’s career is anchored by her debut memoir, *Hot, Wet and Shaking: How I Learned to Talk About Sex*, a work that has become the cornerstone of her public identity and a significant contribution to contemporary non-fiction literature [3], [7]. The book originated from a personal blog she wrote called 'The Fucking Facts', which documented her experiences with sexual pleasure as a disabled woman [5], [6], [10]. This blog served as the foundation upon which the memoir was built, capturing her journey toward embracing her identity as both queer and disabled [10]. The book was first published in 2014 by Invisible Publishing [8], [9]. Its release was met with immediate attention and positive reception, with one reviewer noting having heard many good things about it prior to purchasing it at the Toronto International Book Fair in November 2014 [10]. The memoir explores her path to sexual self-understanding through a series of candid, humorous, and vulnerable personal narratives that spare no details [7], [11]. It is described as accessible, authentic, and free of theoretical abstraction, instead offering intimate stories that feel like confiding in a best friend [8]. This relatable tone lowers barriers for readers confronting difficult and taboo subjects.

The content of *Hot, Wet and Shaking* directly confronts and dismantles normative, able-bodied, homogeneous depictions of sexuality [7]. The book delves into a wide range of topics that are often considered unspeakable, including bladder accessibility challenges, potty humor in intimate contexts, explorations of sex toys, positions, and masturbation [4]. It also addresses struggles with orgasm, bodily autonomy, and street harassment [7]. A particularly poignant chapter, titled 'Looking for Blood,' details her abortion experience and highlights the barriers to reproductive choice faced by women in Nova Scotia [7]. The narrative is rich with specific anecdotes, such as early experiments with sex education using a banana and plum, accidentally retrieving a sex toy in a supermarket, and being caught masturbating while driving [7]. By centering her own lived experience, Trace rejects the dominant literary approach that seeks to define or deconstruct disability and instead presents it as an inherent, untheorized part of her personal story [8]. This approach affirms diversity, authenticity, and unashamed self-definition [7]. The book won the Evelyn Richardson Non-Fiction Award, cementing its critical acclaim [9].

The enduring relevance of her work is demonstrated by its continued publication and engagement. In June 2024, the book was republished in a 10th-anniversary edition by Invisible Publishing [6], [9]. This new edition included additional essays and a foreword by author Christa Couture, signaling the book's lasting influence and its continued importance in the conversation around disability and sexuality [9]. The act of editing this anniversary edition while planning for her death was described by Trace as an "editorial conversation with her younger self," conducted amid a prognosis of two years to live [9]. This context adds a profound layer of meaning to the memoir, reframing it not just as a piece of social commentary but as a conscious practice of radical aliveness [9]. The book's back cover describes its contents as discussions of "her sexual exploits, bodily negotiations and attempts at adulthood, sparing none of the details and assuming you are not polite company," a fitting summary of its bold and unflinching nature [11]. The success of the book and its anniversary edition confirms its resonance with audiences seeking more inclusive and honest narratives about sex and disability [9].

Beyond her memoir, Trace maintains an active online presence through her website, kaleightrace.com, which succeeded her original blog, thefuckingfacts.com [5]. While the provided sources do not detail recent writings beyond her blog posts and the memoir, her continued existence as a writer and sex educator suggests an ongoing engagement with these themes [2]. Her work ethic emphasizes listening, kindness, and empathy, principles she applies in all her communication about sex [5]. This commitment to fostering comfortable and honest dialogue is a hallmark of her entire career, from her earliest blog posts to her later therapeutic practice [2], [12]. The progression from a personal blog to a critically acclaimed, award-winning memoir illustrates a clear and impactful trajectory, establishing her as a pioneering author whose work has reshaped the landscape of sex-positive and disability-inclusive literature.

## Professional Roles and Activism

Kaleigh Trace’s professional life is a tapestry woven from multiple roles that collectively advance her mission to dismantle societal taboos and create inclusive spaces for marginalized voices. Her work as a sex educator, therapist, and public advocate is consistently informed by her identity as a disabled queer woman and her feminist politics [2], [12]. One of the most formative and frequently cited aspects of her career was her five-year tenure working at Venus Envy, a queer, feminist, and education-based sex-and-book shop in Halifax, Nova Scotia [3], [5], [9]. She began working there at the age of 23 after graduating from university [6]. This experience was transformative for her, described as "revolutionary" for the way it reshaped her understanding of human desirability and value [3]. At Venus Envy, she ran workshops on various sex-related topics and focused on dismantling misinformation, fostering honest dialogue, and providing a space where people could ask questions without shame [2], [4]. This hands-on work in a community-facing sex shop provided her with invaluable experience in direct education and allowed her to see firsthand the diverse ways people engage with their sexuality, which later informed her writing and public speaking [3].

Following her time in direct education, Trace pursued a Master of Science in Couple & Family Therapy from the University of Guelph, commencing her studies in 2017 [6]. This academic path marks a significant shift towards clinical practice, positioning her to apply her lived experience and passion for justice within a therapeutic framework. As a registered psychotherapist, she integrates a critical analysis of systems of power and privilege into her work, framing therapy itself as "justice-doing work" [12]. This approach allows her to address individual and relational issues while simultaneously challenging the broader social structures that create harm and limit access to well-being for marginalized communities, including disabled individuals [12]. Her therapeutic practice is therefore an extension of her activism, applying her skills to help heal shame and create more inclusive and equitable relationships at an interpersonal level [12]. Her work at Venus Envy, spanning a decade before becoming a therapist, shows a deep and sustained commitment to sexual health education, demonstrating a clear progression from public-facing outreach to private therapeutic intervention [12].

As an activist, Trace’s work is centered on challenging harmful misconceptions about disability and sex. She explicitly names the biggest misconception as the assumption that disabled people lack sexual desire or partners because they are perceived as undesirable [3]. By openly discussing her own sexuality, desires, and experiences, she actively refutes this pervasive stereotype [1]. Her activism is inherently intersectional, addressing the interconnectedness of disability, queerness, race, fatness, age, and representation [10]. For example, in her writing, she discusses the intersection of femme presentation with dyke identity, exploring the nuances of gendered appearance norms [11]. She also advocates for the valuation of disabled bodies within sexual health frameworks, arguing against the systemic devaluation of disabled people that manifests in inaccessible infrastructure and media underrepresentation [5]. Her collaboration with fellow disability activist Andrew Gurza on his podcast, 'Disability After Dark', to discuss inclusive sex education and consent exemplifies her commitment to public dialogue on these crucial issues [1]. Trace believes that people don't know how to understand disabled bodies as desirable, and her work is a direct attempt to change that fundamental lack of understanding [1].

Trace draws inspiration from a community of disabled activists and writers whom she cites as role models. These include Loree Erikson, Andrew Gurza, Eli Clare, Lyric Seal, Mia Mingus, and Mia Gimp [5]. Engaging with their work situates her own within a larger tradition of disability justice and queer liberation. Her activism is not limited to writing; it extends to her public presence as a speaker and performer. She has participated in literary events, given readings, and engaged in book tours, using these platforms to share her work and connect with audiences directly [11], [13]. Her participation in these events, often alongside other LGBTQ+ authors and artists, reinforces her standing within the literary and activist communities [13]. Through these varied professional roles and forms of activism, Trace builds a multi-pronged approach to social change, combining public education, clinical practice, creative expression, and direct community engagement to challenge ableism and promote a more inclusive vision of human sexuality.

## Personal Life and Terminal Illness

Kaleigh Trace’s personal life is intrinsically linked to her public work, as her identity as a queer, disabled woman serves as the foundation for her advocacy and writing. She resides in Toronto but splits her time with Nova Scotia, maintaining a connection to the city where she worked at Venus Envy and began her career [9]. This geographic duality reflects a balance between her professional life as a couples and family therapist and her roots in the community that shaped her early activism. Her identity is consistently defined by the intersection of her queer, disabled, and feminist selves, a framework she uses to analyze the world and communicate her message [2], [7], [11]. This multifaceted identity is not a collection of separate traits but a cohesive whole that informs her perspective on everything from sex and relationships to social justice and healthcare.

A profoundly significant and defining aspect of her life, which adds a layer of urgency and poignancy to her entire body of work, is her health status. In early 2024, she was diagnosed with terminal cancer and was given a prognosis of two years to live [9]. The exact details of her diagnosis, such as the type or stage of cancer, are not provided in the available sources, which is appropriate for an encyclopedic entry focusing on her public persona and contributions rather than private medical information [6]. However, the diagnosis itself is a central biographical fact that shapes the interpretation of her legacy. It is the context for her final major project: the 10th-anniversary re-publication of her memoir, *Hot, Wet and Shaking* [9]. She described editing this new edition as an "editorial conversation with her younger self," a task she undertook while consciously planning for her death [9].

This framing transforms her life's work from a simple advocacy project into a powerful testament to "radical aliveness." The phrase, cited from psychoanalyst D.W. Winnicott—"Oh God! May I be alive when I die!"—captures the spirit of her actions [9]. Rather than retreating from public life, she chose to amplify her voice and refine her message in the face of her mortality. Her writing and activism become acts of defiance against the stereotypes and assumptions she spends her life fighting. She is not portrayed as a passive object of pity or a cautionary tale; instead, she is an active agent creating meaning, building community, and leaving a lasting legacy. Her decision to publish a new edition of her memoir, a book that so intimately documents her body, desires, and vulnerabilities, becomes a profound statement about living fully and authentically until the very end. This context connects her personal journey to universal questions of mortality, purpose, and the human desire to be seen, valued, and remembered. Her work thus gains a deeper philosophical dimension, serving as an urgent call to embrace life and love in all its messy, imperfect, and beautiful complexity, especially in the face of its inevitable conclusion.

## Legacy and Critical Reception

Kaleigh Trace’s legacy is that of a pioneering voice who has fundamentally altered the discourse around disability and sexuality in Canada and beyond. Her work is characterized by its radical honesty, its refusal to shy away from taboo subjects, and its unwavering commitment to centering the lived experience of a disabled person. Critics and readers have responded to her writing with significant acclaim, recognizing its groundbreaking nature. The memoir *Hot, Wet and Shaking* is widely praised for its innovative approach, which contrasts sharply with dominant academic or literary traditions that often seek to define, deconstruct, or theorize disability as a subject separate from the personal narrative [8]. Instead, Trace presents disability as an inherent, untheorized part of her story, a method lauded for its intimacy and accessibility [8]. The book's narrative style, described as candid, humorous, and vulnerable, has been credited with making difficult topics approachable and relatable, likened to confiding in a best friend [7], [8].

Her critical reception has been overwhelmingly positive, reflecting the resonance of her message. The memoir won the prestigious Evelyn Richardson Non-Fiction Award, a clear indicator of its literary merit and impact within the Canadian publishing industry [9]. Reviews have highlighted its importance in affirming diversity, authenticity, and unashamed self-definition, rejecting normative depictions of sexuality and celebrating the complexities of her identity [7]. The enduring popularity of the book, evidenced by its purchase at a major book fair shortly after its release and its subsequent 10th-anniversary re-publication, attests to its lasting relevance and influence [9], [10]. The re-publication, which included a new foreword by Christa Couture, further solidified its place as a canonical text in disability and LGBTQ literature [9]. Trace is recognized as a significant figure in the 'List of LGBTQ writers', a testament to her impact on queer literature and culture [14].

Ultimately, Kaleigh Trace’s legacy is twofold. First, she has made an indelible mark on literature and activism by creating a space where disabled, queer, and feminist voices can speak with authority and vulnerability about sex and desire. She has challenged the pervasive myth that disabled people are asexual, thereby contributing to a more inclusive and accurate understanding of human sexuality [3], [5]. Second, her legacy is profoundly shaped by her confrontation with mortality. Her terminal illness has transformed her work into a powerful testament to "radical aliveness," a conscious practice of being present and vibrant in the face of an imminent end [9]. This context gives her message a unique urgency and emotional depth. She leaves behind not only a body of influential writing but also an example of living authentically and defiantly until the last possible moment. Her life and work serve as a reminder of the power of storytelling to challenge stigma, inspire change, and find profound meaning in the face of mortality.
