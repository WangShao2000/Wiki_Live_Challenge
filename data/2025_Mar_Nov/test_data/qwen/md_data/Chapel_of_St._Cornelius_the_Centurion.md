# Chapel of St. Cornelius the Centurion

The term "Chapel of St. Cornelius the Centurion" refers not to a single, unique structure but to at least two distinct and geographically separate chapels in the United States that share this name [2], [13]. Both chapels are named for the biblical figure, St. Cornelius the Centurion, whose conversion marked a pivotal moment in Christian history by establishing the precedent for the inclusion of Gentiles into the Church on equal terms with Jewish converts [23], [26]. The first chapel is an English Gothic-style masonry building located on Governors Island in New York Harbor, constructed in 1906 to replace an earlier wooden chapel from 1847 [5], [6]. It stands within a National Historic Landmark District and has undergone significant adaptive reuse, now serving as a venue for contemporary art installations while remaining publicly accessible [7], [15]. The second chapel is a wood-frame Gothic Revival structure located within Fort Monroe in Hampton, Virginia; it was consecrated in 1858 and holds the distinction of being the U.S. Army's oldest wooden building used continuously for religious services [13], [19]. Its construction was funded through private subscription following a personal act of gratitude by a surviving officer after a laboratory explosion [13], [14]. While both chapels honor the same saint, their histories, architectural styles, funding origins, and current statuses are markedly different, reflecting unique developments within their respective military and civilian contexts.

## Governors Island Chapel, New York City

The Chapel of St. Cornelius the Centurion on Governors Island represents a complex history of military service, ecclesiastical transition, and civic transformation, culminating in its current role as a preserved historic landmark and cultural venue. Its narrative begins with a modest wooden predecessor, which itself arose from the growing need for a permanent Episcopal presence on the island's U.S. military installation [5], [6]. The initial chapel was erected in 1847 under the dedicated leadership of Reverend John McVickar, who had been commissioned as the post chaplain at Fort Columbus in 1844 [5], [8]. The project was a collaborative effort, receiving continuous support from Trinity Church in Manhattan, a major financial backer alongside McVickar's own personal contributions [5], [6]. The dedication of this first chapel occurred on April 19, 1847, officiated by Bishop William H. DeLancey of the Episcopal Diocese of Western New York [5]. The cost of this initial construction is reported with some variation across sources, with figures cited as $2,500 (a 1950 source), $4,500 (a 1973 source), or a more substantial $25,000 as mentioned in a letter from McVickar himself in 1846 [5]. Over time, this wooden structure fell into disrepair and was ultimately condemned by the early 20th century, necessitating the construction of a more durable replacement [5].

This replacement was the current chapel, a significant architectural undertaking designed by the American architect Charles Coolidge Haight [5], [21]. Construction took place between 1905 and 1906, marking a new era for the chapel and the island [5], [6]. The cornerstone for the new building was laid on October 27, 1905, by Coadjutor Bishop David H. Greer of the Episcopal Diocese of New York, and the chapel was formally consecrated on October 19, 1906 [5], [7]. The choice of Haight, a Union Army veteran who served under General Winfield Scott Hancock, may have been influenced by his military background, leading him to incorporate integrated military motifs, such as turrets, into the design of the chapel, which stood as a prominent feature on the former army base [20]. The chapel was built on the site of the original 1847 structure, and photographs from the period show the old and new buildings standing contemporaneously, with the new stone edifice positioned behind its wooden predecessor [16]. The chapel is situated in the Noland Park section of Governors Island, a location administered by the Governors Island Preservation and Education Corporation since January 31, 2003, following the island's transfer from the U.S. federal government [7], [10]. This entire area, including the chapel, is designated as both a National Historic Landmark District and a New York City Historic District, underscoring its significant historical and architectural value [7].

Architecturally, the chapel is a prime example of the English Gothic style of the 14th century, specifically executed in the Gothic Revival tradition [5], [7]. Constructed from buff Indiana limestone, the building measures approximately 106 feet in length and 70 feet in its greatest width [5], [7]. Its cruciform plan consists of a nave, transepts, and a chancel [7], [10]. A defining external feature is its three-story northwest tower, which is crowned with a four-turret parapet and surmounted by a slate roof with copper gutters [5]. The exterior is punctuated by 35 lancet-shaped stained glass windows, adding to its visual grandeur [5]. The interior retains white walls and houses a pipe organ [5]. Beneath the chancel lies a vaulted crypt and mortuary chapel, which features flat Italian tile roofing [7]. The chapel also includes carved oak clergy and choir seats, contributing to its traditional liturgical setting [7]. Architectural Preservation Studio was retained by Trinity Real Estate to conduct a comprehensive conditions assessment of the chapel, which included laser-scanning documentation of both the exterior and interior to produce highly accurate drawings for preservation purposes [10].

The chapel's ownership and operational history reflects the broader shifts in governance of Governors Island. Initially, the War Department provided chaplains and supported the facility [6]. However, a pivotal change occurred in 1868 when the War Department ceased providing chaplains, at which point the chapel became a free chapel operating directly under the control of Trinity Parish [6]. This transition solidified its identity as part of the parish rather than solely a military installation. In 1954, the chapel's use was expanded to include interdenominational Protestant services, broadening its community reach beyond its original Episcopal focus [6]. The chapel's relationship with the U.S. government reached a critical juncture in 1986, when Trinity Church formally donated the chapel to the U.S. Government [6]. Crucially, this donation was made with a reversion clause, stipulating that ownership would revert to Trinity if the land was ever returned to civilian use or if the chapel ceased to be used for a military purpose [6]. When the U.S. Coast Guard vacated Governors Island in 1996, this trigger was activated, and ownership legally reverted to Trinity Church, which has retained ownership to this day [6], [8]. Since that time, the chapel has been inactive for regular worship services [6]. Prior to its closure for worship, its nave displayed historic regimental flags, which were later relocated to the U.S. Army Center of Military History in Washington, D.C., in the 1980s [8]. The chapel's dedication to St. Cornelius the Centurion is reinforced through its liturgical elements, including a reredos with three military saints featuring Cornelius prominently on the right and a stained glass window depicting the biblical scene from Acts 10 [2]. The chapel's continued existence as a publicly accessible space and its integration into the island's cultural programming represent a successful adaptive reuse, transforming a former military chapel into a vibrant public destination [15].

## Chapel of the Centurion, Fort Monroe, Virginia

The Chapel of the Centurion at Fort Monroe in Hampton, Virginia, presents a narrative of enduring continuity, distinguished by its age, unique founding story, and unbroken service as a place of worship for the U.S. military. Unlike its counterpart in New York, this chapel was never replaced; it began life as a permanent wooden structure and has served continuously for religious services since its consecration in 1858 [13], [19]. Its construction was initiated in 1855 following a tragic event at the fort's arsenal—a laboratory explosion that killed two men but spared Lieutenant Julian McAllister [13], [14]. McAllister attributed his survival to divine intervention and, as a result, he commissioned the chapel and served as its principal donor, raising $6,000 through private subscription to fund its construction [13], [19]. This compelling origin story, rooted in personal faith and military experience, imbues the chapel with a unique historical character [14]. The chapel was designed by the renowned Gothic Revival architect Richard Upjohn, one of America's most influential architects of the 19th century [13], [17]. His plans, published in his 1852 pattern book 'Rural Architecture', were modified for the site, extending the structure to five bays to accommodate a congregation of 200-225 people [13]. The chapel was completed in 1857 and officially consecrated on May 3, 1858, by Bishop John Johns of St. John's Episcopal Church in Hampton [13], [17].

Architecturally, the chapel is a notable example of Upjohn's work, executed in the wood-frame Gothic Revival style [13], [19]. As one of the oldest wooden churches built on a U.S. Army post, it holds a special place in American architectural history [14], [19]. The chapel is located within the walls of Old Fort Monroe, at Old Point Comfort, on the edge of the Parade Ground facing northeast [17]. It stands adjacent to the Casemate Museum and other historic structures within the fort complex, which is now a National Monument administered by the National Park Service and the Fort Monroe Authority [17], [19]. The chapel's interior features three stained-glass memorial windows, which are attributed to the famous artist Louis Comfort Tiffany and his Tiffany Glass and Decorating Company of New York City, adding a significant artistic dimension to its simple wooden frame [13]. The chapel is named for Cornelius the Centurion, the Roman military officer who, according to the biblical account in Acts 10, was the first documented Gentile convert to Christianity, a theological principle of universal inclusion that resonates deeply within the context of a military garrison [14], [17]. Above the altar is a stained-glass window that depicts the biblical scene of Cornelius sending for Peter, visually connecting the chapel's mission to its foundational story [14].

The chapel's long history is intertwined with the military and religious life of Fort Monroe. Regular religious services at the fort began as early as 1825 under Reverend Mark Chevers, rector of St. John's Episcopal Church, who served as the official post chaplain from December 1, 1838, until his death in 1875 [13], [19]. Following the chapel's consecration, it became the primary venue for Protestant, non-denominational worship services conducted by U.S. Army post chaplains [19]. The chapel has seen several periods of restoration and maintenance over the years, including repairs after fire damage and pipe organ replacement in 1933, and an extensive restoration project guided by historical research from April 1968 to April 1969, which cost $200,000 to ensure authenticity to the original 1858 structure [19]. It also underwent exterior repainting to a white finish with green trim in 1946 [19]. The chapel's ecclesiastical jurisdiction is unique; while the physical building remains property of the United States government, its sacramental records—including baptisms, confirmations, marriages, and funerals—are maintained centrally by the Archdiocese for the Military Services (AMS) [18]. The AMS serves as the canonical authority for Catholic chaplains in the U.S. Armed Forces and oversees all Catholic liturgy and religious education programs within military chapels [18]. This administrative framework ensures the proper recording of sacraments performed within the chapel's walls, even though it operates on military land. As of September 2024, the chapel remains in active use for civilian religious services, continuing its long tradition of spiritual ministry [14].

## Theological Foundation: St. Cornelius the Centurion

The naming of both the Governors Island and Fort Monroe chapels after St. Cornelius the Centurion is not arbitrary but is rooted in a profound theological significance derived from his biblical story, which is recounted in the Book of Acts [2], [9]. St. Cornelius was a Roman centurion, a military officer commanding a cohort, stationed in Caesarea, the capital of the Roman province of Judea during the 1st century CE [1], [3], [23]. He is described in Acts 10 as a devout man who "feared God," gave alms generously to the Jewish people, and prayed constantly [1], [3], [24]. His piety placed him in a category known as a "proselyte of the gate"—a Gentile sympathetic to Judaism who worshipped the God of Israel but had not converted fully through circumcision or adherence to the Mosaic ceremonial law [4], [24]. This characterization is crucial, as it establishes him as a seeker of the true God outside the established covenant community of Israel, yet open to divine revelation [28]. His devotion was so consistent that it is noted he prayed at the ninth hour, around 3:00 PM, aligning his practice with the established times for prayer in the Jewish temple [3], [24], [28].

The pivotal event in Cornelius's life, and in the history of the early Christian Church, occurs when he receives a vision from an angel of God [1], [3]. The angel instructs him to send men to Joppa to find Simon Peter and bring him back to Cornelius's house [3], [23]. At the very same time, Peter, who is in Joppa, receives a parallel vision while preparing for lunch [3]. He sees a great sheet descending from heaven containing all kinds of animals, both clean and unclean according to Jewish dietary laws, and hears a voice command him to "kill and eat" [3], [24]. Peter objects, stating that he has never eaten anything considered "common or unclean" [3]. The voice responds, "What God has made clean, you must not call profane" [3], [23], [24]. This vision is repeated three times, signifying its divine import [24]. Later, the Holy Spirit tells Peter that three men are seeking him and instructs him to go with them without hesitation, as the visit is divinely sanctioned [3].

When Peter arrives at Cornelius's home, he preaches to Cornelius and his assembled household, which includes relatives and close friends [3], [11]. As Peter speaks, the Holy Spirit falls upon all who are listening, just as it did at Pentecost [23], [27]. They begin to speak in tongues and praise God [23]. Witnessing this miraculous outpouring of the Spirit, Peter recognizes it as proof of God's acceptance of the Gentiles and commands that they be baptized "in the name of Jesus Christ" [3], [27]. This baptism marks the first recorded conversion and baptism of uncircumcised Gentiles into the Christian Church, a watershed moment that inaugurated the mission to the Gentiles [3], [4], [26]. The event triggered significant debate among the Jewish Christians in Jerusalem, who questioned whether Gentiles could be admitted to the Church without requiring circumcision [2], [4]. Peter's defense of his actions, highlighting the direct descent of the Holy Spirit, eventually led to a formal resolution at the Council of Jerusalem, which decreed that Gentile converts were not required to follow Jewish ceremonial laws [2], [4].

Post-biblical traditions elaborate further on Cornelius's life. Eastern Orthodox hagiography, in texts like the 'Martyrium of Cornelius the Centurion', states that after his conversion, he retired from the army, accompanied St. Peter on missionary journeys, and was later sent alone to Skepsis in Mysia where he confronted local paganism [1], [12]. According to this tradition, he miraculously restored a prince’s wife and child from rubble after an earthquake destroyed the temple of Zeus, leading to mass conversions and his appointment as bishop of Skepsis by Peter [1], [12]. He is said to have died there as a martyr in old age, buried near the ruins of the pagan temple [1], [12]. These traditions, while not part of the canonical scripture, reinforce his status as a foundational figure in the expansion of Christianity beyond its Jewish origins. His feast day is observed on February 4 in the Episcopal Church calendar, and he is venerated as the patron saint of soldiers, benefactors, philanthropists, and Jewish converts [1], [22]. The theological principles embodied by his story—divine initiative in calling seekers, the abolition of ritual distinctions between Jew and Gentile, and the universality of salvation—are precisely why military chapels, symbols of service and duty, would choose to honor him. His life exemplifies radical hospitality, openness to divine guidance, and the breaking down of social and religious barriers, making him a powerful and fitting patron for institutions dedicated to the spiritual well-being of diverse communities [28], [30].

## Comparative Analysis of the Chapels

While both the Chapel of St. Cornelius the Centurion on Governors Island and the Chapel of the Centurion at Fort Monroe share a common name and theological inspiration, they are distinct entities with divergent histories, architectural identities, and roles in their respective communities. A comparative analysis reveals how two similar names can mask fundamentally different stories of development, purpose, and adaptation. The most immediate difference lies in their construction and material. The Governors Island chapel, in its current form, is a large, permanent masonry structure built of buff Indiana limestone between 1905 and 1906 [5], [7]. It was a replacement for a deteriorating 1847 wooden chapel, representing a significant investment in a more durable and imposing building [5], [16]. In stark contrast, the Chapel of the Centurion at Fort Monroe is a wood-frame Gothic Revival building, completed in 1858 and consecrated in 1858 [13], [17]. It was the original chapel from its inception and has remained in continuous use without replacement, making it a rare and invaluable artifact of 19th-century American ecclesiastical architecture on a military post [13], [19].

Their founding narratives and funding mechanisms also differ significantly. The Governors Island chapel originated from the organized efforts of the Episcopal Church and Trinity Parish, with initial funding coming from donors and the personal contributions of chaplain John McVickar [5], [6]. Its establishment was a response to the needs of a growing military population. The Fort Monroe chapel, however, has a more dramatic and personal origin story. Its construction was directly funded by private subscription led by Lieutenant Julian McAllister, who survived a fatal laboratory explosion at the fort and attributed his safety to divine intervention [13], [14], [19]. His role as the principal contributor provides a compelling human element to the chapel's history, linking its foundation to a moment of profound personal faith and gratitude [19]. This narrative of a single officer's vow gives the chapel a unique and intimate backstory compared to the more institutionalized founding of its New York counterpart.

Architecturally, the chapels reflect different tastes and eras. The Governors Island chapel, designed by Charles C. Haight, showcases a robust English Gothic style of the late 19th century, characterized by its massive limestone construction, a prominent three-story tower, and numerous stained glass windows [5], [7]. Haight's design incorporated subtle military motifs, likely reflecting his own background and the chapel's setting on a former army base [20]. The Fort Monroe chapel, designed by the celebrated architect Richard Upjohn, embodies the refined elegance of the mid-19th-century Gothic Revival movement [13]. Its simpler wood-frame construction belies its artistic importance, particularly due to the presence of three stained-glass windows attributed to Louis Comfort Tiffany, a master of the medium [13]. This highlights the different aesthetic priorities and available resources at the time of their construction. The Governors Island chapel is a monumental statement of permanence, while the Fort Monroe chapel is a testament to the enduring beauty of a carefully crafted wooden structure.

Perhaps the most significant difference lies in their modern histories and current statuses. The chapel on Governors Island has undergone a complete transformation from a military chapel to a public cultural venue. After being inactive for worship since the Coast Guard's departure in 1996, it has been repurposed as a site for contemporary art installations, such as Jacob Hashimoto’s 'The Eclipse' [6], [15]. This adaptive reuse aligns with the broader redevelopment of Governors Island into a public park and cultural destination, managed by the Governors Island Preservation and Education Corporation [7], [15]. In contrast, the chapel at Fort Monroe continues its original function as an active place of worship. As of September 2024, it remains in active use for civilian religious services, maintaining its role as a spiritual center for the community connected to the former military base, now a national monument [14]. This ongoing use preserves its original intent far more directly than its New York counterpart. Their paths of ownership also diverge; the Governors Island chapel was transferred to the city and then reclaimed by its original church owner, Trinity Church, which now manages it as a historic asset [6], [8]. The Fort Monroe chapel, meanwhile, remains on government property, with its sacramental records handled by the Archdiocese for the Military Services, illustrating a different model of stewardship for a chapel on active-duty land [18].

## Current Status and Preservation

The current state of the two chapels named for St. Cornelius the Centurion reflects their divergent paths from active military chapels to their present-day incarnations. The chapel on Governors Island in New York City has successfully navigated a transition from a functional place of worship to a preserved historic landmark and a dynamic public cultural venue. Following the U.S. Coast Guard's departure from the island in 1996, the chapel became inactive for regular religious services [6]. However, its historical significance ensured its preservation. As part of the Governors Island National Historic Landmark District and New York City Historic District, the chapel is protected and maintained as a key component of the island's heritage [7]. Ownership reverted to Trinity Church, which has since managed the property [6], [8]. Recognizing the potential of the space, the chapel has been repurposed for contemporary use. It is now publicly accessible and serves as a venue for art installations and cultural programming, a role that aligns with the island's transformation into a major public park and arts destination [15]. Notably, the chapel hosted Jacob Hashimoto’s 'The Eclipse', a suspended monochrome disk installation adapted from the 57th Venice Biennale, as part of the Governors Island Art Fair programming [15]. This adaptive reuse demonstrates a creative approach to preservation, ensuring the building remains a vibrant and relevant part of the community while honoring its past. The chapel's architectural integrity has been assessed by professionals like the Architectural Preservation Studio, which conducted a comprehensive conditions evaluation, including laser-scanning documentation to create precise drawings for future conservation efforts [10]. This commitment to preservation ensures that the chapel will continue to stand as a significant example of 19th-century ecclesiastical architecture for future generations.

In contrast, the Chapel of the Centurion at Fort Monroe has maintained its core function as a place of active worship, adapting to the changing landscape of Fort Monroe itself. Fort Monroe, once a sprawling U.S. Army base, was decommissioned and transformed into a combined National Monument and Presidential Site managed by the National Park Service and the Fort Monroe Authority [17], [19]. Despite this significant change in administration and purpose for the surrounding area, the chapel has continued its uninterrupted service as a place of worship. As of September 2024, it remains in active use for civilian religious services, fulfilling its original mission [14]. This continuity is remarkable, especially considering the chapel's age and its location within a complex undergoing major redevelopment. The chapel's status is further secured by its unique administrative arrangement with the Archdiocese for the Military Services (AMS). While the physical building is owned by the U.S. government, the AMS maintains canonical oversight and centralizes all sacramental records performed within it, ensuring that the chapel's religious functions are properly documented and sustained [18]. This partnership between a civilian governing body and a specialized ecclesiastical authority allows the chapel to operate effectively on military land. The chapel's physical condition has been maintained through periodic restoration projects, including a major effort in 1968-1969 that cost $200,000 and was meticulously researched to preserve its historical authenticity [19]. This ongoing commitment to preservation and active use underscores the chapel's enduring importance to the community and its successful integration into the new civilian identity of Fort Monroe. To summarize, while both chapels are historic treasures, the Governors Island chapel has embraced a new role as a cultural institution, whereas the Fort Monroe chapel has persevered as a living, breathing center of faith and community.
