# The Well (Doctor Who)

"The Well" is the third episode of the fifteenth series, marketed globally as Season 2, of the British science fiction television programme *Doctor Who* [[1]), [2]]. First broadcast on April 26, 2025, on BBC iPlayer, BBC One, and Disney+, the episode serves as a canonical sequel to the critically acclaimed 2008 episode "Midnight" [[1]), [3]), [4]]. Written by long-standing showrunner Russell T Davies in collaboration with Sharma Angel-Walfall, and directed by Amanda Brotchie, the episode features the Fifteenth Doctor, portrayed by Ncuti Gatwa, and his companion Belinda Chandra, played by Varada Sethu [[1]), [2], [4]]. Set approximately 400,000 to 500,000 years after the events of "Midnight" on the same planet—now designated Planet 6-7-6-7—the story reintroduces the titular Midnight Entity, credited as "It Has No Name," as a terrifying conceptual threat [2], [4], [12]. A central element of the episode is the introduction of Aliss Fenly, a deaf survivor portrayed by deaf actress Rose Ayling-Ellis, whose disability becomes a crucial plot device integral to the narrative's core horror mechanics [2], [4], [17]. The episode is widely recognized for its deliberate return to psychological horror, leveraging audience memory of "Midnight" to amplify its tension and exploring sophisticated themes of perception, vulnerability, and marginalization [2], [17]. The production team implemented significant inclusivity measures, including deaf awareness training for the cast and crew and the use of real-time subtitle displays in the diegesis, marking a notable advancement in accessibility representation on the series [[3]), [18]].

## Production and Broadcast History

The production of "The Well" represents a collaborative effort involving established and emerging talent within the *Doctor Who* creative ecosystem, reflecting a conscious strategy to blend nostalgic callbacks with fresh perspectives. The episode was co-written by Russell T Davies and Sharma Angel-Walfall, a partnership that emerged from Davies's discovery of Angel-Walfall's prior work, which led him to invite her to Bad Wolf Studios for multiple in-person story sessions and remote Zoom meetings to develop the script [[3]), [5]]. This collaborative process underscores Davies's approach to revitalizing the writing team by incorporating new voices while retaining his central authorial hand [5]. The direction was handled by Amanda Brotchie, who brought her vision to the project alongside the producer, Chris May, and the series producer, Vicki Delow [[1]), [3]), [6]]. The executive producers overseeing the production included Davies himself, alongside long-serving figures Julie Gardner, Jane Tranter, Joel Collins, and Phil Collinson, ensuring a high level of oversight and alignment with the series' overall creative direction [6]. The production companies involved were BBC Studios, Bad Wolf, and Disney+ [20].

The development history of "The Well" reveals a dynamic creative process that saw significant changes before the final product was realized. Initially, the episode was developed under the working title "The Thirteen" [[3])]. However, an early concept involving the Orisha, a group of deities from Yoruba mythology, was abandoned due to concerns about potentially disrespecting a real-world religion [[3])]. Following this pivot, Russell T Davies repositioned the story as a direct thematic and narrative follow-up to the 2008 episode "Midnight," a decision that would become the episode's defining characteristic [[3])]. This shift highlights a deliberate move towards leveraging the rich, established lore of the revived series to craft a story that resonated with both new and long-term viewers. To ensure authentic representation, the production team undertook several inclusive measures. All cast members participated in a deaf awareness course to foster a supportive and respectful environment, a crucial step given the central role of deafness in the plot [[3]), [18]]. Furthermore, actors playing troopers underwent a military boot camp to prepare for their roles, demonstrating a commitment to grounding the action elements of the story in realism [[3])].

The episode was filmed as part of Block Three of Series 15, alongside the subsequent episode "Lux," indicating a potential thematic or tonal grouping within the season's structure [15]. The global launch of *Doctor Who* Season Two began on April 12, 2025, with the premiere of "The Robot Revolution" [7], [8]. "The Well" followed as the third episode in the eight-episode season lineup [7], [8]. It was officially announced on March 22, 2025, generating anticipation among fans aware of its connection to "Midnight" [9]. The episode premiered on Saturday, April 26, 2025 [[1]), [3]), [4]]. In the United Kingdom, it aired on BBC One at 6:50 PM BST and was available immediately on BBC iPlayer at 8:00 AM BST [9]. For the global audience, the episode was made available on the streaming platform Disney+ at 3:00 AM ET / 12:00 AM PT, reflecting the international distribution model for the series during this era [9]. The broadcast on all three platforms ensured maximum accessibility for viewers worldwide [[1]), [4], [13]].

## Plot Summary

The narrative of "The Well" begins with the Fifteenth Doctor, Ncuti Gatwa, and his companion Belinda Chandra, Varada Sethu, arriving on a desolate, diamond-rich planet designated Planet 6-7-6-7 [2], [4]. The planet, once known as "Midnight," is now a devastated mining colony, its surface scarred by excavation [6], [10]. The Doctor and Belinda don their environmental suits and helmets to join a recovery mission composed of troopers from a future Space Corps [6], [9]. Their objective is to investigate a distress signal from a carbon-46 mine, a mineral composed of pure carbon [14]. Upon reaching the site, they discover a scene of utter devastation, but also a single survivor: a woman named Aliss Fenly, portrayed by Rose Ayling-Ellis [2], [18]. Aliss is a cook from the station and is profoundly deaf [18]. She communicates primarily through British Sign Language (BSL), a detail that is organically woven into the fabric of the story [18]. Her presence is a mystery, as she is found alive amidst the bodies of her colleagues, who were killed by an unknown entity [4].

As the recovery team explores the mine, the true nature of the threat is revealed. The entity, referred to in the credits as "It Has No Name," emerges from the deepest shaft of the mine, dubbed "The Well" [4], [18]. This non-corporeal being operates on a terrifying principle: it occupies the unobservable space directly behind an individual [10], [18]. When someone turns their back on another person, the entity can manifest physically, causing bone-breaking impacts or even infecting them if they are shot [18]. The Doctor quickly deduces that the entity is vulnerable to reflective surfaces; however, the only substance capable of dispersing it effectively is mercury [18]. The situation escalates when the troopers, driven by fear and panic, open fire on Aliss, believing her to be the source of the danger. In a desperate act to protect her, the serving officer Shaya Costallion, played by Caoilfhionn Dunne, pushes Aliss out of harm's way and takes the bullets meant for her [16]. As the troopers continue their futile assault, Shaya makes the ultimate sacrifice. Realizing that the entity is tied to the well, she deliberately runs into it, falling into the mercury-fed shaft to re-imprison the creature and save the others [4], [16]. The episode concludes not with a clean victory, but with a lingering sense of dread. As the surviving troopers, including Mo Gilliben (Bethany Antonia), begin to leave, Mo hears a faint whisper behind her. The camera pans to reveal a flash of something indistinguishable just over her shoulder, mirroring Belinda's earlier visions of the entity and suggesting that at least one member of the species may have survived the fall, leaving the threat unresolved [13], [16].

## Narrative Continuity with 'Midnight'

A defining feature of "The Well" is its explicit and multifaceted continuation of the narrative and thematic groundwork laid by the 2008 episode "Midnight." This is not merely a thematic echo or a superficial homage; it is a direct, canonical sequel confirmed by creators, dialogue, and visual cues throughout the episode. The connection between the two stories is so deliberate that it functions as a primary driver of suspense for viewers familiar with the original. The temporal link is established almost immediately, with the narrative placing "The Well" approximately 400,000 to 500,000 years after the events of "Midnight" [[1]), [2], [4], [12]]. This vast timespan allows for significant evolution of the central threat while maintaining a powerful sense of legacy and continuity. The physical setting is identical: both stories unfold on the same planet, which in "Midnight" was simply named "Midnight" [10], [19]. In "The Well," the planet is formally designated as Planet 6-7-6-7, a name derived from its coordinates, confirming it as the same world visited millennia later [2], [4], [14].

The most direct and unambiguous proof of this continuity lies in the episode's opening moments. The first line spoken by the Doctor upon arrival is a verbatim callback to Professor Hobbes's exposition in "Midnight": "This is Planet 6-7-6-7 and the atmosphere is charged with galvanic radiation" [14]. This line echoes Hobbes's original description from 2008: "This is Midnight... X-tonic rays, raw galvanic radiation" [14]. This verbal callback serves as an immediate confirmation for viewers, signaling that the writers are intentionally revisiting this specific piece of lore. The narrative continues to build these connections through other established concepts. The episode mentions carbon-46 mining, a nod to the diamonds found on the planet, which were a central feature of the 2008 story [14]. It also references Xtonic stars, further embedding the episode within the same fictional cosmology [4]. Even the sonic screwdriver is used in a manner reminiscent of its function in "Midnight," enhancing the camera feed to see what the Doctor sees, creating a subtle but powerful link for attentive viewers [4].

The creators have been vocal about their intention to treat the entity as a continuous, evolving threat. Director Amanda Brotchie explicitly confirmed that the Midnight Entity in "The Well" is "definitely the same creature" as in "Midnight" [21]. However, she noted that the creature has "morphed" and "is behaving slightly differently," having "grown, the monster has learned, the monster has been waiting for a chance to get out and cause trouble" [21]. This insight provides canonical clarification on the nature of the sequel. While the original entity from "Midnight" operated through psychic possession and mimicry of speech to create chaos among a group of passengers, the entity in "The Well" has adopted a different, more parasitic and spatially-focused modus operandi [10], [19]. This evolution is a deliberate narrative choice, shifting the horror from a social-psychological thriller about betrayal to a more primal, existential terror of being watched and attacked from an unseen angle [21]. This thematic divergence allows the episode to stand on its own while simultaneously enriching the lore of the original story, showing that the entity is not a static monster but a force that adapts and persists across deep time. The very existence of the episode, born from the ashes of a failed initial concept, was a direct response to the enduring power and popularity of "Midnight" in the fan community, positioning "The Well" as a love letter to the show's best-known horror story [[2], [3])].

## The Midnight Entity and Its Evolution

The central antagonist of "The Well" is the Midnight Entity, a conceptual and non-corporeal being credited in the episode's closing titles as "It Has No Name" [2], [4]. Portrayed by creature performer Paul Kasey, the entity's appearance is intentionally kept ambiguous, designed to preserve its enigmatic and terrifying quality rather than revealing its true form or biology [[1]), [4]]. This approach aligns with the broader narrative philosophy of the episode, which deliberately refuses to explain the origins, motivations, or fundamental nature of the creature, focusing instead on the psychological impact of its presence [12], [16]. The entity is depicted as a surviving remnant of the alien force encountered in "Midnight," but its behavior has demonstrably evolved over the 400,000 to 500,000-year gap between the two stories [10]. This evolution is a cornerstone of the episode's plot and distinguishes it from its predecessor.

In "Midnight" (2008), the entity's primary method of attack was infiltration and manipulation. It possessed the passenger Sky Silvestry by perfectly mimicking her voice, using rapid linguistic assimilation to turn the isolated bus full of people against each other [10], [19]. Its goal appeared to be sowing discord and exploiting human weakness, culminating in a narrative collapse where trust evaporated and the Doctor was powerless to intervene [19]. The threat was insidious, psychological, and socially focused. By contrast, the entity in "The Well" operates on a more direct, physical, and spatial plane. It is no longer interested in possession but in parasitic occupation [10]. Its modus operandi is to occupy the unobservable space directly behind an individual [10], [18]. This creates a pervasive sense of dread, as the danger is always present, just out of sight, forcing characters—and by extension, the audience—to constantly check their surroundings and question their perceptions. It has been described by the Fifteenth Doctor as having "played games" and "messes with you," confirming its evolved, more cunning nature [21].

The vulnerability of the entity is also significantly different. In "Midnight," the threat was resolved through a combination of brute force and a clever trick involving a microphone, ultimately leading to the death of the possessed host. In "The Well," the solution is more scientifically grounded and visually spectacular. The entity is shown to be susceptible to reflective surfaces, and its containment requires a specific chemical agent: mercury [18]. The climax of the episode hinges on this knowledge, as Commander Shaya Costallion sacrifices herself by jumping into the well, which is filled with mercury, thereby re-imprisoning the entity [4], [16]. This change in vulnerability reflects the shift in the threat's nature—from a social parasite to a physical one that must be contained chemically. Despite this evolution, the core identity of the entity remains intact. It is still a timeless, ancient being that feeds on isolation and fear, and its actions in "The Well" are a direct continuation of its existence from "Midnight." The creators have emphasized that the entity is a persistent force that has adapted to survive, making its return both logical within the *Doctor Who* universe and deeply unsettling for the characters and audience alike [21]. The episode leaves this adaptation incomplete, with the final scene implying that at least one fragment of the entity may have escaped, ensuring its legend will persist [13], [16].

## Character Analysis and Thematic Exploration

The character of Aliss Fenly, portrayed by deaf actress Rose Ayling-Ellis, is arguably the most significant and innovative creation of "The Well," representing a profound integration of disability into the core of the narrative's horror mechanics [2], [18]. Aliss is the sole survivor of a massacre at a diamond mining colony on Planet 6-7-6-7, and her deafness is not merely a background detail but the primary reason for her survival [4], [17]. The Midnight Entity's power relies on whispering into the ears of its victims, driving them to madness and violence [4]. Because Aliss cannot hear, she is immune to this auditory assault, allowing her to remain lucid while everyone else around her succumbs to the entity's influence [4]. This functional integration of her disability is a masterstroke of storytelling, transforming what could be a simple character trait into the linchpin of the entire plot. The performance of Ayling-Ellis is consistently praised for its critical acclaim, portraying a central character *with* a disability rather than being defined *by* it, a nuanced portrayal achieved through extensive preparation and authenticity [18].

The episode's handling of British Sign Language (BSL) extends beyond mere representation, becoming a tool for both world-building and thematic resonance. The production incorporated numerous authentic accessibility elements, including the use of suit-mounted real-time subtitle displays for the troopers, which translates their speech into text visible within their helmet visors [18], [22]. This technology is not just a production gimmick; it is presented within the diegesis as a standard piece of equipment, seamlessly integrating Aliss's mode of communication into the futuristic setting [18]. Furthermore, the production team included on-set BSL consultants and mandated deaf awareness training for the entire cast and crew, fostering an inclusive environment that informed the performances and ensured respectful portrayal [18], [22]. The narrative itself draws a powerful metaphorical link between the physical mechanics of the entity and the lived experience of marginalized communities. The entity's ability to hide in the space behind someone—a place of assumed safety—is thematically paralleled with the feeling of being ignored, forgotten, or turned away by society [17]. The episode's core message—"don't turn your back on me"—is literalized in the plot and given profound subtextual weight, critiquing systemic erasure and advocating for vigilance and compassion [17].

Several sources interpret this theme as a subtle political statement, particularly in relation to contemporary issues surrounding representation on streaming platforms. One analysis suggests that the episode's focus on finding and protecting a vulnerable, marginalized individual serves as a critique of Disney+'s documented removal of transgender characters from shows like 'Win or Lose' and 'Moon Girl and Devil Dinosaur', framing the Doctor's protective instinct as a direct counter-narrative to such censorship [17]. While this interpretation adds a layer of sociopolitical commentary, the episode's primary success lies in its ability to weave these complex themes into a compelling and frightening story without becoming preachy [17]. The character of the Fifteenth Doctor himself also plays a crucial role. Ncuti Gatwa describes the entity as "the scariest monster that I’ve faced so far," highlighting the effectiveness of Russell T Davies's writing, which relies on implication and personal interpretation to evoke terror [21]. The Doctor's role is less about a decisive battle and more about intellectual deduction and moral support, underscoring his role as a protector of the innocent. His willingness to learn BSL specifically for the role demonstrates a commitment to authenticity that permeates the episode's production ethos [18].

## In-Universe Implications and Series Context

While "The Well" presents a contained self-contained story, its events have significant and far-reaching consequences for the overarching narrative of *Doctor Who* Series 15, advancing multiple plot threads and expanding the series' intricate web of temporal mysteries. One of the most devastating revelations is the confirmation that Earth has been erased from history. The episode establishes that this erasure occurred on the precise date that Belinda Chandra left the planet, meaning that anyone from the 21st century who travels away from Earth after that point exists in a reality where their home planet never was [4], [22]. This creates immense stakes for the Doctor and his companions, transforming their journey through time and space into a search for a lost home and rewriting the fundamental rules of their existence. This development ties directly into the larger arc concerning Mrs. Flood's temporal enigma, which has been a recurring mystery throughout the season [[3])].

The episode also solidifies the importance of the character Mrs. Flood, played by Anita Dobson. Previously seen as a mysterious figure appearing at pivotal moments, Dobson's character is revealed to be a high-ranking superior officer in the Space Corps, hundreds of thousands of years in the future [18], [22]. Her reappearance via video link is not a random occurrence but a deliberate intervention, establishing her as a key player in the wider series arc and providing a crucial piece of intelligence to the Doctor [22]. This elevates her from a quirky enigma to a significant authority figure with a vested interest in the unfolding temporal crisis. Her presence in the distant future also helps to anchor the series' increasingly complex timeline, providing a stable reference point in a universe where history itself is unstable. These narrative developments demonstrate the episode's dual function: it delivers a satisfying standalone horror tale while simultaneously acting as a vital chapter in the serialized saga of the Fifteenth Doctor's tenure.

In summary, "The Well" stands as a landmark episode in the modern *Doctor Who* canon. Its most significant achievement is the successful and respectful return to the psychological horror genre, a tone for which the show is beloved and feared [2]. By directly confronting the legacy of "Midnight," the episode not only honors a fan-favorite classic but also proves that the series can revisit past successes with fresh ideas and greater depth. The integration of deafness and BSL is groundbreaking, moving beyond tokenism to create a narrative where disability is a functional, empowering, and thematically resonant element [17], [18]. Through the character of Aliss Fenly, the story explores profound questions about perception, empathy, and societal responsibility, all while delivering genuine chills and a memorable, tragic hero in Shaya Costallion. Ultimately, "The Well" is a testament to the enduring power of *Doctor Who*'s storytelling, proving that a story can be both a loving tribute to its own history and a bold step forward into new territory.
