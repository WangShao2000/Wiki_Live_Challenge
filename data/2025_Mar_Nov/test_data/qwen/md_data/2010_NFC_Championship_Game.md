# 2010 NFC Championship Game

The 2010 NFC Championship Game was a professional American football contest played between the Minnesota Vikings and the New Orleans Saints at the Louisiana Superdome in New Orleans on Sunday, January 24, 2010 [1], [12], [13]. This game, which concluded the National Football Conference's playoff tournament for the 2009 season, was a highly anticipated clash between two formidable teams with distinct historical narratives [22]. The Minnesota Vikings, representing a franchise seeking its first trip to the Super Bowl since the 1970s, faced off against the New Orleans Saints, a team striving for its inaugural conference title less than five years after the devastation of Hurricane Katrina [22]. The game pitted veteran quarterback Brett Favre, playing what would be his final playoff appearance at age 40, against the young and dynamic offense of the Saints led by quarterback Drew Brees [22]. The contest was a high-scoring affair that saw the statistically dominant Vikings squander numerous opportunities due to critical self-inflicted errors, ultimately leading to their defeat [23]. The decisive moment occurred late in regulation when Saints cornerback Tracy Porter intercepted a pass thrown by Favre intended for Sidney Rice, forcing the game into overtime [9], [14], [24]. In the extra session, Garrett Hartley successfully kicked a 40-yard field goal four minutes and 45 seconds into overtime to secure a 31–28 victory for the Saints [1], [12], [15]. This win sent the New Orleans Saints to Super Bowl XLIV, where they would go on to capture their first-ever NFL championship by defeating the Indianapolis Colts [13], [19]. The game was notable not only for its dramatic conclusion but also for its record-breaking television viewership and the lasting controversy surrounding officiating decisions, particularly regarding hits on Favre and a pass interference call in overtime [5], [11], [13]. The intense nature of the game prompted an immediate rule change to the NFL's overtime format for the subsequent season [11].

## Game Summary and Final Score

The 2010 NFC Championship Game was a tightly contested, high-stakes matchup that culminated in a dramatic overtime victory for the New Orleans Saints over the Minnesota Vikings [1], [10]. The final score of 31–28 in favor of the Saints marked a historic milestone for the franchise, securing their first-ever berth in the Super Bowl [19], [29]. The game took place on Sunday, January 24, 2010, at the Louisiana Superdome in New Orleans, a venue that would see the largest crowd in its history for a Saints game on that day [12], [19]. The decisive play came at the 4:45 mark of overtime when Saints kicker Garrett Hartley booted a 40-yard field goal through the uprights, capping a drive that began after the Saints won the coin toss [12], [15], [24]. This kick ended the game and fulfilled the Saints' long-sought ambition of reaching the league's ultimate stage [22]. The Vikings, despite being the statistically superior team in terms of yardage and time of possession, were undone by a series of costly turnovers that proved fatal in the closing moments of the contest [17], [23]. The game was characterized by a back-and-forth offensive battle, with both teams trading blows throughout the four quarters before the drama unfolded in overtime [30].

A detailed timeline of the scoring provides a clear picture of the game's ebb and flow. The Minnesota Vikings struck first, driving 80 yards on their opening possession to take a 7–0 lead. This drive was capped by a 19-yard touchdown run from running back Adrian Peterson, who demonstrated his explosive power early in the contest [1], [12]. The New Orleans Saints responded immediately, matching the Vikings' drive with one of their own, covering 76 yards in seven plays. Their touchdown came via a 38-yard touchdown pass from Drew Brees to wide receiver Pierre Thomas, evening the score at 7–7 [1], [12]. The Vikings regained the lead later in the first quarter with another sustained drive, this time an 80-yard march that ended with a 5-yard touchdown pass from Brett Favre to tight end Sidney Rice, putting them up 14–7 [1], [12]. The Saints once again matched the Vikings' touchdown, tying the game at 14–14 before halftime with a 64-yard drive finished by a 9-yard touchdown pass from Brees to Devery Henderson [1], [12].

The second half continued the pattern of alternating scores. In the third quarter, the Saints took their first lead of the game. A 61-yard kickoff return by Courtney Roby provided excellent field position, sparking a 37-yard drive that concluded with a 9-yard touchdown run by Pierre Thomas, giving New Orleans a 21–14 advantage [12]. However, the Vikings quickly responded, driving 80 yards again to tie the game at 21–21, with Adrian Peterson powering in from a yard out for his second touchdown of the afternoon [1], [12]. The fourth quarter became even more chaotic. The Saints extended their lead to 28–21 on a 5-yard touchdown pass from Brees to Reggie Bush, a score that followed a crucial defensive play by Will Smith, who forced a fumble from Percy Harvin deep in Viking territory [12], [28]. The Vikings then tied the game at 28–28 with just under five minutes remaining when Adrian Peterson scored his third touchdown of the game on a 2-yard run [1], [22]. With the game seemingly heading to a decisive conclusion, the Saints' defense made its most critical play. On the final drive of regulation, with just under 20 seconds left, Tracy Porter intercepted a desperation pass from Favre intended for Sidney Rice, returning it to the Saints' 48-yard line and forcing the game into overtime [9], [14], [16]. The overtime period was short-lived, as the Saints drove down the field and Hartley's game-winning field goal sealed the victory [1], [12]. The final score breakdown by quarter was as follows: Minnesota Vikings 14–0–7–7–0 (28), New Orleans Saints 7–7–7–7–3 (31) [1], [10].

Pierre Thomas was arguably the Saints' MVP of the game, recording two touchdowns—one receiving and one rushing—and becoming only the second Saint in franchise history to achieve both in a single postseason game [17]. The Saints' defense, often criticized for its lack of pressure during the regular season, rose to the occasion. The Saints' kicker, Garrett Hartley, had been suspended at the start of the season for using a banned stimulant, but he redeemed himself spectacularly with the game-winning 40-yard field goal in overtime, securing his place in Saints lore [29].

## Game Narrative and Key Moments

The narrative of the 2010 NFC Championship Game is one of profound contrasts: the Vikings' statistical dominance versus their catastrophic self-destruction, the Saints' offensive ineptitude punctuated by moments of brilliance, and the physical toll on veteran quarterback Brett Favre that culminated in a defining mistake. From the outset, the Vikings established control, marching 80 yards on their opening drive, with Adrian Peterson breaking tackles to score from 19 yards out, setting the tone for a physically demanding contest [1], [12]. The Saints, however, responded with equal fury, matching the drive with a 76-yard march ending in a 38-yard touchdown pass to Pierre Thomas, signaling that this would not be a one-sided affair [12]. The game remained a seesaw battle, with both offenses trading blows. The Vikings added another touchdown on their next drive, a 5-yard strike from Favre to Sidney Rice, building a 14–7 lead [12]. The Saints answered once more before halftime, with Devery Henderson catching a 9-yard pass from Brees to make it 14–14 at the break [12]. This initial period showcased the strengths of both teams, but also hinted at the volatility to come.

The second half intensified the physicality. The Vikings' dominance in yardage and time of possession became more apparent, but it was their inability to hold onto the ball that began to define their fate. The turning point of the game, and perhaps of the season for both franchises, arrived late in the third quarter. As the Vikings drove towards the end zone, they were stopped cold. The Saints' defense, sensing an opportunity, delivered a ferocious hit on Favre, forcing a fumble that was recovered by the Saints at their own 5-yard line [12]. This turnover completely shifted the momentum. New Orleans capitalized almost immediately, with Will Smith forcing a fumble from Percy Harvin on the ensuing drive, which the Saints recovered deep in Viking territory [12], [28]. The Saints converted this gift into a crucial touchdown, a 5-yard pass from Brees to Reggie Bush that gave them a 28–21 lead and effectively put the Vikings on the brink [12], [28]. This sequence exemplified the game's central theme: the Vikings' statistical dominance was meaningless against the Saints' opportunistic defense.

The fourth quarter descended into chaos, a microcosm of the Vikings' entire performance. Adrian Peterson scored his third touchdown of the game, a 2-yard plunge that brought the Vikings within one point, 28–27 [1], [22]. The Saints managed to extend their lead again, but with the Vikings poised to answer, the defining moment of the game occurred. With just under five minutes remaining, Favre, flushed from the pocket and hampered by a severely sprained ankle he had suffered earlier in the quarter, attempted a desperate cross-body throw to Sidney Rice [24], [32]. The pass was poorly thrown and intercepted cleanly by Tracy Porter at the New Orleans 22-yard line [14], [24]. This interception, the fifth turnover for the Vikings, halted their final drive and sent the game into overtime [15], [22]. The play was described by announcer Paul Allen as emotionally devastating, and Favre himself admitted afterward that his injured ankle had compromised his ability to make an accurate throw [9], [33]. The Saints' defense had done its job, but the Vikings' coaching staff compounded the error. Facing a potential game-winning field goal try from 50 yards away, they opted for a risky rollout designed for Favre, who declined to run when given open space, leading directly to the interception [32]. This sequence of events solidified the perception among many observers that the Vikings "choked" in the biggest moments of the game [23], [32].

Overtime was a brief but tense affair. The Saints received the ball first and drove methodically down the field, aided by a 40-yard return from Pierre Thomas on the opening kickoff [12]. They reached the Minnesota 22-yard line, where Garrett Hartley lined up for the game-winning 40-yard field goal. He connected, and the New Orleans Saints were Super Bowl champions [1], [15]. The aftermath revealed the immense physical toll the game had taken on Favre. He was visibly battered, with a swollen wrist, painful ribs, and a nearly broken ankle, having stated postgame that he "couldn’t get anything on the ball" on his final throw [14], [33]. The game was also marred by officiating controversies. Multiple sources reported that Favre absorbed numerous hits that went uncalled, with Saints defensive ends like Will Smith admitting the strategy was to "get after Favre" knowing his body was compromised [13], [28]. Furthermore, a controversial pass interference penalty was called on Vikings linebacker Ben Leber on the opening drive of overtime, moving the Saints into field-goal range and contributing directly to Hartley's winning kick [11]. These contentious calls added another layer of bitterness to the Vikings' loss, reinforcing the feeling among their fans that the game was unfairly decided [11], [13]. The final narrative is one of a team that dominated every stat sheet but failed in the fundamentals of the game, while a resilient opponent seized every opportunity presented by their opponents' mistakes.

## Broadcast and Viewership

The 2010 NFC Championship Game was not only a landmark event on the football field but also a monumental success for television broadcasting, attracting an enormous audience and setting several records for Fox Sports. The game was televised nationally by the Fox network, with its flagship NFL broadcast team of Joe Buck handling play-by-play duties and Troy Aikman providing color commentary [2], [4]. This duo had been working together for nine seasons and were already preparing for their third Super Bowl call at the end of the 2010 season, underscoring their status as Fox's top NFL broadcast team [4]. Joining them on the sidelines were Pam Oliver and Chris Myers, while the studio show was hosted by Curt Menefee and featured analysis from former players Terry Bradshaw, Howie Long, Michael Strahan, and Jimmy Johnson [2]. The broadcast itself was a technical achievement, delivering the action to millions of viewers across the United States. The telecast achieved a 30.6/45 household rating/share, with an average audience of 57.9 million viewers [7], [8]. This figure made it the most-watched non-Super Bowl NFL playoff game in history at the time, surpassing even the legendary 1982 NFC Championship Game between Dallas and San Francisco, which drew 68.7 million viewers [5], [7]. It also ranked as the eighth most-watched program in television history since the 1998 finale of the sitcom 'Seinfeld', which attracted 76.3 million viewers [7].

The game's popularity was further reflected in its Nielsen ratings. The broadcast delivered a 19.8 Total Persons Rating (Persons 2+) for Fox, making it the fourth highest-rated single telecast of 2010 in the U.S. according to Nielsen's year-end report [6]. This performance significantly outpaced the AFC Championship Game that same day, which was broadcast on CBS and garnered a 16.1 rating [6]. The viewership peaked dramatically during the overtime period, when the tension was at its highest. Between 9:30 p.m. and 10:00 p.m. ET, the broadcast drew a peak audience of 65.2 million viewers and a 34.4/48 rating/share, capturing the nation's attention as Garrett Hartley prepared to kick the game-winning field goal [7], [8]. The telecast was followed by a post-game show that also performed exceptionally well, drawing 23.83 million viewers [5]. Collectively, the four NFC playoff games broadcast by Fox during the 2010 season averaged an astounding 40.2 million viewers, marking the network's best-ever showing for NFC playoffs and the best since the people meter era began in 1987–88 [7].

Beyond the national ratings, the game generated record-setting local viewership in both of its markets, highlighting its profound regional importance. In New Orleans, the broadcast delivered a local rating of 63.2/82, the highest ever recorded for any postseason NFL game in the city's history [7], [8]. The rating peaked at an incredible 67.4/86 at 10:15 p.m. ET, precisely as Garrett Hartley lined up for the winning kick, reflecting the culmination of a historic season for the franchise and its community [7], [8]. The local market in Minneapolis also showed immense interest, posting a 58.7/80 local rating. This figure was the third-highest ever recorded for an NFC playoff game on the Fox network, indicating the deep disappointment felt by Vikings fans after such a close and controversial loss [7], [8]. The combination of massive national and local viewership underscored the game's status as a cultural event, transcending its role as a simple sports contest and becoming a shared experience for millions of Americans. The overwhelming television success ensured that the dramatic finish and the controversial moments would be seen and debated by a vast audience, cementing the game's legacy in the public consciousness.

## Attendance and Venue Record

The atmosphere at the Louisiana Superdome for the 2010 NFC Championship Game was electric, culminating in a record-breaking attendance figure that surpassed all previous marks for a New Orleans Saints home game. The official paid attendance for the contest was 71,276 fans, a number that represented the largest crowd ever to watch a Saints game at the Superdome [15], [19], [29]. This figure shattered the previous Superdome record of 70,940, which had stood since September 2, 1979, and also eclipsed the previous Saints postseason record of 70,149, which was set just eight days earlier on January 16, 2010, during a divisional-round victory over Arizona [19]. The capacity crowd packed the stadium, creating a raucous and intimidating environment for the visiting Minnesota Vikings. The roar of the crowd was a constant presence throughout the game, amplifying the stakes and contributing to the tense, physical nature of the contest. The sheer volume of fans demonstrated the magnitude of the occasion for the city of New Orleans and the Saints organization, which had fought for years to establish itself as a legitimate contender in the NFL.

The significance of this attendance figure extends beyond a simple record. For the New Orleans Saints, the large turnout was a powerful symbol of the city's resilience and its unwavering support for its beloved football team. The franchise had endured a tumultuous period, including the aftermath of Hurricane Katrina, which had forced them to play their home games elsewhere for two seasons [22]. The journey to the NFC Championship Game was seen as a triumphant return to normalcy and a validation of the hard work of the players, coaches, and front office. The capacity crowd in the Superdome was a testament to the community's belief in the team and its desire to witness a historic moment firsthand. The deafening noise level undoubtedly impacted the game's dynamics, particularly for the Vikings, whose communication on the field may have been hindered by the hostile environment. The Saints' defense, accustomed to playing in loud venues, seemed to thrive amidst the chaos, while the Vikings struggled to find rhythm at times.

For the Minnesota Vikings, the massive crowd represented a daunting challenge. Playing in a hostile environment is a known difficulty in the NFL, and the intensity of the Superdome on a night like this was amplified exponentially. The pressure of facing such a passionate fan base, combined with the high stakes of a conference championship, likely contributed to the Vikings' uncharacteristic mistakes, including their five turnovers [29]. The physical toll of the game was exacerbated by the energy of the crowd, as defenders channeled the enthusiasm of the fans into their hits, notably on Brett Favre. The record attendance also speaks to the broader appeal of the game itself. The matchup between two storied franchises—a Vikings team seeking its first Super Bowl appearance in decades and a Saints team on the verge of achieving their first—was compelling enough to draw the largest crowd in the venue's history for a Saints game [22]. The event was a celebration of the sport and a showcase of the passion that defines professional football in America. The 71,276 fans who filled the Superdome were not just spectators; they were active participants in a historic night, and their presence remains a defining feature of the 2010 NFC Championship Game's legacy.

## Legacy and Aftermath

The legacy of the 2010 NFC Championship Game extends far beyond the final scoreboard, leaving a lasting imprint on the NFL landscape through its impact on league policy, the careers of its key participants, and the collective memory of its fans. The most concrete and immediate consequence of the game was a significant change to the NFL's overtime rules. The format used in this game, where the game could end if the team receiving the kickoff scored a field goal, was widely criticized for being unfair to the team that receives the ball second [11]. Following this game, the NFL implemented a new rule for the 2010–11 season that stipulates the game will continue until one team wins unless the team receiving the kickoff first scores a touchdown on their initial possession [11]. This change was explicitly adopted "in the ensuing offseason" following the controversial finish, demonstrating the tangible influence that a single game can have on the evolution of the sport's regulations [11].

For the New Orleans Saints, the victory was the crowning achievement of a remarkable season and the fulfillment of a franchise-defining promise. It secured their first-ever trip to the Super Bowl, a feat that had eluded them for 43 years [19]. The triumph was particularly poignant for a city still recovering from the trauma of Hurricane Katrina just a few seasons prior, transforming the team into a symbol of hope and resilience [22]. The momentum carried forward seamlessly into Super Bowl XLIV, where the Saints defeated the Indianapolis Colts 31–17 to capture their first Lombardi Trophy [13], [19]. The NFC Championship Game interception by Tracy Porter was viewed as a precursor to his even more famous pick-six of Peyton Manning in the Super Bowl, with the media framing Porter as the hero who set the stage for the franchise's ultimate glory [18], [25]. The Times-Picayune newspaper in New Orleans published a celebratory front page on February 8, 2010, bearing the iconic headline "Amen!", which was widely recognized as one of the most important front pages in New Orleans sports history, validating the significance of the entire playoff run rooted in the NFC Championship victory [26].

For the Minnesota Vikings, the aftermath was one of profound disappointment and lingering questions. The loss marked their first NFC Championship Game appearance since 1977, and falling short in such a dramatic fashion was a bitter pill to swallow for a franchise with a long history of playoff heartbreak [22]. The game became a source of debate and frustration for fans and analysts alike, who pointed to a litany of self-inflicted wounds as the primary cause of the defeat. Adrian Peterson's fumbles, particularly in critical situations, were frequently cited as having cost the team countless points [9], [13]. The controversial officiating, especially the unpenalized hits on Brett Favre and the pass interference call in overtime, fueled perceptions of bias and added another layer of injustice to the loss [11], [13]. Favre's final playoff game was a tragic end to a Hall of Fame career, remembered more for its physical toll and its final, flawed moments than for the incredible feats that preceded it [22]. The game solidified a painful chapter in the Vikings' history, serving as a reminder of how close they came to finally breaking through to the Super Bowl and how narrowly they missed their chance. In conclusion, the 2010 NFC Championship Game stands as a classic NFL thriller, a contest defined by its contradictions, its drama, and its lasting consequences. It elevated the Saints to superstardom, inflicted a painful lesson upon the Vikings, and prompted a fundamental change to the rules of the game itself, ensuring that its legacy will be remembered and analyzed for years to come.
