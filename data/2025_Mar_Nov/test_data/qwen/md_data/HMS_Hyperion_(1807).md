# HMS Hyperion (1807)

HMS Hyperion was a frigate of the British Royal Navy, representing the quintessential warship of the late 18th and early 19th centuries [11], [21]. Launched in 1807, she was a fifth-rate vessel, a classification denoting a medium-sized warship typically carrying between 20 and 50 guns [11]. The ship served as a workhorse of the fleet throughout the tumultuous period of the Napoleonic Wars and beyond, participating in numerous actions against French and allied vessels. Her career spanned over two decades, demonstrating both her durability and the Royal Navy's reliance on such well-designed warships. Despite extensive archival records confirming her active service far into the 1820s, a significant point of uncertainty remains regarding her ultimate fate, with some historical accounts suggesting an early demise that is contradicted by more contemporary and detailed personnel records. This report provides a comprehensive analysis of HMS Hyperion (1807), drawing upon authoritative secondary histories and primary archival sources to reconstruct her design, construction, service history, command structure, and final disposition.

## Design and Construction

The genesis of HMS Hyperion lies in the ongoing evolution of Royal Navy frigate design, a process heavily influenced by the need to counter the formidable French frigates of the Revolutionary and Napoleonic eras [21]. She belonged to a class of 32-gun fifth-rate frigates designed by John Henslow, who served as Surveyor of the Navy from 1787 to 1805 [9], [21]. Henslow’s designs were renowned for their balance of speed, firepower, and seaworthiness, often incorporating elements learned from captured enemy vessels. Specifically, the design of HMS Hyperion was based on the lines of the French frigate *Magicienne*, a 36-gun vessel of 1778, which provided a proven and effective hull form that British designers adapted for their own needs [21]. This lineage placed her within a cohort of highly capable ships, including contemporaries like HMS Bucephalus (launched 1808) and HMS Pyramus (launched 1810), all of which were products of the same design philosophy aimed at achieving superiority in single-ship actions [11], [21].

Details concerning her physical construction present a degree of ambiguity, reflecting the fragmented nature of some historical records. One source attributes her construction to Edward Hunt at Chatham Dockyard [25]. However, another, more specific piece of evidence points to a different origin. A signed original ship plan for HMS Hyperion, created by Edward Churchill, Master Shipwright at Plymouth Dockyard, exists in the collection of the National Maritime Museum [20]. This document, held under reference J0416, provides a detailed front and side elevation of the vessel, offering a visual testament to her construction and design at that specific dockyard [20]. The discrepancy between these two claims highlights the importance of consulting multiple primary sources; while one account may attribute construction to a particular yard, official plans might bear the signature of the master shipwright responsible for overseeing the build. The lack of her name appearing in the index to the Navy Board Progress Books, which list building and repair costs, suggests that dockyard records for this period may be incomplete or imprecise, further complicating a definitive reconstruction of her construction timeline [14].

Her dimensions were substantial for a fifth-rate frigate. The ship measured 150 feet along the gun deck, a standard length for a 32-gun vessel of her class, providing ample space for her crew and armament [25]. While her official classification was as a 32-gun frigate, her actual armament appears to have been configured differently. According to one detailed source, her battery consisted of 28 x 18-pounder long guns mounted on the main deck, supplemented by 2 x 9-pounder guns on the quarterdeck [25]. This configuration was augmented by 10 x 32-pounder carronades, powerful short-range weapons ideal for close-quarters combat, which were typical additions to British frigates of the era [10], [25]. Rif Winfield, in his definitive work on the subject, corroborates the use of '32pdr carronades' on Hyperion, reinforcing the mix of long-range broadsides and devastating close-range firepower that characterized her design [10]. The complement of the ship was recorded as 640 men, a figure that seems exceptionally high for a 32-gun frigate, which typically carried crews in the range of 250-400 men. Such a large number could suggest an unusually heavy complement for wartime operations or may reflect an administrative total rather than a typical sailing complement. The only surviving official muster rolls would provide the definitive answer to this question [15], [17]. Her launch date is firmly established as 1807, placing her squarely within the peak of Britain's naval expansion during the Napoleonic Wars [11], [21].

## Service History

The service history of HMS Hyperion is one of remarkable longevity and continuous activity, extending far beyond the conclusion of the Napoleonic Wars. Following her launch in 1807, she was swiftly integrated into the Royal Navy's fleet, serving on various stations where she engaged in the critical mission of countering French maritime power [10]. Contemporary records, such as the List of H.M. Navy corrected to 1809, confirm her active status within the navy by that year, solidifying her presence as a working warship in the immediate post-launch period [1]. Her operational life was not confined to a single theater but encompassed diverse duties across the globe. Historical accounts indicate that she served on the Mediterranean Station, a key area of conflict during the Napoleonic Wars where the Royal Navy sought to blockade French ports and protect British interests [10]. This deployment underscores her versatility and the strategic importance assigned to her by the Admiralty.

One of the most significant documented actions involving HMS Hyperion was the capture of the privateer Rattlesnake [7], [32]. Privateers were privately owned vessels authorized by their governments to attack enemy shipping, and they posed a significant threat to British merchant convoys and naval supply lines. The capture of such a vessel demonstrates Hyperion's role in protecting commerce and asserting British naval supremacy. The chronology of this event is particularly important; a seaman's service record confirms he was serving aboard the ship from March 1811 until June 1812 [35]. This directly corroborates the timing of the action, placing it firmly within her active service period and refuting any notion of a premature end to her career. This engagement aligns perfectly with the broader operational context of the era, where frigates like Hyperion were frequently tasked with hunting down and destroying these elusive raiders in the English Channel, the Bay of Biscay, and the Atlantic approaches [22].

The narrative of her service is marked by a striking contradiction regarding a famous exploit attributed to her. An account claims that in July 1800, Hyperion led a squadron of small craft to attack four blockaded French frigates off Boulogne, successfully capturing one [25]. However, this claim is historically impossible given that the ship in question, launched in 1807, simply did not exist in 1800. This event must be considered a misattribution, likely conflating the identity of this vessel with another ship named *Hyperion* that may have served earlier or with a similarly named ship altogether. This highlights the necessity of rigorous cross-referencing of all historical accounts against a strict chronological framework derived from primary sources. Despite this error, the body of verifiable evidence paints a picture of a durable and active warship. Her career extended well past the Battle of Trafalgar and the initial conclusion of hostilities with France. Multiple entries in Royal Navy pay books prove she remained in commission and actively manned for many years after the end of the Napoleonic Wars [33], [36]. Records show an Able Seaman serving aboard her pay book from September 1829 to December 1829, and another from March 1829 to April 1829, confirming her continued service at least until late 1829 [33], [36]. Another pay book entry shows a crew member serving from June 1828 to February 1831, though this may refer to a different vessel also named HMS Hyperion, a separate ship launched in 1825 [34]. Regardless, the evidence overwhelmingly supports the conclusion that the 1807 Hyperion had a long and distinguished career, serving as a vital asset to the Royal Navy for over two decades.

## Commanding Officers

A definitive and complete list of all commanding officers who served aboard HMS Hyperion (1807) requires direct consultation of primary archival records, specifically the series of commissioned officer service records known as ADM 196, held at The National Archives [6], [13]. These records are the most reliable source for identifying officers who held command or served in senior positions on a ship, detailing their names, ranks, dates of commission, postings, and periods of service [6], [23]. By searching the indexes for ADM 196, covering the period 1756–1917, it would be possible to construct a precise chronology of command for the entire lifespan of HMS Hyperion [23]. Each entry in these records typically lists the ships served on, the ranks held during each posting, and promotions, providing a granular view of an officer's career trajectory [6]. For example, a record for Sir Fleetwood Broughton Reynolds Pellew meticulously documents his service on various vessels, including flag-hoisting and flag-lowering orders, offering a template for the level of detail available for commanders of HMS Hyperion [6].

In addition to the ADM 196 series, other collections of records can supplement the search for information on the ship's commanders. Full and half pay registers for commissioned officers, found in the ADM 22–25 and PMG 15 series, may contain commission dates and postings relevant to identifying commanding officers [13]. Furthermore, the ADM 12 series, which contains indexes to letters received by the Admiralty concerning specific ships, can provide digests of correspondence that might mention appointments or removals of captains [24]. The captains' logs themselves, held in the ADM 51 series, are invaluable as they often begin with the appointment of the new commander, thereby linking the logbook volume directly to a specific individual's command [2], [3]. Finally, the Navy Board Progress Books (ADM 180), which list building and fitting costs, sometimes include survey information that could provide clues about the ship's state when first commissioned, potentially linking it to a particular captain's tenure [14].

The initial commanding officer of HMS Hyperion upon her commissioning in 1807 is not explicitly named in the provided sources. However, her presence in the List of H.M. Navy corrected to 1809 confirms she was fully operational by that time, meaning a commander must have taken charge shortly after her launch [1]. The first confirmed commander mentioned in the materials is Thomas Brodie [23]. The existence of his name in the ADM 196 indexes indicates that he served aboard the ship, and a deeper dive into the specific volumes would reveal the exact dates of his command [23]. It is highly probable that Brodie was one of the early commanders of the vessel. The service history of the ship, spanning from 1807 to at least 1829, implies that she had several commanding officers over her long career [33], [36]. Each change in command would represent a new chapter in her story, with different leaders shaping her performance in battle, discipline at sea, and overall operational effectiveness. Therefore, a comprehensive research effort focused on the aforementioned archival series is essential to populate the article with a complete and accurate list of her commanders, moving beyond the partial information currently available.

## Final Disposition

The final disposition of HMS Hyperion (1807) presents a clear case of conflicting historical narratives, where a modern anecdotal account is decisively refuted by a wealth of contemporary primary source documentation. The contradictory claim states that the ship was "wrecked near Newhaven on the west coast of Ireland in December 1811" [25]. This assertion, if true, would mark a tragic and early end to her career, just four years after her launch. However, a thorough examination of official naval records reveals this date to be incorrect and places her in active service for many years thereafter. The most authoritative source on this matter is the Royal Navy Loss List, a free online database compiled by the Maritime Archaeology Sea Trust (MAST) that catalogs all Royal Navy warships lost in service since 1512 [5], [18]. This database draws on official Admiralty records, memoirs, and archaeological reports to verify its findings [19]. Crucially, HMS Hyperion is not listed among the vessels lost in 1807 or any other year prior to 1812 [5], [18]. The absence of a ship from such a meticulously maintained list is itself strong negative evidence supporting her survival through 1811.

The most compelling evidence against the 1811 wreck claim comes from personal service records of sailors who served aboard the ship. These documents provide irrefutable proof of her continued commission. A detailed service record for an Able Seaman shows him serving aboard HMS Hyperion from 26 March 1811 to 18 August 1814 [35]. His progression from Able Seaman to Ship's Corporal and finally Coxswain over this three-year period confirms he was not just a transient crew member but a part of the ship's permanent establishment during that time [35]. It is physically impossible for such a record to exist if the ship had been lost in a wreck in December 1811. This single piece of evidence is sufficient to dismiss the earlier claim. Further corroboration is found in the ship's pay books. Entries from pay book number SB 3342 confirm an active crew serving aboard the ship between 18 September 1829 and 20 December 1829 [33]. Another pay book entry shows an Able Seaman serving from 4 March 1829 to 26 April 1829 [36]. These records definitively prove that HMS Hyperion remained in Royal Navy service, with a full crew and active administration, at least until mid-1829, over sixteen years after the claimed wreck date [33], [36].

Given this overwhelming evidence, the conclusion is inescapable: the claim of a 1811 wreck is erroneous. The most probable explanation for this historical error is a confusion of identities, perhaps conflating this vessel with another ship named *Hyperion* that may have served in the Boulogne area during the events described, or a general misattribution common in less-detailed historical accounts. The correct historical record indicates that HMS Hyperion (1807) had a long and distinguished career that lasted well into the post-Napoleonic era. She was decommissioned sometime after 1829. The exact circumstances of her decommissioning, disposal, or eventual sale are not detailed in the provided sources. This represents a gap in the known history of the vessel, a common outcome for many ships whose careers ended without a dramatic final act. Her legacy is therefore defined not by a sudden end but by a sustained period of dedicated service to the Royal Navy.

## Technical Specifications

The technical specifications of HMS Hyperion (1807) place her firmly within the standard profile of a Royal Navy fifth-rate frigate of the Napoleonic Wars era. As a 32-gun vessel, she was classified as a fifth-rate frigate, a designation that governed her size, crew complement, and armament [11], [21]. These ships were the workhorses of the fleet, versatile enough for independent cruising, convoy escort, and participation in major fleet actions. The table below summarizes the verified technical data for HMS Hyperion, synthesized from authoritative secondary sources and primary documents.

| Attribute | Specification |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Ship Class** | Fifth Rate Frigate [11], [21] |
| **Armament** | 28 × 18-pdr long guns, 2 × 9-pdr guns, 10 × 32-pdr carronades [10], [25] |
| **Length** | 150 ft (gun deck measurement) [25] |
| **Complement** | 640 men (recorded figure; typical complement for this class was 250-400) [25] |
| **Builder / Yard** | Edward Hunt at Chatham Dockyard (per one source); Edward Churchill, Master Shipwright at Plymouth Dockyard (per ship plan) [20], [25] |
| **Launched** | 1807 [11], [21] |

The armament of HMS Hyperion reflects the tactical doctrines of the age. The primary battery of 28 x 18-pounder long guns on her main deck provided significant long-range firepower, allowing her to engage enemy ships effectively before closing to melee distances [25]. The additional 2 x 9-pounder guns on the quarterdeck added flexibility, while the 10 x 32-pounder carronades represented the shift towards powerful, short-range weaponry ideal for the brutal, close-quarters fighting that often characterized frigate duels [10], [25]. This mixed armament made her a formidable opponent in almost any engagement scenario. The stated complement of 640 men is a point of note and potential discrepancy. While a 32-gun frigate required a substantial crew, a figure of this magnitude is unusually high compared to typical complements for the class, which generally ranged from 250 to 400 men depending on the voyage and operational tempo [25]. This higher number may reflect wartime conditions, an unusually large number of marines, or an administrative total rather than a typical sailing complement. The official muster rolls held in The National Archives (ADM 36 series) would be the only definitive source for verifying the actual crew numbers per muster period [15], [17].

The ship's design, based on the French frigate *Magicienne*, ensured she possessed the qualities necessary for a successful frigate: speed, maneuverability, and a robust hull [21]. Her 150-foot gun deck length was characteristic of her class, providing a stable platform for her heavy guns while maintaining a relatively low silhouette [25]. The existence of a signed ship plan by Edward Churchill at Plymouth Dockyard provides a rare glimpse into her precise construction and proportions [20]. This document, now housed in the National Maritime Museum, serves as a crucial primary source for naval historians, offering details on her hull form and structural features that are not always apparent from textual descriptions alone [20]. In summary, HMS Hyperion was a well-proportioned and heavily armed frigate, embodying the best practices of British naval architecture in the early 19th century. Her specifications made her a potent instrument of British naval power, suited for the demanding roles she undertook throughout her long and active service career.
