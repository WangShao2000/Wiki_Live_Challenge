### Overview
Parasitic ants, or social parasitic ants, refer to ant species that depend on other ant species' colonies for reproduction and survival, often through infiltration and exploitation of host resources. This behavior has evolved independently numerous times, highlighting adaptations in social insects. While not without debate on exact mechanisms, studies indicate a progression from free-living ancestors to specialized parasitic lifestyles.

### Key Types
Temporary parasitism involves queens invading and replacing host queens during early colony stages. Slave-making entails raiding for host brood to serve as workers. Inquiline forms coexist permanently, often without producing their own workers.

### Evolutionary Insights
Parasitism likely stems from loss of independent colony founding, leading to dependent strategies. Genomic studies show convergent traits like reduced chemoreceptors in parasites.

Parasitic ants are ant species that engage in social parasitism, a form of interspecific exploitation where one ant species relies on the labor and resources of another ant species to survive and reproduce. Also known as socially parasitic ants, these species form mixed colonies with their hosts and exhibit various degrees of dependency, ranging from temporary reliance during colony foundation to permanent coexistence without producing their own worker caste. Over 400 species of parasitic ants have been identified, representing about 3% of all known ant species, and this lifestyle has evolved independently at least 60 times across six ant subfamilies. Parasitic ants are notable in evolutionary biology for illustrating transitions from free-living to exploitative social behaviors, often following Emery's rule, which posits that parasites and their hosts are closely related. They are predominantly found in temperate regions of the Holarctic, with higher diversity in subfamilies like Formicinae and Myrmicinae, and play a role in studies of speciation, chemical mimicry, and social evolution.

## Types
Social parasitism in ants manifests in three primary forms, distinguished by the duration and nature of the host-parasite interaction. These types reflect varying levels of integration and dependency on the host colony.

### Temporary social parasitism
In temporary social parasitism, the parasitic queen infiltrates a host colony, typically after her nuptial flight, and eliminates or supplants the resident queen. The host workers then rear the parasitic queen's offspring until the host workforce naturally dies off, leaving a pure colony of the parasitic species. This strategy is common in species that retain the ability to produce their own workers but depend on hosts only for initial establishment. Parasitic queens employ methods such as chemical camouflage, mimicking the host's cuticular hydrocarbons, or using force to gain acceptance. Examples include species in the genus *Lasius*, such as *Lasius claviger*, which targets related host species.

### Slave-making (dulosis)
Slave-making ants, or dulotic species, establish colonies similarly to temporary parasites but additionally conduct raids on nearby host colonies to capture brood, which ecloses as enslaved workers integrated into the parasite's nest. These slaves perform essential tasks like foraging, brood care, and defense, as many slave-makers have reduced mandibles or other adaptations that impair their ability to function independently. Dulosis can be facultative, where parasites can survive with few or no slaves, or obligate, requiring a high proportion of slaves (often over 90%). Raids involve chemical signals to pacify hosts and high-efficiency tactics in obligate forms. Notable genera include *Polyergus* and *Formica*, with *Polyergus breviceps* enslaving *Formica argentea* using specialized sickle-shaped mandibles.

### Inquiline parasitism
Permanent inquilines coexist with the host queen, producing primarily reproductive offspring while relying on host workers for all labor. These parasites often lack a worker caste entirely or produce reduced, vulnerable workers. Inquilines may physically attach to the host queen, as seen in *Teleutomyrmex schneideri*, which has adaptations like concave abdomens and long claws for gripping. This form is typically host-specific and involves advanced chemical mimicry to avoid detection. Examples include *Acromyrmex insinuator* in leaf-cutter ants and *Formica talbotae*.

## Evolution
The evolution of parasitic strategies in ants is interconnected with variations in colony-founding behaviors, particularly the shift from independent colony founding to dependent methods. Phylogenetic studies indicate that social parasitism arose from ancestors that lost the capacity for independent founding, leading to exploitation of host colonies. In the genus *Formica*, parasitism originated around 30 million years ago in Eurasia, with temporary parasitism emerging first about 18 million years ago, followed by dulosis around 14 million years ago, and inquilinism evolving independently in some lineages. This progression supports a model where temporary parasitism serves as a precursor to more specialized forms, with no observed reversals to free-living states.

Emery's rule, in its loose form, is supported by evidence showing parasites and hosts are often closely related, potentially through sympatric speciation, though allopatric processes are also evident in some cases. Genomic analyses reveal convergent evolutionary traces in slave-makers, including contractions in chemoreceptor gene families (odorant and gustatory receptors) and intensified selection on genes related to raiding behaviors, contrasting with hosts' retention of duplicated genes for defense. Mutant studies in species like *Harpagoxenus sublaevis* demonstrate how caste differentiation changes can lead to workerless parasites, mirroring natural transitions from free-living ancestors. Factors promoting parasitism include cooler climates facilitating polygyny and polydomy, which may predispose colonies to infiltration.

In specific lineages, such as bulldog ants (*Myrmecia*), social parasitism shows biogeographic patterns tied to Australian and New Caledonian distributions, with evolutionary origins linked to ancestral raiding behaviors. Overall, the repeated evolution of parasitism underscores its adaptive value in resource-scarce environments, with ongoing research exploring genetic bases and ecological drivers.

## Distribution and ecology
Parasitic ants are disproportionately distributed in temperate regions, particularly in the Palearctic and Nearctic zones, where they can comprise up to 30% of local ant diversity. They are rare in tropical areas, possibly due to higher host diversity and stronger defenses in warmer climates. Hotspots include Europe, North America, and parts of Asia, with genera like *Formica* showing high parasitic diversity in these areas.

Ecologically, parasitic ants influence host populations by reducing fitness through brood loss, resource theft, and queen elimination. Hosts may evolve countermeasures, such as increased aggression or slave rebellions, where enslaved workers kill parasitic pupae to favor host reproduction. Parasites, in turn, use advanced mimicry—chemical, morphological, and behavioral—to integrate, including adopting host odors or producing appeasing secretions. This arms race highlights co-evolutionary dynamics, with implications for biodiversity and ecosystem roles, as ants affect soil aeration and pest control.

## Examples
Prominent examples illustrate the diversity of parasitic strategies:

- *Polyergus breviceps*: An obligate slave-maker in North America, known for its red coloration contrasting black host slaves like *Formica argentea*. It conducts group raids, using chemical signals for recruitment and pacification.
- *Formica sanguinea*: A facultative slave-maker widespread in Europe and Asia, raiding multiple *Formica* species; it can maintain colonies with minimal slaves.
- *Teleutomyrmex schneideri*: A rare inquiline in Europe, physically attaching to host queens of *Tetramorium* species as an ectoparasite-like form.
- *Protomognathus americanus*: A dulotic species in North America, targeting *Temnothorax* hosts; studies show host rebellion behaviors.
- *Acromyrmex insinuator*: An inquiline in South American leaf-cutter ants, integrating into *Acromyrmex echinatior* colonies with minimal worker production.

These species demonstrate adaptations like specialized mandibles, reduced body size, and chemical deception, underscoring the complexity of parasitic lifestyles.
