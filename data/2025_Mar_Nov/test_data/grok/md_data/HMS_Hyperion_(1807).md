### Construction and technical specifications  
Ordered on 13 June 1805 as part of wartime shipbuilding efforts, Hyperion was built by John Perry at Kingston upon Hull, Yorkshire, under contract. She was launched on 3 November 1807 and completed at Sheerness Dockyard for sea service. The design by Sir John Henslow drew from the French Magicienne (captured 1781), resulting in a vessel optimized for pursuit and engagement. Approximate dimensions included a gun deck length of 126 feet, beam of 35 feet, and draught of 8–13 feet depending on load, with a burthen of about 690 tons. Armament comprised 26 18-pounder guns on the main deck, supplemented by 6 9-pounders and carronades on upper decks. Crew size varied but typically ranged from 220 to 250, including officers, seamen, and marines. A 1829 plan by Henslow documents her lines and profile, preserved in museum collections.

### Service history  
#### Commissioning and Mediterranean service  
Fitted out at Sheerness in 1807, Hyperion entered service in 1808 under Captain Thomas Cecil Brodie. She sailed for the Mediterranean on 30 June, supporting British operations against Napoleonic forces through patrols and convoy duties.  

#### Caribbean station and Haitian incident  
By 1811, Hyperion was on the Jamaica station. While at Gonaives, Saint-Domingue, to resupply, a British merchant sought refuge aboard, prompting Haitian batteries to fire on her boats, killing three and capturing Brodie and two officers. Lieutenant Morgan positioned the ship to threaten the town and a Haitian frigate, sending an ultimatum that secured their release. This event occurred amid Haiti's civil war, where British vessels faced risks from local blockades enforced by Henri Christophe's forces. Earlier in 1810, Hyperion had encountered a seized British ship, the Crown, but allowed Christophe's navy to retain it after documentation review.  

#### North American operations and impressment attempt  
Under Captain William Pryce Cumby from September 1811, Hyperion sailed to Mexico and Cuba before refitting in England. In 1812, she protected whalers in Davis Strait and escorted convoys, wintering in St. John's, Newfoundland. In April 1813, Cumby tried to land a press gang for recruits, but local magistrates restricted impressment to harbors due to land-based unrest. This incident illustrated colonial opposition to naval recruitment, with Cumby unable to proceed ashore. She departed with convoys in November 1813.  

#### Capture of Rattlesnake and end of war  
In 1814, operating in European waters, Hyperion captured the American privateer Rattlesnake on 26 June after a chase. The brig, armed with 16 guns and crewed by 130, had disrupted British trade by taking 28 merchants. Brought to Plymouth, it was sold into merchant service. Temporary command by James Lillicrap in 1815 involved Oporto convoy escort; Cumby returned, and the ship was decommissioned post-war.  

#### South American mission and Cape station  
Recommissioned in 1818 under Captain Thomas Searle, Hyperion hosted the Prince Regent for a cruise before heading to South America. She relieved Captain Shirreff of Andromache, criticized for non-neutrality in independence wars due to ties with Lord Cochrane. Searle profited from transporting specie home.  

Under Lillicrap from 1821, she carried Lord Charles Somerset to the Cape, assuming temporary station command. In 1822, she rescued the Albion and Russian warships during a gale, earning rewards. She supplied Ascension and St. Helena, then joined Jamaica for anti-piracy cruises off Cuba, condemning captured pirates.  

#### Jamaica and anti-smuggling duties  
Captain George Rich commanded from 1823 on Jamaica. From 1825, Captain William James Mingaye led anti-smuggling patrols at Newhaven, hosting numerous lieutenants over five years until paid off in 1831.  

### Fate and legacy  
Broken up in 1833, Hyperion's career spanned 26 years, embodying the frigate's versatility in war and peace. Her actions contributed to British naval dominance, though no prominent controversies emerged. Archival records and biographies preserve her history, with artifacts like ship plans aiding modern understanding.
