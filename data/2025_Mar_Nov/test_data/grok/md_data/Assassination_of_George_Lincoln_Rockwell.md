### Context and Significance
George Lincoln Rockwell established the American Nazi Party in 1959, promoting white supremacist ideologies influenced by National Socialism. The assassination highlighted tensions within extremist groups, as Patler had been expelled earlier that year. Sources describe the event as a turning point that fragmented the organization but did not eliminate its influence on later far-right movements.

### Key Controversies
Accounts vary on the exact motive, with some emphasizing ethnic divisions within the party and others pointing to disagreements over strategy and symbolism. Evidence from trials and reports leans toward internal rivalry rather than external opposition, though the topic involves sensitive historical interpretations of extremism.

### Background
George Lincoln Rockwell was born on March 9, 1918, in Bloomington, Illinois, to parents who worked as vaudeville performers. He attended Brown University to study philosophy but left in 1941 to join the United States Navy, where he served as a pilot during World War II in the Atlantic and Pacific theaters. After the war, Rockwell pursued a career in advertising and illustration, attending Pratt Institute. During his service in the Korean War, while stationed in Iceland, he read Adolf Hitler's *Mein Kampf*, which influenced his adoption of National Socialist ideas. He came to view these ideologies as a means to address what he perceived as racial and societal issues in the United States.

In the mid-1950s, Rockwell returned to civilian life and began organizing far-right activities. He briefly collaborated with figures like Harold N. Arrowsmith to produce anti-Semitic materials. In 1959, he founded the American Nazi Party in Arlington, Virginia, where he established its headquarters. The party adopted uniforms resembling those of Nazi storm troopers, used provocative symbols such as swastikas, and published materials like *The Storm Trooper* magazine. Rockwell's tactics included public rallies, pickets against civil rights events, and disruptions of films and visits by figures associated with Judaism or African American rights. For instance, party members picketed the 1960 showing of the film *Exodus* and protested the visit of Israeli Prime Minister David Ben-Gurion. Rockwell denied the Holocaust in public statements, claiming it was a fabrication, and advocated for the deportation of African Americans to Africa and extreme measures against Jewish people.

The American Nazi Party operated from several locations in Arlington, including 928 North Randolph Street (now an apartment complex) and a house on Wilson Boulevard (now part of Upton Hill Regional Park). Membership peaked at around 50 to 200 individuals, primarily young men from urban areas. The group faced local opposition, including bomb threats against community sites and efforts by groups like Citizens Concerned to revoke its charter. Rockwell ran for governor of Virginia in 1965, receiving 6,366 votes. He was arrested multiple times for disturbances but avoided convictions. In a 1966 interview published in *Playboy*, he expressed ambitions for national leadership by 1972.

Internal conflicts grew in the party during 1967, including disputes over ethnic composition and strategy. Rockwell expelled several members, including John Patler, amid accusations of fostering division.

### The Assassination
On August 25, 1967, around noon, Rockwell drove his 1958 Chevrolet to the Dominion Hills shopping center in Arlington, Virginia, to do laundry at the Econ-o-Wash laundromat. After completing his wash, he told the proprietor he needed to retrieve bleach from home. As he backed out of a parking space, two shots were fired from the roof of the one-story shopping center building. The bullets, from a 7.63 mm Mauser semiautomatic pistol, struck Rockwell in the head and chest, causing fatal wounds to his heart. He collapsed beside his car, next to a box of laundry detergent and a newspaper. Witnesses, including laundromat owner J.W. Hancock and nearby residents like Glenn Hall and Nancy Thoburn, heard the shots and saw a man fleeing across the roof, jumping walls, and running through yards. The perpetrator discarded clothing items and the weapon in a nearby stream at Four Mile Run near Bon Air Park. Police recovered two bullet casings from the roof and footprints that were later matched to the suspect.

Rockwell, aged 49, was pronounced dead at the scene. The location is now part of northern Arlington Regional Park, adjacent to the remaining shopping center. This was not the first attempt on his life; in June 1967, a shot had been fired at him near party headquarters, though he did not obtain a firearm permit afterward.

### Perpetrator
John Patler, born Yanacki Patsalos on January 6, 1938, in New York City, was a Greek-American former U.S. Marine. He changed his name to John Patler in the early 1960s. Patler joined the American Nazi Party in its early years and rose to a high-ranking position, serving as editor of *The Storm Trooper* and managing a printing plant in Spotsylvania County, Virginia. He was described in party circles as having dark features, which contributed to ethnic tensions within the group. Patler had an intermittent involvement with the party: he left in 1961 to form a rival organization, returned, and was expelled again in March or April 1967 by Rockwell for fomenting dissension. Reports indicate he accused fair-skinned members of being "blue-eyed devils" and stirred conflicts between members of different ethnic backgrounds, such as those with blond versus dark hair.

Patler's motives appear rooted in ideological and personal grievances. He advocated removing overt German Nazi elements from the party, proposing a name change to the National Socialist White People's Party to focus more on white nationalism. Some accounts suggest he felt unprotected from abuse by other members, including Matthias Koehl and William Pierce. A letter from Patler found in Rockwell's wallet after the assassination indicated an attempt at reconciliation. Patler was arrested about 45 minutes after the shooting at a bus stop on Washington Boulevard and North Inglewood Street, three-quarters of a mile from the scene. He was unarmed, did not resist, and matched witness descriptions. After his release in the 1980s, he changed his name to John Christ Patsolos, started a failed Spanish-language newspaper, and worked as a commercial artist.

### Trial and Conviction
Patler's trial took place in the Circuit Court of Arlington County, Virginia, before Judge Charles S. Russell. Commonwealth's Attorney William J. Hassan prosecuted, seeking the death penalty, while defense attorneys Thomas Harrigan and Thomas Morris argued Patler was not at the scene, claiming he was running errands with his family. Evidence included witness identifications, footprints on the roof, the recovered Mauser pistol (test-fired on Patler's father-in-law's property in Highland County, Virginia), discarded clothing, and bullet casings analyzed by the Federal Bureau of Investigation. Only one party member, Matthias Koehl, testified for the prosecution.

On December 15, 1967, after four hours of deliberation, a jury of 10 men and two women convicted Patler of first-degree murder. He was sentenced to 20 years in prison. His wife protested the verdict in court. Patler appealed, alleging constitutional issues such as tainted witness identifications, illegal evidence seizures, and delayed disclosure of FBI test results under Brady v. Maryland. The Virginia Supreme Court upheld the conviction in 1970, and the U.S. Supreme Court denied certiorari in 1972. In a 1974 federal appeal (Patler v. Slayton), the Fourth Circuit Court affirmed the denial of habeas corpus, ruling on grounds including the open fields doctrine for searches and timely evidence disclosure. Patler was paroled in 1975 but violated parole and returned to prison until the early 1980s. In 1969, he won a $15,000 libel judgment against a party official.

### Aftermath and Legacy
The assassination disrupted the American Nazi Party, leading to a leadership transition under Matthias Koehl, who renamed it the National Socialist White People's Party in January 1967 (formally after Rockwell's death). Membership declined, with the group relocating within Arlington to sites like 806 Taylor Street and 2507 North Franklin Road. Activities continued into the 1980s, including parades and commemorations, but the party splintered into smaller factions like the New Order in 1982. A dispute arose over Rockwell's burial: party members sought interment at Culpeper National Cemetery with Nazi honors, but officials denied it due to the symbols; he was cremated instead.

The event weakened organized neo-Nazism in the U.S. at the time, fragmenting it into decentralized cells. Rockwell's ideologies, including the phrase "white power" and Holocaust denial, influenced later far-right figures and groups. By the 1970s, neo-Nazi organizations persisted, with estimates of several active entities. The assassination has been linked in historical analyses to ongoing patterns of internal violence in extremist movements. In Arlington, local resistance intensified post-1967, with media coverage and community efforts contributing to the group's eventual relocation out of the area.
