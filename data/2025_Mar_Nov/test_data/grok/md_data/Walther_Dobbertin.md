### Early life
Dobbertin was born in Berlin, Germany, on 28 August 1882.[1][2] Little is documented about his early education or training in photography, but by his early twenties, he had developed skills in the field. In 1903, at the age of 21, he emigrated to German East Africa, a colony established by the German Empire in the 1880s that encompassed present-day Tanzania, Burundi, and Rwanda.[3][4] He settled in Dar es Salaam, the colonial capital, where he established himself as a commercial photographer.[5]

### Career in German East Africa
From 1903 onward, Dobbertin operated as a photographer and publisher in German East Africa, founding book and art shops in Dar es Salaam, Tanga, and Moshi.[6] His work focused on producing postcards, photograph albums, and prints that captured daily life in the colony. These included street scenes, colonial infrastructure such as hospitals and fortresses, indigenous communities, and natural landscapes.[7] For example, one album from around 1905 features platinum prints of Tanga, showing urban development and local architecture.[8]

Dobbertin also documented big game hunts and wildlife, with one 1910 album containing 48 images of animals like lions, elephants, and rhinos.[9] Other collections from 1910–1911 depict African people engaged in activities such as transporting goods, village life, and fishing.[10] His photographs often reflected a European colonial perspective, emphasizing exploration and resource exploitation.[11] By 1910, he had published works like *Landschaftsbilder aus Deutsch-Ost-Afrika*, a 24-page collection of landscape images.[12]

His business, Kunstverlag Walther Dobbertin, produced series of postcards and albums that were sold commercially, contributing to the visual representation of the colony in Germany and beyond.[13]

### World War I
With the outbreak of World War I in 1914, Dobbertin joined the German colonial forces under General Paul von Lettow-Vorbeck.[14] As the only known photographer documenting the German side of the East African Campaign, he captured images of askari (African soldiers in German service), military operations, and the harsh terrain.[15] His photographs included scenes of skirmishes, troop movements, and daily military life, providing a rare visual record of the guerrilla warfare that characterized the campaign.[16]

Dobbertin served until 1916, when he was captured by British forces.[17] He was interned as a prisoner of war in India until his release after the war's end in 1918.[18] Some of his negatives and photographs from this period were later preserved, though questions remain about how they were transported back to Germany during his internment.[19]

### Post-war career
After his release, Dobbertin returned to Germany, where his colonial assets had been expropriated under the Treaty of Versailles.[20] He settled in Wiedenhof (now part of Jesteburg, Lower Saxony) and reopened a bookshop and photography business.[21] In the 1930s, he compiled and published collections of his East African photographs. Notable works include *Die Soldaten Lettow-Vorbecks* (1932), a book of photogravures with 119 plates depicting the East African Campaign, and *Schwarz-Weiss-Rot Über Ost-Afrika* (1933), featuring 96 plates from his images.[22][23]

During the Nazi era, Dobbertin was a member of the Sturmabteilung (SA), a paramilitary wing of the Nazi Party, and served as a district leader in the Reich Colonial Association, an organization promoting German colonial interests.[24] Due to these affiliations, his business license was revoked by Allied authorities in 1945 following World War II.[25] The license was later restored, allowing him to continue operating his bookshop until 1960.[26]

### Death
Dobbertin died on 12 January 1961 in Jesteburg, Germany, at the age of 78.[27]

### Legacy
Dobbertin's photographs are preserved in several institutions, including the Bundesarchiv in Germany, which holds his personal papers documenting colonial life in German East Africa.[28] Collections also exist at Yale University's Beinecke Rare Book and Manuscript Library, Northwestern University's Melville J. Herskovits Library of African Studies, and the New York Public Library's Photographers' Identities Catalog.[29][30][31]

His work has been used in historical research, such as studies of environmental changes in the Usambara Mountains and representations of colonial cities like Bagamoyo.[32][33] Reprints of his books, including a 2005 English-German edition of *Lettow-Vorbeck's Soldiers*, have made his images accessible to modern audiences.[34] While his photographs offer valuable historical documentation, they also reflect the biases of colonial-era imagery, focusing on European achievements and often portraying African subjects through a lens of otherness.[35]
