## Publication history

The Banshee was first published in 1920 by Sands & Company in London and Edinburgh.[3] O'Donnell, known for his works on ghosts and the supernatural, wrote the book as part of his broader output on paranormal phenomena. The text was later made available through Project Gutenberg in 2010, facilitating digital access.[1] Subsequent editions include reprints by CreateSpace Independent Publishing Platform in 2016 and enriched versions with additional notes, such as a 2021 edition emphasizing cultural interpretations.[4] The book spans approximately 200 pages in its original form and includes an addenda with supplementary anecdotes.[5]

## Content

The book is structured into 13 chapters and an addenda, presenting a mix of folklore analysis, case studies, and comparative mythology. O'Donnell defines the banshee (from the Irish "bean sidhe," meaning "woman of the fairy mound") as a spirit attached to specific ancient Irish families of Celtic descent, signaling impending death through wails, screams, or other sounds.[1] He categorizes banshees into friendly types, which express sorrow, and malevolent ones, which exhibit glee or malice.[6]

Key chapters include discussions of origins, suggesting banshees may derive from disembodied souls, elemental spirits, or ancient sorcery.[1] Historical cases feature accounts like Lady Fanshawe's 17th-century encounter at Lemaneagh Castle, where a banshee's wail preceded a death, and reports from Irish families such as the O'Flahertys and Kenealys.[1] O'Donnell addresses manifestations abroad, on battlefields, and at sea, with examples from the Battle of the Boyne and Napoleonic Wars.[1] He differentiates banshees from similar entities, such as Welsh "Gwrach y Rhibyn" or German "White Ladies," and includes literary depictions from authors like Thomas Crofton Croker and Thomas Moore.[1]

The author's personal experiences form a dedicated chapter, recounting auditory omens before family deaths, including his father's murder in Abyssinia.[1] The addenda provides additional tales, such as banshee knocks signaling a relative's passing.[1]

## Themes

O'Donnell emphasizes the banshee's exclusivity to families of Milesian (ancient Celtic) lineage, arguing it does not appear to those of Anglo-Saxon or Norman descent.[1] Themes of prophecy and fatality recur, with banshees foretelling not only death but also ruin or tragedy.[1] The book contrasts benevolent and hostile banshees, linking malice to past family misdeeds like murders.[1] Cross-cultural comparisons highlight unique Irish elements, such as familial attachment, while noting parallels in global death omens.[1] O'Donnell's narrative blends skepticism with belief, presenting the banshee as a persistent cultural phenomenon amid modernization.[2]

## Reception

Contemporary reception viewed The Banshee as a straightforward compilation of folklore, appreciated for its accessibility but noted for inconsistencies in banshee descriptions across accounts.[7] Later assessments position it within O'Donnell's body of work on the occult, praising its role in preserving Irish oral traditions while critiquing its reliance on unverified anecdotes.[8][9] The book has been referenced in studies of Irish mythology and ghost lore, though some scholars dismiss it as sensationalist due to O'Donnell's self-proclaimed ghost-hunting background.[10] Digital editions have renewed interest, with readers highlighting its value as a historical artifact of early 20th-century paranormal literature.[2]
