### Overview
The incident highlighted vulnerabilities in large industrial buildings to extreme weather, particularly in tornado-prone areas. An Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) probe identified gaps in training and access to emergency tools, though no penalties were issued. State-level responses included forming a task force that recommended mandatory tornado shelters in warehouses, reflecting broader concerns about worker protections in logistics facilities.

### Key Controversies
Evidence suggests Amazon directed employees to shelter in restrooms not designed for tornadoes, with some reports indicating workers were discouraged from leaving amid holiday demand. Families and survivors have questioned whether safer alternatives, like evacuation, could have prevented fatalities. While Amazon maintains the building met codes and procedures were followed, critics, including U.S. lawmakers, have argued for stronger safeguards, noting the site's location in a high-risk wind zone.

### Recent Developments
As of late 2025, the rebuilt warehouse operates without a dedicated storm shelter, per local codes at the time of reconstruction. Legislative efforts to implement task force recommendations remain under discussion, with no statewide mandates yet enacted.


### Edwardsville Amazon Warehouse Collapse

The Edwardsville Amazon warehouse collapse was a structural failure at an Amazon fulfillment center in Edwardsville, Illinois, United States, on December 10, 2021. An EF-3 tornado with peak winds of 150 mph (240 km/h) struck the facility during a severe weather outbreak, causing the roof to collapse and 40-foot (12 m) concrete walls to fall inward. Six workers were killed, and several others were injured. The incident prompted investigations into building codes, workplace safety protocols, and emergency response measures. It also led to wrongful death lawsuits against Amazon and calls for enhanced regulations on warehouse construction in tornado-prone regions. The facility, known as DLI4, was rebuilt with improved wind-resistant features and reopened in September 2024.

#### Background
The DLI4 warehouse, located in the Gateway Commerce Center industrial park straddling Edwardsville and Pontoon Beach, Illinois, was a 1.1 million-square-foot (100,000 m²) facility operated by Amazon as a delivery station. Constructed in 2020, it employed over 100 workers, many of whom were contracted delivery drivers handling increased holiday shipments during peak season. The building was designed to meet local codes, including walls rated to withstand 90 mph (145 km/h) winds for three seconds, but it lacked a dedicated storm shelter. [1][2]

Edwardsville is situated in a region classified as Wind Zone IV by the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA), indicating the highest risk for tornadoes in the United States. The area experiences frequent severe weather, and the warehouse was part of a growing logistics hub in the St. Louis metropolitan area, where large industrial structures are common. Amazon, as the tenant, leased the building from a San Diego-based real estate firm, which was responsible for construction decisions. [1][3]

#### Tornado and Collapse
On the evening of December 10, 2021, a tornado warning was issued for Madison County, Illinois, between 8:06 p.m. and 8:16 p.m. Central Standard Time. The EF-3 tornado, part of a larger outbreak affecting multiple states, touched down and struck the southern end of the DLI4 warehouse around 8:27 p.m. The storm's 150 mph winds caused catastrophic damage, ripping off the roof and collapsing approximately 40-foot-high, 11-inch-thick (28 cm) concrete walls inward. The northern portion of the building sustained less severe damage. [2][5]

Amazon managers directed employees to shelter in place, using megaphones and alerts to guide them to designated areas, primarily restrooms. However, the southern restroom—where many sought refuge—collapsed under the debris. Survivors reported being trapped under rubble, with rescue efforts complicated by unstable structures and ongoing weather threats. Emergency responders from Edwardsville Police Department and surrounding areas, including St. Louis, conducted search and recovery operations that extended into the following day. [2][4][5]

#### Casualties and Rescue Efforts
Six workers died in the collapse, all of whom were in the southern section of the warehouse. The victims were identified as:
- Deandre S. Morrow, 28, of St. Louis, Missouri
- Kevin D. Dickey, 62, of Carlyle, Illinois
- Etheria S. Hebb, 34, of St. Louis, Missouri
- Larry E. Virden, 46, of Collinsville, Illinois
- Austin J. McEwen, 26, of Edwardsville, Illinois
- Clayton Lynn Cope, 29, of Alton, Illinois

Five of the deceased were contracted delivery drivers, while one (Cope) was a maintenance mechanic employed directly by Amazon. At least one survivor, Craig Yost, suffered severe injuries including a fractured pelvis, fractured hip, and concussion, requiring surgery. Reports indicated that some employees were unaware of the designated shelter location or had not participated in recent severe weather drills. [2][8]

Rescue operations involved local fire departments, police, and specialized teams using heavy equipment to clear debris. All victims were accounted for by December 11, 2021. The incident drew comparisons to other workplace disasters during severe weather, such as the 2021 Mayfield, Kentucky, candle factory collapse. [2][5][9]

#### Investigations and Findings
The U.S. Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) initiated an investigation on December 13, 2021, focusing on Amazon's emergency action plan and response to the tornado. In April 2022, OSHA issued a Hazard Alert Letter, expressing concerns that the plan lacked site-specific details for hazards, employees were sometimes unaware of shelter locations, and a megaphone for alerts was inaccessible during the event. No fines or citations were imposed, as OSHA determined Amazon did not violate federal standards. The agency recommended voluntary improvements, such as better drills and customized instructions. [4][2]

An internal Amazon review examined structural claims, including allegations from a first responder engineer that support columns were not properly anchored to the foundation—a potential code violation. Amazon denied these claims, stating the building complied with codes, but did not release the full internal findings publicly. During rebuilding in 2022, inspectors identified loose or missing anchor bolts on nearly a dozen columns, which were corrected. [5][1]

The U.S. House Oversight Committee accused Amazon in June 2022 of obstructing its probe into the collapse, requesting documents on safety policies. In December 2022, U.S. Senator Elizabeth Warren, Representative Cori Bush, and Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez sent a letter to Amazon questioning the decision to rebuild without a storm shelter, noting the site's high tornado risk. [3][5]

In response to the incident, the Illinois General Assembly created the Warehouse Safety Standards Task Force in January 2023, a 16-member group including lawmakers, safety experts, and local officials. The task force held meetings on emergency responses, management policies, and building design. Its December 2024 report, released in early 2025, recommended amending the International Building Code—adopted statewide in Illinois in 2024—to require tornado shelters in warehouses. It also called for site-specific emergency plans shared with first responders, emphasizing accountability for owners and operators. As of December 2025, no legislation has been enacted based on these recommendations. [6][1][5]

#### Legal Actions
Families of the deceased and survivors filed at least five wrongful death and negligence lawsuits against Amazon and related parties, including the building's constructors and owners. A suit by the parents of Austin McEwen alleged Amazon failed to provide safe shelter, warn employees adequately, or evacuate despite knowing of the impending storm, prioritizing holiday operations over safety. The complaint claimed workers were directed to an unsafe restroom and discouraged from leaving. [7]

One lawsuit was scheduled for mediation in 2024, with a potential trial in 2025, according to Madison County court records. Other suits raised similar claims of negligence, including the absence of a proper storm shelter. Amazon has defended its actions, stating procedures aligned with best practices. Outcomes of the cases remain pending as of late 2025. [1][7][5]

#### Rebuilding and Safety Improvements
Amazon rebuilt the DLI4 facility to pre-collapse specifications but incorporated updates to Edwardsville's building codes, including walls rated for 114 mph (183 km/h) winds for three seconds. The warehouse reopened on September 4, 2024—1,000 days after the tornado—as a delivery station, with operations ramping up gradually. Amazon hired a meteorologist for weather monitoring and implemented extreme weather training, but no dedicated storm shelter was added, as such features were at the landlord's discretion and not required by codes at the time. None of the approximately 30 warehouses in the Gateway Commerce Center have shelters. [1][3]

The rebuilding process included safety testing of mechanical equipment. Critics, including the congressional letter signatories, argued the lack of a shelter represented a missed opportunity for improvement in a high-risk area. [3][1]

#### Impact and Legacy
The collapse raised awareness of warehouse worker safety during natural disasters, contributing to broader discussions on labor conditions in the logistics industry. It influenced the veto of the proposed Warehouse Worker Protection Act in Illinois in 2025, which aimed to address quotas and safety but was deemed to have operational issues. The task force's recommendations have informed ongoing policy debates, with advocates emphasizing the need to prioritize human lives over profits in industrial design. Memorial services for the victims have been held annually, and the incident is cited in studies on tornado resilience in large structures. [9][6][5]
