# Morris Park Aerodrome

The Morris Park Aerodrome was an early airfield located in the Bronx, New York City, operating from 1908 to 1910. Established on the site of the former Morris Park Racecourse, it served as a venue for aviation experiments and public exhibitions during the pioneering era of flight. Research indicates it was among the first dedicated airfields in the United States, hosting tests of helicopters, gliders, and early airplanes, though many attempts faced technical challenges. Evidence suggests its significance in promoting heavier-than-air aviation, but operations ceased after a fire damaged the facilities.

### Key Points
- **Establishment**: Leased in 1908 by the Aeronautic Society of New York to foster aviation innovation, amid growing interest following the Wright Brothers' achievements.
- **Notable Activities**: Included exhibitions in 1908 and 1909, with attempts at helicopter flights and Glenn Curtiss's early airplane demonstrations, marking some of the first flights over New York City.
- **Closure and Legacy**: Ended around 1910 due to a fire and subsequent land development; the site highlights early aviation's experimental nature and local contributions to flight history.
- **Controversies or Uncertainties**: Sources vary on crowd sizes and success rates of flights, reflecting the era's rapid but uneven progress in aviation technology.

### Location and Background
The aerodrome occupied approximately 372 acres in what is now the Morris Park neighborhood of the Bronx. It repurposed the grounds of the Morris Park Racecourse, which had operated from 1889 to 1904 as a prominent horse racing venue.

### Operations and Events
Activities focused on testing experimental aircraft, with public events drawing crowds to witness emerging flight technologies. Key demonstrations involved inventors like Wilbur R. Kimball and Glenn H. Curtiss.

### Significance
The site played a role in advancing public awareness of aviation, though its short lifespan underscores the challenges of early 20th-century flight development.


The Morris Park Aerodrome was an airfield in the Bronx, New York City, United States, active from 1908 to 1910. It was established by the Aeronautic Society of New York on the site of the former Morris Park Racecourse to promote experiments in heavier-than-air flight. The facility hosted early aviation exhibitions, including tests of helicopters, gliders, and airplanes, and is noted for Glenn H. Curtiss's flights in 1909, which were among the first over New York City. Operations ended following a fire in 1910, after which the land was sold for residential development. The aerodrome's brief history reflects the experimental and public-facing aspects of early aviation in the United States.

## Background

The site originated as the Morris Park Racecourse, opened in 1889 by John Albert Morris, a businessman known for his involvement in the Louisiana State Lottery Company. The racecourse, spanning 372 acres, was described in contemporary accounts as a leading venue for horse racing and hosted the Belmont Stakes from 1890 to 1904. Attendance declined in the early 1900s, leading to its closure in 1904. Briefly repurposed for automobile racing, it featured the first Vanderbilt Cup race in 1905 before falling into disuse.

In 1908, the Aeronautic Society of New York, formed in July of that year by individuals dissatisfied with the Aero Club of America's focus on ballooning, leased the property from the City of New York for two years. The society aimed to advance heavier-than-air aviation, inspired by the Wright Brothers' successes. They converted stables and barns into workshops for building and testing aircraft, creating what sources describe as the world's first formal airfield.

## Establishment and Facilities

The aerodrome was prepared by removing fences to create an open straightway for flights of at least one kilometer. Initial activities included assembly and testing of experimental machines, such as Wilbur R. Kimball's helicopter in September 1908. The site attracted local interest, with residents observing the inventors' efforts.

## Exhibitions and Events

### 1908 Exhibition

On November 3, 1908, the Aeronautic Society held its first public exhibition and tournament at the aerodrome, combined with championship motorcycle races organized by the Federation of American Motorcyclists. The event drew over 20,000 attendees and alternated between aeronautic demonstrations and races. Featured inventions included Kimball's 20-rotor helicopter, powered by leather belts and pulleys; the Rickman Helicopter, with 32 propellers operated by two men on a tandem bicycle; and C. W. Williams's monoplane. No heavier-than-air machines achieved sustained flight, but wind-powered gliders succeeded. One glider pilot, Lawrence Lesh, crashed after spectators encroached on the landing area, resulting in a broken ankle and extended hospital recovery. The event was depicted in Rudolph Dirks's painting *The Fledglings*, capturing the carnival-like atmosphere.

### 1909 Activities

In 1909, the aerodrome continued to host tests and exhibitions. An "inflatable bag" flying machine was trialed, along with airships such as the Riggs-Rice *American Eagle*. On June 16, 1909, Glenn H. Curtiss conducted the first flight of his biplane, later known as the *Golden Flyer* or *Gold Bug*, at the site. Ten days later, on June 26, he performed exhibition flights, including his first circle, before an estimated 2,000 to 5,000 spectators. These were the first airplane flights over New York City. The aircraft had been purchased by the society to promote aviation.

Additional events in 1909 included demonstrations possibly involving Stanley Yale Beach, an aviation promoter and editor associated with monoplane designs. A newspaper report from July 5, 1909, referenced an aerial event at the aerodrome, described as a significant occasion.

## Closure

A fire in April 1910 damaged much of the facilities, leading to the end of operations. The lease expired, and the property was auctioned in 1913 to real estate developers for residential use. The Aeronautic Society relocated to Mineola, Long Island, in 1910 and disbanded in 1918.

## Legacy

The aerodrome contributed to early aviation by providing a dedicated space for experiments and public engagement. Local historians have advocated for recognition of its role in Bronx aviation history, though no formal memorials exist as of 2025. Artifacts, such as event programs, are preserved in institutions like the Smithsonian.
