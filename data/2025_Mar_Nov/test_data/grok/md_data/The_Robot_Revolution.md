### Meteorological history  
The 2011 Super Outbreak developed under a highly unstable atmospheric environment driven by a large upper-level trough over the central United States, which progressed eastward. This system interacted with a surface low-pressure area that lifted through the Ohio Valley, drawing moist air from the Gulf of Mexico northward. Daytime temperatures reached 77 to 81 °F (25 to 27 °C), with dew points of 66 to 72 °F (19 to 22 °C), contributing to convective available potential energy (CAPE) values of 2,500–3,000 J/kg. Strong wind shear, with winds turning significantly with height, promoted the rotation necessary for supercell formation. These conditions led to 219 tornadoes on April 27 alone, surpassing the previous 24-hour record set in 1974. [4][5]  

The parent supercell for the Flat Rock–Trenton tornado originated in Mississippi and tracked northeastward. It produced the tornado at approximately 4:01 p.m. CDT (2101 UTC) about 3 miles (4.8 km) northeast of Section, Alabama, initially as an EF0 to EF1 with winds of 70–90 mph (110–145 km/h). Radar data from the National Weather Service (NWS) office in Hytop, Alabama (HTX), showed a multi-vortex structure as the tornado rapidly intensified while descending Sand Mountain into Jones Cove. It reached EF4 intensity with winds up to 190 mph (310 km/h) near Flat Rock, Alabama, before crossing into Georgia at 4:35 p.m. CDT, where it weakened to EF3 with winds around 150 mph (240 km/h). The tornado dissipated at 4:57 p.m. CDT (2157 UTC) about 0.9 miles (1.4 km) northwest of Fort Oglethorpe, Georgia, after ascending and briefly weakening over Lookout Mountain. The total path length was 46.98 miles (75.60 km), with a maximum width of 1,260 yards (1,150 m). [1][2][3][5]  

### Impact  
The tornado caused catastrophic damage along its path, destroying over 200 homes, numerous farms, and businesses while denuding vast forested areas. Damage assessments revealed high-end EF4 effects in Alabama, including debarked trees, scoured ground, and structures exploded or swept away. In Georgia, EF3 damage predominated, with similar but slightly less intense destruction.  

#### Alabama  
The tornado began in Jackson County, snapping and uprooting trees as it intensified. It struck Camp Jackson Scout Reservation, destroying buildings, vehicles, and campsites; cleanup involved removing over 250 loads of debris and replanting 1,000 trees years later. Nearby, Camp ToKnowHim lost RVs, a mobile home, and recreational facilities, though volunteers aided recovery. [3]  

Near County Roads 369 and 357, 13 dwellings were destroyed, including the first fatalities: Ann Satterfield (81) and Herbert Satterfield (90), whose frame house was swept away. A truck was blown 20 yards (18 m). Further along County Roads 364, 652, and 363, double-wide homes were obliterated, and a pickup truck was rolled 300 yards (275 m). Crossing Alabama Highway 117 and 71, it damaged residences and a strip mine. High-end EF4 damage occurred on County Road 95, where Katherine Whited (75), John Whited (77), Shelby Shannon (58), and Janey Shannon (80) died; survivor Ronnie Shannon was thrown hundreds of feet. [1][3][6]  

At the Perry farmstead, a well-built house was swept clean to the flooring, with all farm buildings and 40 cattle destroyed. Entering DeKalb County south of Higdon, it crushed transmission towers and razed neighborhoods on County Roads 960 and 155, killing Linda Boatner (67) and Chelsea Black (20). On County Roads 155 and 823, 11 homes were destroyed, killing Terry Tub Tinker (50) and Jewell Ewing (73). Near County Roads 155 and 814, 13 residences were obliterated, killing Martha Michaels (72) and William “Buddy” Michaels (70). In the Shiloh community, cinder-block homes and mobile homes were swept away, thousands of trees were sheared, a van was lofted 400 yards (365 m), and chicken barns collapsed with high livestock losses. Additional fatalities in Alabama included Kathy Gray Haney (46) in Pisgah. Overall, 12 direct deaths occurred in Alabama, with extensive agricultural losses. [1][3][6]  

#### Georgia  
Crossing into Dade County 4.5 miles (7.2 km) southwest of Trenton, the tornado razed two houses on County Road 267, one swept from its foundation. It damaged Sand Mountain Bible Camp, causing $2.3 million in losses. Entering southern Trenton around 4:40 p.m. CDT, it destroyed 21 residences near South Main Street and Walnut Avenue, including Village Green Apartments stripped to walls. Businesses like Moore Funeral Home collapsed (rebuilt by July 2012) and S&L Tans were damaged; survivors sheltered in a tanning bed. Fatalities included Donnie Walston (47) and Jerry Williams Sr. (49). Low-income housing was heavily impacted, with 70% of units affected and slow rebuilding. [2][3]  

Ascending Lookout Mountain (1,970 feet or 600 m elevation gain), it flattened trees in an 800-yard (730 m) swath. Regenerating after a brief weakening, it destroyed 14 houses on Mountain View and Eagle Landing Drives in Walker County, plus trailers and a home near State Highway 27. Two fatalities and 12 injuries were reported in Georgia, with tens of thousands of trees downed and 18 homes suffering major damage. [2][3]  

### Casualties  
The tornado resulted in 14 direct fatalities: 12 in Alabama and 2 in Georgia. Three indirect deaths occurred in Alabama—a fire killed Wayne White (68) and Judith White (63) in Ider, and stress caused Inez Bright's death. Injuries totaled at least 50, though estimates reached 125 including underreported cases. Many survivors sustained severe injuries, such as Glynis Lawson, who required multiple surgeries after being found alive in a body bag at a makeshift morgue. [3][6]

### Aftermath  
Recovery efforts involved local fire departments, the Red Cross, Salvation Army, and churches like Southwood Presbyterian. A long-term recovery committee in Jackson County raised funds and rebuilt over 160 homes, with the first completed by July 2011. In Trenton, community broadcasts disseminated aid information, and a benefit concert supported cleanup. Rebuilding low-income housing lagged, but stories of survival fostered unity, such as at Camp ToKnowHim where volunteers noted "blessings amid destruction." Total damages from this tornado were estimated at $55 million. [3][7]
