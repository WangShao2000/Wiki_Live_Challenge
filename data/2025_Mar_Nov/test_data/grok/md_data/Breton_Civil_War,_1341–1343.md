# Breton Civil War, 1341–1343

The **Breton Civil War of 1341–1343** was the opening phase of the longer War of the Breton Succession, a dynastic conflict in the Duchy of Brittany triggered by the death of Duke John III without a direct heir on 30 April 1341. The dispute centered on two rival claimants: John of Montfort, the late duke's half-brother, who argued for inheritance under Salic law excluding female lines, and Charles of Blois, husband of John III's niece Joan of Penthièvre, whose claim derived from her descent from the duke's elder brother. The war quickly drew in external powers, becoming a proxy struggle within the Hundred Years' War (1337–1453), with Montfort receiving support from King Edward III of England and Blois from King Philip VI of France. Notable for the active military roles played by noblewomen, including Joanna of Flanders (Montfort's wife) and Joan of Penthièvre, the conflict earned the informal name "War of the Two Joans." Key engagements included the Battle of Champtoceaux (October 1341), the Siege of Hennebont (May–June 1342), and the Battle of Morlaix (September 1342). This phase concluded with the Truce of Malestroit in January 1343, which permitted both sides to retain their territorial holdings and provided a temporary pause for papal-mediated peace negotiations, though low-level fighting persisted and the broader war resumed in 1345. The conflict underscored tensions over feudal succession, regional autonomy, and the strategic importance of Brittany's ports for Anglo-French rivalry.

## Background

The Duchy of Brittany, a semi-independent feudal territory under nominal French suzerainty, had long navigated complex inheritance customs blending French Salic law—which barred women from inheriting—and local traditions allowing female succession in certain cases.[1] Duke John III, who ruled from 1312, faced ongoing disputes over his succession due to the lack of surviving children from his marriages. He attempted to resolve this by designating Joan of Penthièvre as his heir in 1337, but later revoked this in favor of his half-brother John of Montfort, only to reverse again amid family pressures.[2] John III's death on 30 April 1341, during a period of heightened Anglo-French tensions in the Hundred Years' War, left the duchy without a clear successor, igniting immediate rival claims.[3]

John of Montfort based his claim on proximity of blood as the duke's half-brother, invoking Salic law to exclude Joan of Penthièvre's line.[1] In contrast, Charles of Blois, Joan's husband and nephew to Philip VI, asserted rights through her inheritance, supported by French royal interests in maintaining influence over Brittany.[4] The duchy's strategic location, with its Atlantic ports offering potential naval bases, made it a valuable prize; Edward III sought to secure it as a foothold against France, while Philip VI aimed to prevent English encroachment.[2] Initial appeals to the French Parlement in Paris favored Blois, prompting Montfort to seek English alliance.[3]

## Outbreak and Montfort's initial advances

Following John III's death, John of Montfort moved swiftly to consolidate control. In May 1341, he seized the ducal capital of Nantes and the treasury at Limoges, securing oaths of loyalty from local nobles and estates in eastern Brittany.[2] By July and August, Montfort had captured key strongholds including Rennes, Dinan, and Vannes, controlling much of the duchy.[3] He paid homage to Philip VI in an attempt to legitimize his position but simultaneously negotiated with Edward III, offering fealty to England in exchange for military aid.[1]

Philip VI, viewing Montfort's actions as treasonous, summoned him to Paris and declared Charles of Blois the rightful duke in September 1341.[4] French forces, bolstered by Genoese mercenaries and Breton allies, advanced under Blois to reclaim territory.[2] Montfort's rapid gains alarmed the French court, which feared English intervention could extend the Hundred Years' War into northwestern France.[3]

## French counteroffensive and capture of Montfort

The French response began in late September 1341, with Blois assembling an army of approximately 5,000 French soldiers, 2,000 Genoese mercenaries, and additional Breton troops at Angers.[5] Targeting Montfort's eastern holdings, Blois besieged Champtoceaux, a fortified castle guarding the Loire Valley, starting on 10 October.[3]

Montfort attempted to relieve the siege with a small force from Nantes but encountered Blois's vanguard at the farmstead of l'Humeau, three miles from Champtoceaux.[5] In the ensuing Battle of Champtoceaux (also known as the Battle of l'Humeau), fought over two days in mid-October, Montfort's outnumbered troops engaged in skirmishes but failed to break through.[3] French reinforcements arrived, forcing Montfort to retreat to Nantes.[5] Champtoceaux surrendered on 26 October after learning of Montfort's flight.[3]

Blois then besieged Nantes, encircling the city for three weeks amid supply shortages and internal discontent.[2] Montfort's sallies, including one at the end of October where his mercenaries deserted, weakened defenses.[5] On 2 November 1341, the city council compelled Montfort to surrender; he was imprisoned in the Louvre in Paris.[3] In the following months, French forces captured additional Montfortist holdings, though resistance persisted in western coastal areas under Joanna of Flanders, who assumed regency for their young son, John.[4]

## Montfortist resistance and English intervention

With Montfort imprisoned, Joanna of Flanders emerged as a key leader, rallying supporters from strongholds like Hennebont, Auray, and Brest.[3] She declared her son the nominal duke and appealed to Edward III for aid, framing the conflict as part of the broader Anglo-French struggle.[2]

In May 1342, Blois besieged Hennebont, a fortified port defended by about 500 Montfortists.[4] Joanna organized the defense, reportedly leading sorties and using fire ships to disrupt French vessels on the Blavet River.[6] She briefly escaped to Auray for reinforcements, returning after five days.[4] A small English force under Sir Walter Manny (234 men-at-arms) arrived in early June, conducting raids that forced Blois to lift the siege by late June.[2]

Further English reinforcements landed in July 1342, led by William de Bohun, Earl of Northampton, with 2,400 troops (half men-at-arms, half archers).[3] This relieved pressure on Brest and shifted momentum, allowing Montfortists to regain ground in western Brittany.[2]

## Battle of Morlaix

In September 1342, Blois returned with a large army—estimated at up to 30,000, including cavalry, Genoese crossbowmen, and Breton infantry—to relieve Morlaix, besieged by Northampton.[3] The English, numbering around 1,500–2,000, adopted a defensive position on a wooded rise, with dismounted men-at-arms in the center, archers on the flanks, and a hidden trench in front.[2]

The Battle of Morlaix unfolded in three waves: the first Breton infantry assault faltered under English arrow fire; the second mounted charge was disrupted by the trench and reserves; the third exhausted both sides, leading the English to retreat into the woods while inflicting heavy casualties.[3] Blois withdrew, marking an early demonstration of English tactics like the longbow and dismounted combat, which would prove effective in later Hundred Years' War battles.[2]

## Edward III's campaign

Edward III personally intervened in October 1342, landing with additional forces to bring English troops in Brittany to about 5,000.[3] His army captured southern and western territories through raids aimed at undermining French morale, though progress stalled at the Siege of Vannes in late 1342.[2] Facing a larger French army under Jean, Duke of Normandy, Edward risked encirclement.[3]

By December 1342, with supplies dwindling and winter approaching, negotiations began. The Truce of Malestroit was signed on 19 January 1343 at the Chapel of the Madeleine in Malestroit, allowing both sides to hold their current territories and calling for papal mediation at Avignon.[7] Provisions included the release of John of Montfort, though he was not freed until September 1343 under restrictive conditions and played no further active role.[3]

## Aftermath

The truce provided a respite but did not resolve underlying disputes, with sporadic skirmishes continuing in Brittany.[7] John of Montfort's release was short-lived; he fled to England in 1345 and died soon after.[2] The war resumed in earnest that year, culminating in Montfortist victory at the Battle of Auray in 1364 and the Treaty of Guérande in 1365, recognizing John V (Montfort's son) as duke.[1] The 1341–1343 phase highlighted Brittany's vulnerability to external powers and the effectiveness of English military tactics, influencing the course of the Hundred Years' War.
