# Red and Purple Modernization

The Red and Purple Modernization (RPM) is a multi-phase infrastructure program by the Chicago Transit Authority (CTA) to rebuild aging sections of the Red Line and Purple Line Express rail lines on Chicago's North Side. Research indicates that the project, addressing infrastructure built mostly between 1900 and 1924, seeks to enhance capacity, reliability, accessibility, and safety along a 9.6-mile corridor from Belmont station in Chicago to Linden station in Wilmette, Illinois. As of December 2025, Phase One has largely been completed, marking the largest capital investment in CTA history at approximately $2.1 billion, though some finishing work continues.

### Key Points
- **Scope and Purpose**: The program focuses on reconstructing tracks, stations, and signals to accommodate growing ridership, which exceeds 128,000 weekday trips on the corridor, representing about 19% of CTA rail usage.
- **Phase One Achievements**: This phase included a new flyover bypass north of Belmont station to reduce train conflicts, full reconstruction of four Red Line stations (Lawrence, Argyle, Berwyn, and Bryn Mawr) with ADA accessibility, and upgraded signals between Belmont and Howard stations.
- **Timeline Highlights**: Planning began with a 2009 vision study; environmental reviews concluded by 2015; construction started in 2019; major elements like the new stations opened in July 2025.
- **Funding and Challenges**: Primarily funded through federal grants, local bonds, and tax increment financing (TIF), the project faced a federal funding pause in October 2025 due to reviews of contracting practices, though its impact on RPM remains unclear given Phase One's near-completion.
- **Benefits and Concerns**: Evidence suggests improvements in travel times and capacity by up to 15%, supporting economic growth; however, construction caused temporary disruptions, and environmental assessments noted potential effects like noise and land acquisition, which were mitigated.

### Background
The corridor's infrastructure, dating to the early 20th century, had deteriorated, leading to frequent delays and maintenance issues. A 2009-2010 vision study identified modernization as the preferred approach to meet increasing demand in a transit-reliant area.

### Current Status
As of December 2025, Phase One construction is wrapping up, with ongoing activities such as rail grinding and fence installations. The project has improved service for riders in neighborhoods like Uptown and Edgewater.


The Red and Purple Modernization (RPM) is a multi-phase infrastructure program undertaken by the Chicago Transit Authority (CTA) to reconstruct and upgrade the northern segments of the Red Line and Purple Line Express rail services in Chicago, Illinois.[1] Initiated in response to the aging condition of tracks and stations constructed primarily between 1900 and 1924, the project encompasses a 9.6-mile corridor from Belmont station in Chicago to Linden station in Wilmette, serving over 128,000 weekday passengers, which accounts for approximately 19% of CTA's total rail ridership.[2][3] Phase One, valued at $2.1 billion and representing the largest capital investment in CTA history, was substantially completed by 2025 and featured the addition of a flyover bypass, reconstruction of four stations, and signal system upgrades to increase capacity by up to 15%.[4][5] The program has been supported by federal and local funding sources but encountered a funding review in 2025 amid concerns over contracting policies.[6] Planning for subsequent phases continues, with goals to further enhance service reliability, accessibility, and economic development along the corridor.[1]

The Red and Purple lines form a critical part of Chicago's elevated rail network, with the northern corridor originally built by the Northwestern Elevated Railroad Company between 1900 and 1924.[3][7] By the late 2000s, the infrastructure had exceeded its useful life, resulting in slow zones, frequent maintenance, and limited accessibility—only six of the 21 stations along the corridor were compliant with the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) prior to the project.[3][8] Ridership growth, driven by population increases in North Side neighborhoods and suburbs, exacerbated these issues, with peak-hour crowding rising by 40% in the five years leading up to the project's planning.[5]

In 2009, the CTA conducted a vision study for the 9.6-mile corridor between Belmont and Linden stations, evaluating options to address deterioration and capacity constraints.[5][9] This study, completed in 2010, recommended modernization as the locally preferred alternative, which was incorporated into the region's long-range transportation plan that year.[5] The corridor's high usage for work, education, and recreational trips, combined with its role in supporting reverse commutes and economic activity, underscored the need for upgrades.[3]

## Planning and Environmental Review
In January 2011, the FTA and CTA issued a notice of intent to prepare a Tier 1 Environmental Impact Statement (EIS) for the project, focusing on broad alternatives and potential impacts.[4][10] The EIS examined options ranging from no action (minimal repairs) to full modernization, including variations with four-track, three-track, or two-track underground configurations.[4] Key considerations included reducing travel times, improving ADA access, and supporting transit-oriented development while minimizing disruptions.[3][4]

Environmental issues assessed in the EIS included land acquisition, displacements, cultural resources, noise, vibration, air quality, and effects on parks and ecosystems.[3][4] Public scoping meetings in January 2011 gathered input, leading to a draft EIS in 2012 and a record of decision anticipated thereafter.[3] By 2015, the environmental review concluded with categorical exclusions for signal work and findings of no significant impact for the bypass and station reconstructions.[5] The process identified potential adverse effects, such as temporary noise during construction and property acquisitions, which were addressed through mitigation plans, including community engagement and environmental justice considerations for diverse neighborhoods.[11]

## Phases
The RPM program is structured in phases to manage costs and minimize service interruptions.[1][5] Phase One was prioritized for its focus on the most congested sections, with future phases in early planning as of 2025.[1]

### Phase One
Phase One targeted a 5.6-mile segment from Belmont to Howard stations, comprising three main components: the Red-Purple Bypass, the Lawrence to Bryn Mawr Modernization, and signal system upgrades.[2][5]

- **Red-Purple Bypass**: Constructed north of Belmont station, this grade-separated flyover allows northbound Brown Line trains to cross over Red and Purple Line tracks, eliminating a flat junction that caused delays for up to 15,000 daily riders.[9][12] The bypass, completed in 2021, improved operational efficiency and safety at Clark Junction, a bottleneck dating to 1907.[9]

- **Lawrence to Bryn Mawr Modernization**: This involved reconstructing 1.3 miles of track and four Red Line stations—Lawrence, Argyle, Berwyn, and Bryn Mawr—into modern, ADA-accessible facilities with wider platforms and elevators.[2][5] The stations reopened on July 20, 2025, following reconstruction that included replacing century-old embankments with concrete structures.[13] The project also added new tracks to support Purple Line Express service.

- **Signal Upgrades**: A new signal system was installed between Belmont and Howard, enabling more frequent and reliable trains.[2][5] Additional elements included power substation improvements and the purchase of 32 new railcars.[5]

Phase One aimed to bring the corridor into a state of good repair for 60 to 80 years, with capacity expansion meeting federal Core Capacity program requirements.[5]

## Funding
Funding for Phase One totaled $2.1 billion, sourced from federal grants (approximately $1.07 billion through the FTA's Core Capacity program), local sales tax bonds, a Tax Increment Financing (TIF) district generating $359 million, and other flexible funds.[5][10][14] The Red-Purple Modernization Phase One Project TIF, designated in 2016, covers 16,704 parcels and supports debt service for infrastructure improvements.[14] A Full Funding Grant Agreement was anticipated in 2016, with the project admitted to FTA's Core Capacity development in 2013.[5]

## Construction and Timeline
Construction on Phase One began in 2019, following preliminary engineering in the mid-2010s.[5][15] Major milestones included the bypass opening in 2021 and station reconstructions progressing through 2025.[9][13] As of December 2025, final activities such as rail grinding, fence installations, and utility relocations are ongoing, with most infrastructure in service.[1] The timeline extended beyond initial projections of 2023 due to complexities like community coordination and supply chain issues.[5]

## Impacts
The project has enhanced transit capacity by 15%, reduced crowding, and improved travel times, benefiting riders in transit-dependent areas.[2][5] Economic analyses project $4.4 billion in activity during construction, with long-term support for job access and development.[16] Community responses have been generally positive, with collaborations minimizing disruptions through business support programs and public space frameworks under elevated tracks.[1][17] Environmental mitigations addressed noise, vibration, and displacements, though some residents reported temporary inconveniences like alley closures.[1][11]

## Controversies
While largely supported, the project faced challenges related to construction impacts and funding. In October 2025, the U.S. Department of Transportation paused $2.1 billion in funding for Chicago projects, including RPM, pending a review of contracting practices amid concerns over diversity, equity, and inclusion requirements.[6][18][19] This action, affecting both RPM and the separate Red Line Extension, prompted responses from local officials emphasizing the projects' economic benefits.[16][20] Earlier debates during planning included alternatives that proposed station consolidations or underground alignments, which raised concerns about access but were not selected.[4]

## Future Phases
As of 2025, the CTA is conducting the RPM Next Phases Planning Study to evaluate extensions of modernization efforts, potentially including further track reconstructions and station upgrades north of Howard.[1] This multi-year process involves public engagement to define goals and alternatives, building on Phase One's framework.[1]
