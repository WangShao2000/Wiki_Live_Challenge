### History
Everyone Hates Elon originated in January 2025 from a group chat among a small circle of friends in the UK, frustrated by Elon Musk's increasing involvement in British politics.[1][2] Key triggers included Musk's X (formerly Twitter) posts calling for the release of far-right activist Stephen Yaxley-Lennon (known as Tommy Robinson), who was jailed for contempt of court after repeating false accusations against a Syrian refugee.[1] Musk also accused UK Prime Minister Keir Starmer of being "deeply complicit in the mass rapes in exchange for votes" regarding a historical child-grooming scandal and labeled minister Jess Phillips a "genocide rape apologist."[1] Further, Musk endorsed far-right parties in Germany and Britain, posted "Make Europe Great Again!," and suggested dissolving the UK Parliament for a new election.[1][2] A pivotal moment was Musk's hand gesture at Donald Trump's inauguration, interpreted by the group as resembling a Nazi salute, prompting them to brand Tesla vehicles as "Swasticars."[1]

The co-founders, including individuals identified only as Emma (in her early thirties, based in South London) and Jason, had prior experience in nonprofit campaigns for progressive causes.[2] They sought to move beyond traditional protests, which they viewed as ineffective and lacking appeal, toward humorous, memorable actions that could compete with far-right messaging.[2] Initial ideas focused on crowdfunding to donate small amounts per Musk tweet to opposed causes, evolving into physical stunts.[1][3] The group remains anonymous and decentralized, consisting of a handful of friends spread across the UK, with no formal hierarchy.[1] By mid-2025, their actions had expanded to include international support, with over half of merchandise orders coming from the US, Canada, and Australia.[1]

### Campaigns and actions
Everyone Hates Elon specializes in low-cost, high-impact guerrilla tactics designed to "piss off Elon Musk one small action at a time."[1][3] Their campaigns often target Tesla as a proxy for Musk's wealth and influence, aiming to link his political views to consumer boycotts.[2][3]

Early actions included plastering "Don’t Buy a Swasticar" stickers on hundreds of Tesla vehicles in London.[3][4] In one stunt, members posed as corporate representatives to place a life-size cardboard cutout of Musk performing the controversial salute in a North London Tesla showroom, filming customer reactions—including one telling them to "fuck off"—which went viral on Instagram with millions of views.[1][2] Another involved distributing custom air fresheners labeled "Musk B-Gone" and "Covers the stench of fascism" at a showroom, resulting in the group being escorted out.[2]

Poster campaigns have been central, featuring fake advertisements on bus stops, Tube stations, and billboards. Examples include:
- A poster showing Musk in a Tesla with the caption "Goes from 0 to 1939 in 3 seconds," alluding to the start of World War II.[1][2]
- Signs depicting Musk and Donald Trump with a Tesla, reading "Autopilot for your car. Autocrat for your country."[2]
- A medical-style flyer titled "Know Your Parasites," listing "Ticks," "Worms," and "Billionaires" (including Musk, Mark Zuckerberg, and Jeff Bezos), advocating for a wealth tax.[2]

These unauthorized posters led to interventions by Transport for London, which instructed their removal.[2] A TikTok video of installing a "Swasticar" ad garnered over 10 million views and 1.1 million likes.[1]

The group's most ambitious action was a "rage room" event in late 2025, where they acquired a Tesla (with the battery removed for recycling) and allowed around 100 participants, equipped with sledgehammers and hardhats, to smash it for 30 seconds each.[2] The event was described as "super cathartic," with the wreckage planned for auction to benefit food banks.[2] Other actions include parody ads highlighting Tesla's stock decline, such as "Hate Doesn’t Sell. Just Ask Tesla."[2]

The collective encourages public replication, providing downloadable PDFs of posters and stickers to empower "ordinary people" globally.[1][3] They emphasize safety and legality, using high-visibility vests to blend in during installations.[2]

### Fundraising
The collective's primary fundraiser, "People Versus Elon," launched in January 2025 via GoFundMe, allows donors to pledge as little as one penny per Musk X post (approximately 2,000 monthly), with caps to limit contributions (e.g., £5–£25 per month).[1][3][4] By December 2025, it had raised £48,248 toward a £50,000 goal.[4] Funds are allocated as follows: 63% for a core team of three to design and execute actions through May 2026; 32% for production of posters, ads, and videos; and 5% for travel, tools, and administration.[4] Donations support organizations Musk has criticized, including Everyday Racism, Rainbow Migration (for queer refugees), Women for Refugee Women, and Hope Not Hate (anti-fascism).[1] A separate one-time donation option exists, with 100% of proceeds funding campaigns.[3][4] The group positions this as turning Musk's online activity into "a weapon against him," emphasizing small donations for collective impact.[3]

### Reception
Everyone Hates Elon's campaigns have received mixed responses. Media coverage includes features in The Hollywood Reporter, The New Yorker, and The Guardian, framing the group as innovative activists countering billionaire influence with humor.[1][2] Endorsements came from figures like author Stephen King, who shared a poster on Bluesky, and comedians on podcasts such as The Guilty Feminist.[1] Viral success on TikTok and Instagram, with millions of views, indicates broad appeal, particularly among those disillusioned with Musk's politics.[1][2] A March 2025 YouGov poll showed 80% disapproval of Musk in the UK, including among Reform UK voters, suggesting the group amplifies existing sentiment.[2]

Critics argue the provocative language, such as "Swasticar," risks alienating audiences or trivializing historical references.[2] Tesla's European sales declines (amid a 71% profit drop) have been linked by the group to their efforts, though experts attribute this more to market competition and economic factors.[2][3] The collective has faced no reported legal actions, but stunts prompted removals by authorities and confrontations with customers.[1][2] Overall, the group is seen as part of a global pushback against figures like Musk, with supporters viewing it as empowering and detractors as niche or ineffective.[1]
