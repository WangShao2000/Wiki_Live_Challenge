The 2020 Jonesboro tornado was an EF3 tornado that struck the city of Jonesboro in Craighead County, Arkansas, United States, on March 28, 2020. The tornado caused significant damage along a 12.6-mile (20.3 km) path, affecting approximately 300 buildings, including commercial structures, residences, and infrastructure. It resulted in 22 injuries but no fatalities, a outcome attributed in part to reduced public gatherings due to the ongoing COVID-19 pandemic. The storm led to estimated damages in the hundreds of millions of dollars and prompted a federal major disaster declaration to support recovery efforts. As the strongest tornado in Arkansas since an EF4 event in 2014, it highlighted vulnerabilities in urban areas during concurrent public health crises.

**Meteorological history**

The tornado originated from a supercell thunderstorm that developed in eastern Arkansas on the afternoon of March 28, 2020, amid a broader weather pattern featuring mild temperatures and scattered showers across the state. Rotation was first detected in a storm near Tupelo in Jackson County, leading to the formation of an initial EF1 tornado with maximum winds of 110 mph (177 km/h) that traveled just over five miles (8 km), causing damage to homes, agricultural structures, and trees before dissipating near the Poinsett County line.

The supercell continued northeastward, with rotation temporarily weakening before intensifying again as it approached the south and east sides of Jonesboro shortly before 5:00 p.m. CDT. The resulting tornado touched down in southeast Jonesboro, reaching EF3 intensity with estimated peak winds of 140 mph (225 km/h). It maintained a maximum width of about 1,800 feet (550 m) and followed a northeast path through the city, before continuing into Brookland in Craighead County and areas just east of Paragould in Greene County. The tornado lifted northeast of Brookland after covering approximately 12.6 miles (20.3 km). A separate EF1 tornado was also confirmed in southeast Greene County during the same event.

**Warnings and preparations**

The National Weather Service (NWS) office in Little Rock issued a tornado warning for the initial storm near Tupelo at 3:59 p.m. CDT, based on radar-detected rotation. As the supercell progressed, additional warnings were extended to cover Jonesboro and surrounding areas. Local officials and media outlets disseminated alerts, urging residents to seek shelter. The timing coincided with statewide restrictions related to the COVID-19 pandemic, which had led to closures and reduced occupancy in public spaces such as shopping malls, potentially contributing to lower exposure risks.

**Impact**

The tornado inflicted EF3-level damage in Jonesboro, where it struck commercial and residential zones. Among the most severely affected areas was the Mall at Turtle Creek, a large shopping center, where portions collapsed, impacting stores including Best Buy and Barnes & Noble. Nearby retail buildings, such as Ulta and Kirkland's, experienced complete structural failure. The Jonesboro Municipal Airport sustained heavy damage, with hangars destroyed and aircraft displaced. A train was derailed north of the airport, and a wind gust of 74 mph (119 km/h) was recorded in the vicinity.

Residential damage included roofs removed from homes and widespread tree uprooting. Power outages affected over 2,200 customers due to downed poles and lines. In total, 22 people sustained injuries, primarily minor, with two requiring hospitalization; no deaths occurred. Damage extended to Brookland and near Paragould, though less intense there. Initial estimates placed public infrastructure damage at around $7.9 million, with overall costs projected in the hundreds of millions of dollars.

**Aftermath**

Immediate response involved search and rescue operations, which concluded without finding additional victims. Arkansas Governor Asa Hutchinson declared a state disaster for Craighead County on March 30, 2020, citing the combined effects of the tornado and the COVID-19 outbreak. A federal major disaster declaration was approved by President Donald Trump on May 10, 2020, enabling aid for recovery. Relief efforts included utility restoration and debris removal, supported by local agencies and volunteers.

In the years following, Jonesboro experienced redevelopment in commercial areas outside the damaged mall site, such as Uptown (formerly the Indian Mall property) and along Caraway Road. Sales tax revenues remained stable or increased, indicating economic resilience. However, the Mall at Turtle Creek's future remained uncertain as of 2023, with its private owners not announcing reconstruction plans, leading to calls for a new shopping center among residents. By 2025, reflections on the event emphasized the lack of fatalities as notable given the damage extent, though some residents reported heightened anxiety during subsequent storm seasons.


The 2020 Jonesboro tornado, occurring on March 28, 2020, in Craighead County, Arkansas, represented a significant severe weather event in the region's history, marked by its EF3 rating and the absence of fatalities despite substantial structural damage. This tornado emerged from a dynamic meteorological setup involving a supercell thunderstorm, which had earlier produced an EF1 tornado in Jackson County, illustrating the progressive intensification common in such systems during spring in the southern United States. The broader atmospheric conditions included mild air masses and instability that favored shower development, though precipitation was sporadic earlier in the day, allowing for the isolated supercell to form and sustain rotation.

Detailed radar analysis showed the initial rotation near Tupelo at around 3:59 p.m. CDT, prompting the first tornado warning, with ground reports confirming EF1 damage such as partial roof loss on residences, dismantled farm buildings, and uprooted trees over a path exceeding five miles. As the storm moved northeast, a brief lull in rotation preceded re-intensification, leading to touchdown in southeast Jonesboro where the tornado rapidly achieved EF3 strength with winds estimated at 136–140 mph (219–225 km/h). The path, measuring 12.6 miles in length and up to 1,800 feet in width, traversed urban and suburban zones, resulting in varied damage indicators that informed the NWS survey.

Warnings were extended promptly as the storm approached Jonesboro, with local emergency management coordinating with broadcasters to advise sheltering; the concurrent COVID-19 measures, including business closures, likely diminished potential casualties by limiting crowds in vulnerable locations like the mall. Impact assessments revealed approximately 300 affected structures, with key damage at the Mall at Turtle Creek—including collapse of anchor stores—and the municipal airport, where hangars were obliterated and planes relocated by winds. Other notable effects included a derailed freight train, a recorded 74 mph gust, and extensive utility disruptions. The 22 injuries were mostly minor, with two cases requiring extended medical care, and the zero-fatality count was described in reports as fortunate given the tornado's urban track.

Economic evaluations post-event indicated public damage at $7.9 million, encompassing $5.8 million in city assets, while broader private losses pushed totals into hundreds of millions, triggering state and federal declarations for aid. Recovery involved immediate debris clearance and power restoration, evolving into long-term redevelopment; by 2023, alternative commercial districts like Uptown and Caraway Road saw growth, offsetting the mall's stagnation, with sales taxes reflecting sustained economic activity. Five years on, community accounts noted persistent storm-related anxieties but overall resilience, with no major unresolved controversies surrounding the event's handling. The tornado remains a case study in how public health measures can intersect with natural disasters to influence outcomes.
