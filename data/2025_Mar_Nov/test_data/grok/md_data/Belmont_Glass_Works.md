## History

### Founding and early years

The Belmont Glass Works was organized in 1865 as Barnes, Faupel & Co. by experienced glassmakers, including George Barnes and Henry Faupel, who drew on expertise from prior work at Hobbs, Brockunier and Company in nearby Wheeling, West Virginia.[1][2] Production began in 1866 at a facility located at the corner of 34th and Hamilton streets in Bellaire, a riverfront village in Belmont County along the Ohio River.[3][4] The site's advantages included access to transportation via the river, the National Road, and two railroads, as well as proximity to coal from the Pittsburgh seam for fuel and a workforce familiar with glassmaking from the Wheeling area, where glass production had been active since the 1820s.[5][6]

The company was incorporated in 1868 as Belmont Glass Works, named after Belmont County.[7] Other key figures involved from the outset included glass-blowers Charles Henry Over, John Robinson, and brothers Henry Crimmel, John Crimmel, and Jacob Crimmel, who contributed to operations and innovations.[8][9] In its early phase, the firm functioned as a cooperative where laborers held ownership stakes, reflecting common practices in the emerging Ohio Valley glass industry.[10] By 1870, Belmont had established its own foundry and began producing molds not only for its own use but also for other companies, such as McKee & Brothers, Crystal Glass Company, Central Glass Company, Elson Glass Company, Gillinder Brothers, and Fostoria Glass.[11]

### Operations

Belmont Glass Works initially focused on blown glass products, particularly chimneys for lanterns and lamps, before transitioning to pressed glassware for kitchens, bars, and households.[12][13] The factory expanded rapidly, operating dozens of coal-fired furnaces at its peak and employing skilled workers in a labor-intensive process.[14] In 1872, Charles Henry Over, John Robinson, and Henry Faupel patented an improved seamless mold for lamp chimneys, enhancing production efficiency by eliminating seams that were common in earlier designs.[15]

By the mid-1880s, under manager Harvey Leighton (appointed in 1884), the company introduced colored glass production in 1885, including shades like canary yellow, which could develop an amethyst tint over time due to manganese in the glass mixture reacting to sunlight.[16][17] Pieces were typically unmarked, lacking logos or company insignia, which is common for pressed glass of the era.[18] The firm's output included affordable alternatives to cut glass, often used for special occasions, and its designs were pressed using iron molds that could take over a month to create and cost thousands of dollars.[19]

Belmont's operations contributed to the broader growth of the glass industry in Belmont County, which ranked sixth nationally in glass manufacturing by 1880.[20] Bellaire hosted more glass houses than any other city in the Upper Ohio Valley during this period, solidifying its "Glass City" status amid a regional boom driven by abundant natural resources and transportation networks.[21][22]

### Closure

Economic difficulties in the late 1880s, including rising fuel and labor costs, profit pressures, and an oversupply of glass in the market, led to the company's liquidation in July 1890.[23][24] The factory ceased operations that year, and its molds were sold to nearby firms such as Crystal Glass Company in Bridgeport, Ohio, and Central Glass Company.[25] The site was demolished by 1893 to make way for a new factory operated by Novelty Stamping Company.[26] Although some historical accounts suggest a possible connection to later firms like Rodefer Glass Works (which originated separately as National Glass Works in 1870), no direct operational link exists; any site reuse appears incidental.[27][28]

## Products

Belmont Glass Works produced a range of blown and pressed glass items, emphasizing functional and decorative pieces for domestic use. Early products included blown glass chimneys for lamps and lanterns, while later output focused on pressed tableware, bar goods such as tumblers and goblets, and lamps.[29][30] The company is particularly recognized for its intricate pressed patterns, which were affordable alternatives to more expensive cut glass and often featured in holiday or formal settings.[31]

Notable patterns included:

- **No. 100 (Daisy and Button)**: Introduced around 1886, this ornate design was produced in colors like canary yellow (vaseline glass) and is considered among the most elaborate Daisy and Button variants of the period. Items included covered compotes assembled from multiple pressed sections, with scalloped bases and finial lids.[32][33]

- **No. 99**: Used for items like berry bowls, featuring detailed motifs typical of Early American Pattern Glass (EAPG).[34]

- **Heart Stem**: An elaborate pattern seen in sets such as covered sugars, characterized by stemmed designs.[35]

- **No. 666**: Applied to cruets and condiment sets.[36]

- **Zipper (also known as Herringbone or Grasshopper without Insect)**: Intricate textured patterns collected for their complexity.[37]

Other patterns encompassed numbered lines (e.g., No. 4, No. 5 Paragon, No. 80 Polka Dot, No. 492) and named designs like Excelsior, Gem, Royal, and Peek-A-Boo, covering items from table sets and water sets to bitters bottles, casters, salts, and toothpicks.[38] Many pieces were constructed in sections due to technological limitations in pressed glass manufacturing, with molds chipped or shared across companies.[39]

Products were generally unmarked, and surviving examples are valued by collectors for their historical significance in American pattern glass.[40]

## Legacy

Belmont Glass Works influenced the American glass industry through its innovations, mold production, and the careers of its former employees. Individuals like Charles Henry Over and John Robinson left in 1876 to co-found Bellaire Goblet Company, while Henry Crimmel became a manager at Novelty Glass Company and later other firms in Ohio and Indiana.[41][42] The company's molds continued in use at successor firms, extending its design legacy.

Today, Belmont's glassware is featured in museums and collections, such as those at Iowa State University and the National Imperial Glass Museum in Bellaire, which preserves the broader history of the local glass industry.[43][44] The firm's role in Bellaire's "Glass City" era is commemorated in regional historical markers and publications, including a 2016 book by collector Sid Lethbridge that includes color photographs, historical details, and reproductions from an original Belmont catalog.[45]
