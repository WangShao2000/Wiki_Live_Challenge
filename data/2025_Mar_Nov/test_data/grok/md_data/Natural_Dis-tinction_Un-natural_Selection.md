### Background
Alexander McQueen, born Lee Alexander McQueen (1969–2010), was a prominent British fashion designer known for his provocative and innovative collections that often incorporated historical, cultural, and environmental references.[11][12] By 2008, McQueen had established his eponymous label under the Gucci Group (now Kering), achieving financial stability that allowed for creative experimentation while considering commercial potential.[12] The Spring/Summer 2009 collection followed a series of acclaimed shows, such as his Fall/Winter 2008 "The Girl Who Lived in the Tree," and reflected a period of reflection on global issues, coinciding with the approaching 150th anniversary of Darwin's *On the Origin of Species* in 2009.[11][13]

McQueen's work frequently addressed humanity's relationship with nature, and this collection built on that motif by juxtaposing untouched natural beauty with the distortions caused by industrial progress.[9][11][24] According to program notes and post-show statements, McQueen aimed to highlight the "danger of killing the planet through greed" without intending to deliver a didactic message.[11][12]

### Inspiration and Themes
The collection's title, *Natural Dis-tinction Un-natural Selection*, plays on Darwin's concept of natural selection, adapting it to critique how human industrialization has imposed an "unnatural" evolutionary path on the environment.[9][11][13][19] Inspirations included the survival of the fittest, the deleterious effects of the Industrial Revolution on the natural world, and broader environmental concerns.[9][11][24] McQueen divided the presentation into two distinct sequences to illustrate this duality: an initial exploration of a pristine, organic world untouched by humanity, followed by a depiction of modernity's synthetic and destructive interventions.[11][12]

Themes of biomimicry were prominent, with designs borrowing from natural defense mechanisms like camouflage patterns in animals and insects.[23] For instance, rupture patterns inspired by zebras and beetles, such as the resplendent beetle (*Cetonia dorata*), were used to create optical effects that blend the wearer into environments or dazzle observers through reflections.[23] The collection also incorporated semiotics from biology, using patterns to elevate fashion as a form of adaptive protection.[23] Overall, it presented a neutral examination of evolution's principles applied to human society, avoiding judgment while prompting reflection on ecological balance.[9][11][24]

### Design and Garments
The collection featured 44 looks, emphasizing innovative digital printing and engineering techniques where patterns were precisely mapped to garment shapes for multidimensional effects.[10][11] Materials ranged from natural fibers like silk and tulle in the first half to synthetics such as Lycra and bonded leather in the second, symbolizing the shift from organic to artificial.[11][12]

The first sequence focused on soft, romantic elements with organic shapes and a palette of soft colors, including taupes, browns, pinks, and yellows.[9][11][12][13] Key designs included:
- Dresses cut from a single piece of fabric with digital prints of wood grains, meadow flowers, and animal markings (e.g., tiger stripes or flamingo motifs).[11][12][13]
- Silk floral prints trapped under nude netting or tulle, evoking Victorian specimen jars.[9][11][12]
- Embroidered details with whitework, beetle-wing sequins, and metallic buttercups on mini dresses, paired with acid-yellow enamels.[11]
- Fringed garments in dégradé gray or pink filaments, draped across the body for fluid movement.[9]
- Tailored pieces like frock coats and skinny-leg pants with eased-up, fluid wood-print patterns.[9]

The second sequence introduced harder, angular aesthetics with a color palette of black, white, vivid pinks, sapphires, and metallic tones.[11][12][13] Designs here reflected human interference, including:
- Angular prints of crushed crystals, metallic structures (e.g., Eiffel Tower motifs), granite mountains, and diamond patterns morphing into human skeletons.[11][13]
- Bell-jar-shaped dresses and skintight bodysuits encrusted with jet, gold, and silver Swarovski crystals.[9][11][12][13]
- Geometric suits and dresses combining animal spines with stiff hourglass silhouettes.[13]
- Leather cocoon dresses with zip lines, grey python-print blazers, and crystal-encrusted jumpsuits resembling shimmering insects.[12][13]
- Multicolored allover prints on jackets, leggings, and cocoon dresses, incorporating lace underlayers with bird and flower motifs echoed in ankle wrappings.[9]

Accessories complemented the themes, with shoes molded to individual toes, nude stockings clinging to ankles, and lace ankle-wrappings.[13] Hair styling was flat against the face, secured by mesh masks for a streamlined, body-conforming look.[13]

### Presentation
The runway show took place at Le 104, a venue at 5 Rue Curial in Paris that was formerly a morgue, enhancing the themes of life, death, and evolution.[11][14] The set featured a catwalk surrounded by antique taxidermy animals, including an elephant, giraffe, tiger, zebra, polar bear, peacocks, and lions, symbolizing endangered species and human impact.[9][11][12][13][19] At the back, a metal plinth held a revolving globe projecting images of a glowing sun, silver moon, and rotating Earth, underscoring planetary concerns.[11][13]

Models emerged through the animal-lined runway, with music shifting from steel drums and tribal chants in the first half to evoke a jungle atmosphere, to darker tones in the second.[12] The invitation was a lenticular card designed by Gary James McQueen, showing the designer's face morphing into a human skull, foreshadowing mortality themes.[11][12] At the show's conclusion, McQueen appeared on the runway in a large bunny costume, waving to the audience—a playful contrast to the collection's serious undertones.[13]

Video footage of the full show is available online, capturing the progression from light, easy fabrics to structured, jewel-encrusted pieces.[3][16]

### Reception
Contemporary reviews highlighted the collection's unexpected environmental message, delivered without aggression or preachiness.[9] Critics noted its commercial potential, praising the vibrant colors, intricate details, and relaxed tailoring as a departure from McQueen's more provocative earlier works, potentially making it one of his most sellable lines.[9][12] The innovative digital prints and fluid shapes were lauded for their couture sensibilities, with close-up details in lace and fringing described as "sublime" and "breathtaking."[9] While some saw it as restrained compared to past spectacles, it was appreciated for its technical precision and coherence.[12]

The collection avoided contentious labels, presenting environmental themes neutrally through design rather than explicit statements.[9][11] McQueen himself emphasized post-show that he "doesn't want to preach" about the subject, focusing instead on artistic expression.[9][11]

### Legacy
*Natural Dis-tinction Un-natural Selection* has endured as a key example of McQueen's engagement with natural sciences and ecology in fashion.[24] It was prominently featured in the 2011 "Alexander McQueen: Savage Beauty" exhibition at the Metropolitan Museum of Art and later at the Victoria and Albert Museum (V&A) in 2015, where it was analyzed in the "Encyclopedia of Collections" for its thematic depth.[11][14] The Los Angeles County Museum of Art (LACMA) highlighted its pioneering digital print exploration in guides to McQueen's work.[10][15]

In the 2017 "Force of Nature" exhibition at the Fashion Institute of Technology (FIT), the collection was contextualized within historical fashion's relationship to natural principles, emphasizing its commentary on evolution and industrialization's harms.[24][22] Pieces from the collection continue to appear in retrospectives, with details shared on platforms like Instagram as recently as 2025, underscoring its lasting influence on discussions of biomimicry and sustainable design in fashion.[18][23] The collection's balanced portrayal of natural and unnatural forces has been cited in analyses of how fashion can inform without bias, maintaining relevance in ongoing environmental debates.[11][23][24]
