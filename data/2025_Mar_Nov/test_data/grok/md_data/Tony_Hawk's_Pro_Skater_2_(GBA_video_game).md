## Development

Vicarious Visions handled the development of the Game Boy Advance version, adapting the gameplay from the original console release to suit the handheld's hardware limitations. The game uses a polygonal engine for the skater models to allow for fluid animations, while the environments are pre-rendered backgrounds to maintain visual detail within the system's constraints.[1][2] This approach enabled multi-tiered levels with scrolling perspectives, though it resulted in a fixed isometric view that differs from the 3D freedom of the original.[3]

The soundtrack consists of original compositions by Shin'en Multimedia, replacing the licensed music from the console versions due to technical and licensing considerations.[4] Sound effects were carried over directly from the original game.[5] Hidden credits in the game's code reference the GAX Sound Engine version 1.99d, dated March 30, 2001, and list tracks composed by Manfred Linzner.[6]

The Japanese release, titled SK8: Tony Hawk no Pro Skater 2 and published by Success, includes modifications such as three exclusive pro skaters—Shin Okada, Junnosuke Yonesaka, and Shinnosuke Yonesaka—replacing Andrew Reynolds, Geoff Rowley, and Jamie Thomas, respectively. These replacements maintain identical stats and movesets.[7] Certain board designs were altered, including Steve Caballero's, to avoid potential licensing issues.[8] Menu text was simplified, and parts of the tutorial and pre-session screens were translated into Japanese.[9]

Unused content discovered in the game includes a level select cheat code, accessible by pausing and inputting a specific button sequence while holding the right shoulder button.[10] Additional unused text strings, such as "test gap string," "dummy," and "Unknown trick," are present in the code.[11]

## Gameplay

Players control one of 13 professional skateboarders, including Tony Hawk, Bob Burnquist, Steve Caballero, Kareem Campbell, Rune Glifberg, Eric Koston, Bucky Lasek, Rodney Mullen, Chad Muska, Elissa Steamer, and Jamie Thomas, among others.[12] In the Japanese version, three skaters are replaced with local professionals.[13] Each skater has customizable stats that can be upgraded using in-game currency earned from completing objectives.[14]

The game retains core modes from the original: Career Mode, where players progress through levels by achieving goals; Single Session, for timed high-score attempts; and Free Skate, for unrestricted practice.[15] A tutorial mode introduces basic controls and allows retrying specific sections.[16] Objectives in Career Mode include achieving high scores, collecting items like letters spelling "SKATE," finding hidden tapes, and performing specific tricks or destructions within time limits.[17] Completing these unlocks new levels and upgrades.

Controls utilize the Game Boy Advance's buttons: the A button for ollies (jumps), B for grabs, L for spins, and R for switching stance or manuals.[18] Tricks encompass flips (up to 10 per skater), grabs (up to 8 or 10), lips (4), and specials (6), with combos extended through manuals, grinds, and balances.[19] A special meter fills as tricks are performed, enabling powerful special moves when full.[20]

Levels consist of six skateparks: five adapted from the console version (Hangar, School II, Marseille, New York, and Venice Beach) with modifications for the isometric perspective, and one returning from the first Tony Hawk's Pro Skater (Warehouse).[21] Environments feature rails, ramps, quarterpipes, and interactive objects, but omit moving elements like vehicles from the originals.[22] The fixed isometric view can make judging distances and alignments challenging, particularly for airborne items or precise grinds.[23]

A "Kid Mode" option provides enhanced balance for beginners, reducing the difficulty of maintaining tricks.[24] The game supports saving progress but lacks multiplayer features and creation tools like park or skater editors present in console versions.[25]

## Release

Tony Hawk's Pro Skater 2 launched for the Game Boy Advance in North America on June 11, 2001, coinciding with the console's debut in the region.[26] It released in Europe on June 22, 2001, and in Japan on December 14, 2001.[27] The Japanese version includes regional adjustments and was published by Success.[28]

## Reception

The game was well-received by critics, who highlighted its successful adaptation of the series to a handheld format. On Metacritic, it holds an aggregate score of 95 out of 100, ranking it among the highest-rated Game Boy Advance titles.[29]

GameSpot awarded it 9.3 out of 10, describing it as a technical achievement that captures the essence of the console versions, with strong controls and modes, though noting frustrations from the isometric view in certain objectives.[30] IGN gave it 9.5 out of 10, calling it a "technical marvel" with addictive gameplay and impressive visuals, but criticizing the absence of multiplayer and a steeper learning curve due to the perspective.[31] Reviewers praised the sound design, with accurate effects and fitting original music, while acknowledging the lack of licensed tracks.[32]

Critics noted the game's suitability for portable play, with short sessions ideal for on-the-go gaming, and its overall faithfulness to the series despite hardware constraints.[33]
