# Archaeology of North Korea

The archaeology of North Korea encompasses the study of human activity on the northern portion of the Korean Peninsula from the Paleolithic era to historical periods, revealing evidence of early hominin presence, sedentary settlements, ancient kingdoms, and dynastic capitals. Archaeological research in the Democratic People's Republic of Korea (DPRK) began in earnest after 1945, shaped by state institutions and influenced by nationalist ideologies that emphasize indigenous Korean origins and cultural independence. Key sites include Paleolithic caves, Neolithic dwellings, Bronze Age dolmens, the tombs and cities of the Koguryo kingdom, and the palace complexes of the Koryo dynasty, two of which are UNESCO World Heritage Sites. While excavations have uncovered significant artifacts and structures, the field faces challenges from political isolation, limited international collaboration, and ideological interpretations that sometimes prioritize national narratives over empirical evidence. Despite these factors, findings contribute to understanding East Asian prehistory and the development of Korean civilization.

## History of Archaeological Research

Archaeological investigations in North Korea developed in the post-World War II period, building on limited surveys conducted during the Japanese colonial era (1910–1945). [1] Following the establishment of the DPRK in 1948, early efforts focused on protecting cultural relics through laws enacted in 1946 and 1948, with initial excavations concentrated around Pyongyang and the Yalu River basin. [3] The Academy of Sciences, founded in 1952, played a central role, establishing the Institute of Archaeology in 1955 to oversee systematic digs. [1] Research was initially influenced by Soviet models and Marxist interpretations, but by the late 1950s, it shifted toward Juche ideology, emphasizing self-reliance and linking archaeological findings to Korean ethnic homogeneity. [2] [11]

In the 1960s and 1970s, directives from leaders like Kim Il-sung and Kim Jong-il guided excavations to support nationalist claims, such as reconstructing tombs attributed to legendary figures like Dangun, the mythical founder of Gojoseon. [1] [2] Peaks in activity occurred in the 1960s and 1980s, with publications highlighting sites tied to ancient kingdoms like Koguryo and Goryeo. [1] However, economic difficulties in the 1990s reduced fieldwork, and research became more isolated, often prioritizing ideological alignment over scientific rigor. [1] [9] North Korean archaeology differs from South Korean approaches by being more hypothesis-driven and focused on proving indigenous development, sometimes leading to claims that diverge from international consensus, such as denying significant foreign influences. [1] [11]

International cooperation has been sporadic, with notable joint projects including excavations at Manwoldae palace with South Korea starting in 2007 [6] [12] and collaborations with French researchers in Kaesong. [3] These efforts have facilitated access to advanced technologies but are often interrupted by geopolitical tensions. [6] [9] As of 2025, recent discoveries, such as a Goryeo-era Buddha statue, continue to emerge through state-led initiatives. [10]

## Paleolithic Period

Evidence of early human occupation in North Korea dates to the Pleistocene, with sites suggesting hominin presence from over a million years ago. [2] North Korean archaeologists claim independent evolution of Homo species on the peninsula, including Homo erectus (termed Wonin), archaic Homo sapiens (Goin), and modern Homo sapiens (Shinin), without external migrations. [2] Key sites include Komunmoru Cave near Pyongyang, attributed to the Upper Paleolithic [4], and Ryonggok Cave, which yielded fossils from at least five archaic Homo sapiens individuals dated to 49,000–71,000 years ago, showing a mix of archaic and modern traits. [7] [8]

Other notable locations are Hangmok-ri Cave in Gangdong County, excavated in 2020, with layers dated 34,000–36,000 years old containing stone tools and faunal remains [2], and Soongnisan Cave, associated with hominin fossils and artifacts. [8] These findings are interpreted domestically as the cradle of the Korean ethnic group (Joseon Minjok), linking Paleolithic inhabitants directly to modern Koreans. [2] International scholars note the need for better dating and contextual analysis, as some claims emphasize nationalism over taphonomic studies of hominin-carnivore interactions. [7] [8]

## Neolithic Period

The Neolithic era in North Korea, corresponding to the Chulmun period (circa 8000–1500 BCE), marks the transition to sedentary lifestyles with pottery, ground stone tools, and early agriculture. Settlements were primarily along rivers and coasts, with major excavations in Pyongyang revealing dwellings and artifacts. [1] Sites like those in the Taedong River basin feature comb-pattern pottery and evidence of fishing, hunting, and millet cultivation. [1]

North Korean research posits a "Daedong River culture" centered on Pyongyang as a foundational Neolithic hub, rivaling ancient civilizations like Mesopotamia in indigenous development. [1] However, limited publications and access restrict detailed comparisons with southern Korean sites. [9]

## Bronze Age and Gojoseon

Bronze Age remains (circa 1500–300 BCE) include dolmens, dagger-shaped artifacts, and early state formations associated with Gojoseon, Korea's first kingdom according to tradition (founded 2333 BCE). [1] [11] Excavations near Pyongyang claim to link these to Dangun's era, with reconstructed tombs like that of Dangun (dated to around 3000 BCE with bone fragments) supporting narratives of ethnic continuity. [1] [2] Artifacts such as bipa-shaped daggers are interpreted as evidence of advanced metallurgy without foreign diffusion. [1]

North Korean historiography extends Gojoseon's territory broadly, emphasizing its role as a precursor to later states, though some claims, like the denial of Chinese influences, are contested internationally. [1] [11]

## Three Kingdoms Period: Focus on Koguryo

The Three Kingdoms period (1st century BCE–7th century CE) in North Korea is dominated by Koguryo (37 BCE–668 CE), known for its mountain cities and elaborate tombs. The UNESCO-listed Capital Cities and Tombs of the Ancient Koguryo Kingdom include Wunu Mountain City (built 37 BCE), Guonei City, and Wandu Mountain City (built 209 CE), featuring palaces, walls, and 40 tombs. [4] Imperial tombs are stepped pyramids of stone, while noble tombs have earthen mounds with wall paintings depicting daily life, hunting, and mythical figures. [4] A stele from 414 CE recounts the kingdom's founding. [4]

North Korean interpretations portray Koguryo as a powerful empire embodying Korean resilience, with excavations in the 1970s uncovering sites like Daeseongsan Fortress and mural tombs to affirm territorial claims. [11] However, some attributions, such as reassigning Chinese-origin tombs to Koguryo, reflect ideological priorities. [1] [11]

## Goryeo Period

The Goryeo dynasty (918–1392) left significant remains in Kaesong, its capital, including the UNESCO-listed Historic Monuments and Sites. [5] This ensemble comprises palace ruins like Manwoldae (built 919, spanning over six acres with asymmetric designs integrating geomancy), city walls, gates like Namdaemun, educational institutions such as Songgyungwan, and royal tombs. [5] [6] Excavations at Manwoldae, a joint North-South project since 2007, have revealed over 7,500 artifacts, including celadon vases, roof tiles with dragon motifs, and Sanskrit-inscribed items reflecting Buddhist influences. [6] [12]

A recent 2025 discovery in Sukchon County uncovered a 1.7-meter Amitābha Buddha statue from the early 10th century, highlighting Goryeo's Buddhist heritage and craftsmanship. [10] Sites illustrate the shift from Buddhism to neo-Confucianism. [5]

## Challenges and International Cooperation

Archaeological work in North Korea faces significant hurdles, including political isolation, surveillance, restricted access for foreigners, and funding shortages, leading to uneven publication and potential biases in interpretations. [1] [9] Fieldwork is often limited to approved sites, with data influenced by state ideology. [9] [11]

Despite this, international efforts have grown, such as the Manwoldae excavations with South Korea [6] [12] and French missions in Kaesong. [3] UNESCO designations in 2004 and 2013 for Koguryo and Kaesong sites have encouraged preservation. [4] [5] These collaborations provide technological support but are vulnerable to geopolitical shifts. [9]

The archaeology of North Korea, conducted primarily by state institutions like the Academy of Social Sciences' Institute of Archaeology, has evolved under the influence of Juche ideology since the 1960s, prioritizing narratives of ethnic homogeneity and indigenous development to bolster national identity. [1] [2] [11] Early post-1945 efforts, shaped by Soviet models, transitioned to leader-directed excavations that align findings with political goals, such as proving Pyongyang's centrality in Korean history. [1] This approach contrasts with more evidence-based methods in South Korea, often leading to claims like the denial of Chinese cultural impacts on sites such as the Lelang Commandery or Anak Tomb No. 3, which are reattributed to purely Korean origins despite scholarly debates. [1] [11]

In the Paleolithic period, North Korean sites contribute to regional anthropology, with fossils from Ryonggok Cave showing mosaic traits that could inform models of human evolution in East Asia, though interpretations emphasizing no external migrations remain contentious. [7] [8] The Hangmok-ri excavation exemplifies how stratigraphy is used to assert unbroken lineage from 36,000 years ago to modern Koreans, tying into broader claims of a million-year hominin presence without diffusion. [2] Challenges in taphonomy and dating persist, as open-air sites like those in the Imjin-Hantan River Basins lack direct hominin remains, relying on artifacts for inferences. [8]

Neolithic archaeology reveals sedentary communities along the Daedong (Taedong) River, with pottery and tools indicating local adaptations rather than wholesale imports, though limited comparative studies due to isolation hinder full understanding. [1] Bronze Age evidence, including dolmens and the reconstructed Dangun Tomb (dated to circa 3000 BCE with controversial bone analysis), supports Gojoseon as a foundational state, but attributions extend territories unrealistically in nationalist historiography. [1] [2]

For the Koguryo kingdom, the UNESCO site's components—such as Guonei City's stone walls and Wandu Mountain City's integration with natural defenses—demonstrate innovative urban planning that influenced later East Asian constructions. [4] Wall paintings in noble tombs, depicting daily scenes and mythical elements, reflect cultural syncretism with Chinese influences, though North Korean sources minimize this to emphasize Korean ingenuity. [4] [11] Excavations in the 1970s, including the Tomb of King Dongmyeong (relocated claims from China), were ideologically driven, with reconstructions lacking full authenticity. [11]

Goryeo sites in Kaesong, including the triple-walled defense system and Manwoldae's organic layout, highlight geomantic principles and Buddhist-Confucian transitions. [5] [6] The 2025 Amitābha statue discovery, with its serene features and absence of typical bodhisattvas, offers insights into early dynasty artistry, potentially linked to ritual practices. [10] Joint digs with South Korea at Manwoldae, uncovering Sanskrit tiles and ritual cups, underscore shared heritage but face interruptions from relations. [6] [12]

Overall challenges include surveillance limiting critical analysis, disinformation in sources, and focus on visible regimes, making fieldwork a negotiated process. [9] International ties, like French collaborations and UNESCO efforts, provide balance but are constrained. [3] [9] While North Korean archaeology advances knowledge of East Asian prehistory, its ideological framework invites scrutiny, with calls for more open data sharing to resolve controversies like Goguryeo's ethnic affiliations amid disputes with China. [1] [11]
