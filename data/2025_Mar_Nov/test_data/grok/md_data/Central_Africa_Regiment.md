### Formation
The origins of the Central Africa Regiment trace back to the early 1890s, when British forces in the Central Africa Protectorate sought to establish a local military presence to combat Arab slave traders and enforce colonial administration. Initial efforts began in May 1891 with the recruitment of irregular troops, including Zanzibari and Makua auxiliaries, to support campaigns against slavers. By 1895, the force included approximately 200 Sikhs from India, 100 Makua regulars, and 200 local irregulars, drawn from ethnic groups such as the Atonga, Yao, and Marimba. 

In 1896, following the conclusion of the Slavers' War (1888–1896), these elements were formalized into the British Central Africa Rifles, comprising six rifle companies of African soldiers, each led by a British officer and assisted by Sikh sergeants. The Sikhs, numbering around 185 initially, served as instructors, artillery crew, and post commanders, while the African companies emphasized recruitment from groups perceived as martial, such as the Yao (noted for self-reliance and marksmanship) and Tonga (often linked to missionary education). Recruitment methods included voluntary enlistment, chiefly quotas, and occasional press-ganging, with economic incentives like pay (initially 2–6 shillings per month, rising to 5 shillings in 1897) and hut tax exemptions playing key roles.

The unit was retitled the 1st Battalion Central Africa Regiment in December 1898, with a 2nd Battalion formed on 1 January 1899 to prepare for potential overseas service amid tensions in South Africa. By 1900, the regiment had adopted its official name, the Central Africa Regiment, and maintained a structure of ethnically based companies—Yao dominant in the 2nd Battalion, with mixtures of Tonga, Ngoni, Nyanja, and others in the 1st. Officers were typically seconded from the British Army, with attachments lasting 1–3 years, and few learned local languages like Chinyanja proficiently. African non-commissioned officers (ANCOs) emerged from 1900, replacing some Sikh roles after incidents like a 1899 riot in Mauritius.

Facilities included mud huts in segregated lines, with provisions for families (up to 95% of soldiers married by the 1930s), hospitals, schools, and recreational activities like ngomas (traditional dances). Rations consisted of salt, rice, or maize, supplemented by gardens, and conditions improved over time with better pay and gratuities for long service.

### Campaigns and Operations
The Central Africa Regiment engaged in various internal and external operations, primarily focused on pacification and enforcement of colonial rule.

#### Internal Pacification in Central Africa
From its inception, the regiment participated in expeditions to suppress slave trading and local resistance in Nyasaland. Between 1891 and 1914, units conducted patrols, tax enforcement, and punitive actions, such as burning villages in response to rebellions like those led by Zarafi in 1895 and M'Quamba in 1899, where looting included thousands of livestock. Garrisons were maintained at forts like Lister and Mangoche, with spatial segregation reflecting colonial hierarchies.

A notable internal campaign was the 1898 Angoniland Rebellion in Northern Rhodesia (now Zambia), where the regiment suppressed resistance from the Angoni people, a Zulu-descended group with up to 25,000 warriors. Triggered by land disputes involving the North Charterland Exploration Company, the conflict saw CAR forces, under Captain H.E.J. Brake, march from Zomba to Fort Jameson in January 1898. Equipped with Maxim guns and 7-pounder artillery, they relieved a besieged garrison at Luangweni, repelling Angoni advances through rifle volleys and bayonet charges. Key events included the capture and execution of Angoni leader Singu on 5 February 1898, and the surrender of Chief Mpeseni on 9 February. Minor skirmishes continued into April, with punitive expeditions in November 1900 destroying over 300 villages and capturing Chief Tambala. Participants received the Central Africa Medal with the "Central Africa 1894-1898" clasp, and later the Africa General Service Medal with "B.C.A. 1899-1900" clasp.

#### External Deployments
In 1899, the 2nd Battalion was deployed to Mauritius for garrison duty but was withdrawn after a riot involving attacks on local villages, stemming from abuses and leading to only one prosecution. More successful was the 1900 Ashanti Campaign in West Africa, where a detachment of Sikhs and CAR soldiers served against the Ashanti rebellion. Noted for discipline, the unit earned commendations, with gallantry awards like the Distinguished Conduct Medal to individuals such as Sergeant Adada and Corporal Chitenga. This campaign involved press-ganged recruits and was described in sources as one where the CAR performed as the "best corps."

The regiment also contributed to Somaliland campaigns in 1899 and 1903, with 124 soldiers fighting against forces estimated at 10,000, earning further Distinguished Conduct Medals. Battle honors like "Ashanti 1900" were later carried over to KAR colors.

### Transition and Legacy
On 1 January 1902, under War Office direction, the Central Africa Regiment was amalgamated with other colonial units—the East Africa Rifles and Uganda Rifles— to form the King's African Rifles. The 1st and 2nd Battalions became the 1st and 2nd (Central Africa) Battalions of the KAR, recruited mainly from Nyasaland and retaining ethnic compositions like Yao dominance. The 2nd Battalion was disbanded in 1911, with some soldiers transferred to German forces in Tanganyika, but reformed during World War I. The KAR expanded significantly in both world wars, with Nyasaland providing over 30,000 soldiers in World War II alone, serving in East Africa, Madagascar, Burma, and elsewhere.

Post-1902, the legacy of the CAR influenced KAR structures, including tribal-based units and long-service traditions, with high retention rates (low desertions in the 1920s–1930s) and economic benefits for veterans, such as police preferences and gratuities. By the 1950s, under the Federation of Rhodesia and Nyasaland, the battalions integrated with other regional forces, leading to Africanisation by independence in 1964, when the 1st Battalion became the Malawi Rifles. Historical assessments note the regiment's effectiveness in colonial consolidation but also highlight coercive elements in recruitment and operations, contributing to broader discussions on imperial military history in Africa.
