## Meteorological history
The tornado developed during a multi-day severe weather outbreak that affected the central United States from May 15 to 17, 2025. A strong low-pressure system over west-central Minnesota drove a cold front eastward, interacting with a humid air mass where dew points reached near 70°F (21°C). This setup created high atmospheric instability, with effective bulk shear values of 45 to 60 knots and low convective inhibition, fostering conditions for supercell thunderstorms.[1] The Storm Prediction Center's mesoscale analysis highlighted a broad area of elevated Significant Tornado Parameter values, particularly over south-central Kentucky between 10:00 p.m. and midnight EDT on May 16.[1]

The parent supercell formed in southeastern Missouri around 4:30 p.m. CDT, producing intermittent tornadoes as it tracked eastward across southern Illinois, Indiana, and western Kentucky.[2][3] By evening, it entered Kentucky, spawning an EF1 tornado near Lake Barkley at 6:54 p.m. CDT and an EF2 in Todd County at 7:42 p.m.[2] The supercell intensified as it approached Russell County, where the Somerset–London tornado touched down at approximately 10:27 p.m. EDT, about 4 miles (6.4 km) southeast of Russell Springs.[1][2] Initially narrow with EF2 to low-end EF3 intensity, it strengthened and widened as it progressed east-northeastward.[1]

Radar data showed strong rotation and debris lofted to at least 27,000 feet (8,200 m).[1] The tornado reached its peak EF4 strength with winds of 170 mph (270 km/h) near Mount Victory in Pulaski County, maintaining a width of up to 1,700 yards (1,600 m).[1][4] It crossed the Daniel Boone National Forest and the Rockcastle River, narrowing slightly before impacting London in Laurel County around 11:35 p.m.[1][2] The tornado lifted about 6 miles (9.7 km) southeast of London at approximately 11:56 p.m., after lasting 89 minutes.[2] The parent supercell continued eastward, causing additional wind damage in Clay, Leslie, Perry, and Letcher counties before weakening.[1]

This event was part of a broader outbreak that produced at least 10 tornadoes across the region, including an EF4 south of Marion, Illinois, an EF3 north of Sikeston, Missouri (with 2 fatalities), and an EF3 south of Morganfield, Kentucky.[3] Accompanying hazards included large hail up to baseball size and straight-line winds of 60–90 mph (97–145 km/h).[1][3]

## Impact
The tornado caused widespread destruction across three counties, affecting residential areas, commercial structures, infrastructure, and natural landscapes. Damage surveys by the National Weather Service (NWS) documented EF4-level impacts, including homes swept from foundations, vehicles thrown, and extensive tree damage.[1][4] Power outages affected nearly 200,000 customers in Kentucky, with some areas experiencing prolonged disruptions.[5] More than 24 state highways were closed due to debris, flooding, and downed lines.[6]

### Russell County
The tornado began in rural southeast Russell County, causing EF2 to EF3 damage to farms and outbuildings. Barns and camper trailers were destroyed, and about 50 homes sustained varying levels of damage.[2] High-tension power line towers were twisted and displaced up to 250 yards (230 m).[1] No immediate fatalities occurred here, though one later death was attributed to carbon monoxide poisoning from a generator used post-storm.[2]

### Pulaski County and Somerset
As the tornado entered Pulaski County, it impacted areas near Faubush, Nancy, and the southern edge of Somerset. Residential homes and the Southern Kentucky RECC headquarters suffered considerable damage, with roofs removed and walls collapsed.[1][6] At least 20 businesses in Somerset, including a cinema and manufacturing facility, were damaged or destroyed.[2] The tornado reached EF4 intensity near Mount Victory, where a farmhouse and outbuildings were leveled.[1] One fatality occurred in the county.[7]

### Laurel County and London
The most severe damage and highest casualties were in Laurel County, particularly south and west of London. The tornado crossed the Daniel Boone National Forest, uprooting and debarking thousands of trees across nearly 30,000 acres (12,000 ha) and 47 miles (76 km) of roads, creating a visible scar up to a mile wide observable on Landsat 9 satellite imagery acquired on June 4, 2025.[1][8] In the Sunshine Hills subdivision southwest of London, entire neighborhoods were flattened, with homes swept clean from concrete slabs, porches displaced, and debris such as lumber impaled into structures.[1] A church was reduced to concrete walls, and a trailer frame was wrapped around a tree.[1]

The tornado struck the London-Corbin Airport, mangling hangars, crushing multiple airplanes, and damaging a medical helicopter.[5][6] Nearby, an apartment complex and manufacturing center were heavily impacted.[2] Damage extended to Levi Jackson Wilderness Road Park, the Oaks of London subdivision, and areas along U.S. 25, Old Whitley Road, and Hart Church Road.[2][5] In Laurel County, 817 homes were affected, including 280 completely destroyed and 195 with major damage.[1] Seventeen fatalities occurred here.[7] The tornado crossed Interstate 75 without causing major incidents on the highway itself.[2]

## Casualties
The tornado resulted in 19 confirmed fatalities, making it the deadliest in the NWS Jackson service area's history.[4] Seventeen deaths were in Laurel County, one in Pulaski County, and one in Russell County.[7] A firefighter died from injuries sustained during response efforts in Laurel County.[7] At least 108 people were treated for injuries at CHI St. Joseph Hospital in London.[1] An additional death later raised the storm-related toll to 20, including a man from McCracken County.[6]

The outbreak as a whole caused at least 25 deaths across Missouri and Kentucky, with additional fatalities in northern Virginia from related storms.[9]

## Aftermath and recovery
In the immediate aftermath, search and rescue operations involved first responders, military personnel, and volunteers, including high school students and out-of-state helpers from Michigan and Tennessee.[4] Over 4,000 calls for help were received, and shelters were established at locations such as Cumberland Falls and Lake Cumberland state resort parks, as well as local churches.[6] Schools in affected areas closed temporarily.[4]

State and federal aid included declarations from Governor Andy Beshear and involvement from FEMA and the American Red Cross.[2] Private organizations contributed: Samaritan's Purse deployed disaster relief units; Kroger donated $25,000 and supplies; Walmart provided free meals via Operation BBQ Relief; and Meade Tractor gave $100,000 to London.[2] A town hall meeting on May 30 allowed residents to discuss relief with officials.[2] Recovery focused on debris removal, rebuilding infrastructure, and supporting displaced families, with ongoing concerns about weather service staffing shortages potentially impacting warning timeliness.[5]
