### Meteorological history
The tornado developed within a highly unstable atmospheric setup characterized by strong wind shear, high humidity, and warm surface temperatures, conditions that favored the formation of supercell thunderstorms across the southeastern United States. The National Weather Service had issued a high-risk outlook for severe weather several days in advance, anticipating widespread tornado activity due to a potent low-pressure system and associated cold front interacting with a moist air mass from the Gulf of Mexico. On April 27, multiple waves of storms produced dozens of tornadoes in Alabama, with the Sawyerville–Eoline tornado emerging from one such supercell.

The tornado touched down at approximately 5:30 p.m. CDT, about 3.8 miles southwest of Tishabee in southwestern Greene County, near the coordinates 32.6152° N, 88.0543° W. It initially caused minor damage consistent with EF1 intensity before intensifying as it moved northeast. Radar data from the National Weather Service in Birmingham indicated a well-defined hook echo and velocity couplet associated with the parent supercell, which persisted throughout the tornado's lifespan. The storm maintained EF3 strength for much of its path, with occasional fluctuations, before weakening and lifting at 6:55 p.m. CDT, 1.8 miles east of West Blocton in Bibb County, near 33.1054° N, 87.0802° W. The total duration was about 85 minutes, during which the tornado paralleled or crossed several waterways, including the Black Warrior River.

Scientific analyses of the outbreak, including kinetic energy estimates, have identified this tornado as one of the more powerful EF3 events in the dataset, with significant rotational energy contributing to its longevity and damage potential. Satellite imagery and post-event surveys using Landsat data confirmed extensive vegetation scarring along the path, aiding in the verification of its track and intensity.

### Path and damage
The tornado began in rural Greene County, west-southwest of Tishabee, initially inflicting EF1 to EF2 damage to mobile homes, barns, and trees along County Road 69 and nearby areas. As it crossed the Black Warrior River into Hale County west of Sawyerville, it intensified to EF3 strength, with winds up to 145 mph. In the Sawyerville area, multiple structures sustained severe damage: roughly 1.5 miles northeast along County Road 18, two houses and one mobile home were destroyed, and further along near the intersection with County Road 30, eight manufactured homes were shredded, while a brick house was reduced to rubble. The tornado continued northeast, crossing Clary Hill Road and portions of National Forest Road 708, where EF2 to EF3 damage occurred to pine trees north of Payne Lake.

Entering Bibb County through the Talladega National Forest, the tornado snapped and debarked thousands of trees at EF3 intensity. It then restrengthened upon reaching Eoline, crossing U.S. Highway 82, where over a dozen residences along County Road 83 experienced varying degrees of destruction. Significant structural damage was noted here, including the Eoline Fire Station, several mobile homes, and site-built homes. The tornado weakened to EF2 intensity northeast of Eoline, continuing to impact areas along County Road 9, Alabama State Highway 5, and County Road 26 south of West Blocton, where additional homes were damaged or destroyed. It dissipated in rural northeast Bibb County near Marvel, after a total path of 72.13 miles.

Damage surveys by the National Weather Service documented a consistent swath of destruction, with the maximum width reaching one mile. Impacts were predominantly in rural and forested settings, affecting hundreds of structures overall. Vegetation damage was extensive, with post-event analyses using remote sensing to map the scar, revealing clear boundaries of snapped and uprooted trees. No urban centers were directly hit, which may have limited the casualty count compared to other outbreak tornadoes.

### Casualties
The seven fatalities occurred primarily in Hale County, with six deaths there and one in Bibb County. Victims included Gerald and Cora Brown, killed in their home near Sawyerville, and Ricky Smith, a father of three, whose vehicle was blown off U.S. Highway 82 in Eoline. Other deaths involved individuals in destroyed residences or vehicles caught in the path. Approximately 50 injuries were reported, ranging from minor to severe, often due to flying debris or structural failures. Emergency response was complicated by the outbreak's scale, with multiple simultaneous events straining resources.

### Aftermath
In the wake of the tornado, community organizations such as the Hale Empowerment and Revitalization Organization (HERO), Habitat for Humanity, Hale County, and the Episcopal Diocese of Alabama collaborated to rebuild at least three homes in the Sawyerville area. One recipient was Kervin Jones, a local pastor whose relatives were among the fatalities. The event underscored the vulnerability of rural communities to severe weather, leading to enhanced preparedness initiatives, including weather awareness campaigns in the affected regions. Broader recovery from the Super Outbreak involved federal aid exceeding $4.2 billion nationwide, with Alabama receiving significant support for infrastructure and housing reconstruction.

The tornado's impacts have been studied in academic contexts, including evaluations of kinetic energy and vegetation recovery using satellite data, which inform future risk assessments. No major disputes have arisen regarding the tornado's rating or effects, though general discussions on the outbreak emphasize the importance of timely warnings and community resilience.
