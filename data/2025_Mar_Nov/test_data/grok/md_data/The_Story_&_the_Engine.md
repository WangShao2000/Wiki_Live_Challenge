### Plot
The Doctor and his companion Belinda Chandra arrive in Lagos, Nigeria, in 2019, intending to visit the Doctor's old friend Omo Esosa, a barber. They discover that people have been disappearing from Omo's barbershop. Investigating, the Doctor realizes the shop exists simultaneously in Lagos and on a giant mechanical spider traversing a cosmic web in space. The Barber, a storyteller seeking revenge for not receiving credit for his narratives, is extracting stories from patrons to fuel his ship, the Engine, with the goal of deposing ancient gods of storytelling from various mythologies, such as Dionysus, Sága, Bastet, and Anansi.[1][2][3]

Trapped in the barbershop, the Doctor confronts the Barber, who views gods as oppressive figures that subjugate humanity. The Doctor argues that these gods, born from stories, enrich human life rather than control it. The conflict escalates as the Barber attempts to sever the connections between myths and humanity, which could lead to catastrophic consequences for human creativity and progress. The Doctor ultimately defeats the Barber using a six-word story: "I'm born. I die. I'm born." This phrase underscores the Doctor's own regenerative nature and eternal narrative, reinforced by a montage of clips from the show's history featuring previous incarnations of the Doctor.[1][2][3]

The episode includes references to the Doctor's past, including a brief appearance by the Fugitive Doctor (Jo Martin), revealed to have a connection to Anansi's daughter. It also ties into broader series continuity, such as recent encounters with god-like beings in the Pantheon.[1][2]

A prequel short story, "What I Did On My Holidays By Omo Esosa", written by Inua Ellams, was released on the official "Doctor Who" website on 8 May 2025. Set in 1965, it depicts a young Omo's first encounter with the Doctor during an environmental crisis in Etsako, Nigeria, involving an oil spill and forest fire caused by corporate exploitation. The Doctor assists in extinguishing the fire and provides seeds to restore the land, establishing their friendship and setting up Omo's barbershop as a future meeting point.[4]

### Production
"The Story & the Engine" was written by Inua Ellams, a Nigerian-British poet and playwright known for works such as "Barber Shop Chronicles". Ellams drew inspiration from African storytelling traditions and his own cultural background, incorporating elements of mythology and the cultural significance of barbershops as community spaces.[1][2][3] The episode was directed by Makalla McPherson in her debut for "Doctor Who".[5][6] Production took place under BBC Studios and Bad Wolf, with filming primarily in studio sets representing the barbershop and Lagos locations.[1]

The episode runs approximately 47 minutes, slightly longer than the standard for the season.[5] Visual effects include animated sequences depicting extracted stories and the mechanical spider, contributing to a storybook aesthetic.[2][3] Ellams made a cameo appearance as a market seller, following a tradition of crew members appearing in the series.[5]

### Broadcast and reception
"The Story & the Engine" premiered on BBC One on 10 May 2025 at 7:10 pm GMT, with simultaneous availability on BBC iPlayer and Disney+ internationally from 8:00 am GMT.[5] It was the second "Doctor Who" episode to feature an ampersand in its title, after "Love & Monsters" in 2006.[5]

Critics generally praised the episode for its originality and thematic depth. GamesRadar+ described it as "one of the most original episodes this show has produced in years", highlighting its inventive structure and exploration of the Doctor's mythology.[1] Den of Geek called it "poetic, daring, and visually sumptuous", noting its positive portrayal of the Doctor's Black identity and community acceptance in Lagos.[2] Blogtor Who lauded it as "pure poetry", emphasizing Ellams' writing and the performances, particularly Gatwa's emotional range and Bakare's charismatic antagonist.[3] On Rotten Tomatoes, reviews included high marks such as 5/5 from Vulture and A- from AV Club, with critics appreciating the blend of theater and sci-fi, though some noted the plot's abstract nature.[7]

Audience reception was more mixed. Fan polls on sites like Doctor Who TV gave it an average score of 5.18 out of 10, ranking it lower among the season's episodes, with some viewers criticizing the complexity and lack of clear stakes.[8] Discussions on platforms like Reddit and fan forums highlighted divisions, with praise for cultural representation but criticism for underdeveloped elements and sidelining of the companion.[9] No major controversies were reported, though some debates arose regarding the episode's handling of mythological figures from diverse cultures.[2][3]
