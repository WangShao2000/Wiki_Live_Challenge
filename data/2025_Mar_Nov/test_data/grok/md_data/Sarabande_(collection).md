# Sarabande (collection)

'''Sarabande''' is the spring/summer 2007 ready-to-wear fashion collection designed by British fashion designer Alexander McQueen. The collection was presented on 6 October 2006 at the Cirque d'Hiver during Paris Fashion Week. It explored themes of romanticism, fragility, and impermanence through historical references and natural motifs, including decaying flowers. The show has been described by sources as one of McQueen's more romantic presentations, balancing theatrical elements with refined craftsmanship. Following McQueen's death in 2010, the Sarabande Foundation—a charitable organization supporting emerging artists and designers—was established and named after this collection, as specified in his will.

## Inspiration and themes

The collection drew from a range of historical and artistic sources. McQueen cited influences including the sarabande—a slow, stately dance from George Frideric Handel's music—as well as portraits by Spanish artist Francisco Goya and the flamboyant lifestyle of Italian socialite Marchesa Luisa Casati, known for her eccentric and dramatic persona in the early 20th century. Garden flowers and botanical elements were incorporated, often with a focus on their transient nature. McQueen explained in a 2007 interview that he used flowers because "flowers die," drawing parallels to artist Sam Taylor-Wood's works depicting rotting fruit to emphasize decay and melancholy.

Themes of impermanence and decaying beauty permeated the designs, blending structured historical silhouettes with soft, ethereal fabrics to create a sense of fragility. Sources describe this as a "darkly romantic" approach, evoking a lyrical stream of consciousness about decadence and decline. The collection also revisited elements from McQueen's earlier work, including skills honed during his time at Givenchy, such as couture-level embroidery and tailoring.

## Presentation

The runway show was staged in the round at the Cirque d'Hiver in Paris, creating an intimate, theatrical atmosphere reminiscent of a deserted theater. A single crystal chandelier hung overhead, and a live chamber orchestra performed, including pieces evoking Handel's sarabande, to accompany the models. Models walked a circular path, allowing the audience a 360-degree view of the garments.

Notable elements included special effects tied to the theme of decay, such as the finale dress worn by model Tanya Dziahileva, which shed petals as she walked, leaving a trail on the runway. The lighting dimmed progressively, enhancing the melancholic mood, with the orchestra's music building to a crescendo. Viewer accounts from video uploads note the absence of audio in some recordings but praise specific entrances, such as a model in a white bowler hat and floral designs.


The collection comprised 46 looks, featuring a muted color palette of dusty gray, ivory, faded pinks, and occasional gold accents. Designs emphasized exaggerated silhouettes, drawing from 19th-century and Edwardian styles, with fitted bodices, flared trousers, and corseted forms that accentuated the hips to create an hourglass figure.

Materials included silk organza, chiffon, lace, wool, and fresh or embroidered flowers. Botanical prints depicted birds, swallows, leaves, and blooms, while some garments trapped faded flowers in layers of fabric. Wearable pieces, such as sleek gray trousers paired with ornamented tunics, contrasted with more elaborate gowns.

Contemporary reviews noted the collection's resolution of McQueen's ongoing balance between theatricality and commercial viability, presenting a cohesive summation of his technical abilities without excessive spectacle. Vogue's review described it as a "poetic rendering" of perfected skills, highlighting the lightness and delicacy in romantic dresses, though it observed that the self-referential nature did not advance broader discussions on evolving beyond historical fixations in fashion. Other sources have called the show a "hauntingly-beautiful spectacle" and a "theatrical masterpiece," emphasizing its emotional depth and artistic statement.

Retrospective analyses, such as in a 2025 article, frame it as a "love letter to impermanence," praising the execution of themes through elements like the petal-shedding dress and muted palette for capturing beauty in transition. Some critiques from the time varied, with one 2011 review noting McQueen's "dark romance" tinged with melancholy, while acknowledging the Gothic undertones. Overall, sources agree on its refinement but differ on its innovation relative to McQueen's oeuvre.

## Legacy

The collection's name was adopted for the Sarabande Foundation, founded by McQueen in 2007 and fully operational after his death, to support creatively fearless emerging talent in fashion and art. By 2025, the foundation had nurtured over 320 artists and designers, hosting events, studios, and collaborations like a 2022 rug collection inspired by alumni works.

Pieces from the collection have been exhibited, such as in the Metropolitan Museum of Art's "Alexander McQueen: Savage Beauty" (2011), and restored for later shows like Sarah Burton's "Roses" exhibition in 2019. Items remain collectible, appearing in auctions and private sales, including chrome mannequins from the era. The show continues to be referenced in fashion discussions for its blend of emotion, history, and craftsmanship.
