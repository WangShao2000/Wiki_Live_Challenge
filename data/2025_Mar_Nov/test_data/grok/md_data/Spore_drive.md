### Development and history
In the ''Star Trek'' universe, research on the spore drive began in 2244, led by scientists Paul Stamets and Straal.[1] Their work explored the intersection of biology and physics at the quantum level, positing that spores could function as building blocks of energy throughout the universe.[1] By the 2250s, amid the Federation–Klingon War, Starfleet adapted the technology for military purposes, installing it on two vessels: the USS ''Discovery'' and the USS ''Glenn''.[1] The ''Glenn'' achieved significant progress using an alien creature similar to a tardigrade as a navigator, enabling jumps of up to 90 light years in 1.3 seconds.[1][2] However, an accident involving a Hawking radiation firewall destroyed the ''Glenn'' and its crew in late 2256.[1]

The technology was transferred to the ''Discovery'', where Stamets injected himself with tardigrade DNA to serve as a human interface after ethical issues arose with using the creature.[1][3] Schematics were shared with Starfleet, leading to production at a facility on Earth.[1] Following the war's end in 2257, Starfleet decommissioned the drive due to the lack of a reliable non-sentient navigator.[1] It was reactivated in 2258 under Captain Christopher Pike for a mission to investigate anomalous signals, traveling 51,450 light years.[1] After the ''Discovery'' was presumed destroyed later that year, knowledge of the drive was classified to prevent misuse.[1]

In the 32nd century, following the ''Discovery'''s arrival in 3189 via a wormhole, the drive was refitted with a nanogel interface.[1] A second-generation prototype was developed but stolen, highlighting security risks.[1] The drive suffered burnout during a 3190 mission to contact an extraterrestrial species, stranding the ship temporarily.[1] By 3191, research shifted to alternative technologies like the pathway drive, though the spore drive remained operational on the ''Discovery'' for specific operations, including against hostile forces.[1] In the 33rd century, it was used for a final classified mission before the ship was retired.[1]

### Mechanism
The spore drive operates by injecting mycelium spores into a reaction cube, allowing a starship to enter the mycelial plane—a subspace network spanning the universe and potentially other dimensions.[1][4] Jumps are initiated under "black alert" status, with the ship's saucer section rotating during activation.[3] Navigation relies on probabilistic calculations, measured in units called Speirin, where higher values indicate greater displacement and increased risk of error.[1] Early versions required a biological navigator to interface with the network, later replaced by technological alternatives.[1]

Key components include the spore chamber for containment, a cultivation bay for growing ''Prototaxites stellaviatori'', and diagnostic systems to monitor germination rates and energy cavitation.[1] The process can be performed at warp speed and enables techniques like transporting other vessels by positioning them between separated ship sections.[1] All personnel near the drive receive inoculations to mitigate biological risks.[1]

### Usage and limitations
The drive enables near-instantaneous travel, far surpassing conventional warp propulsion—for instance, covering distances that would take years at maximum warp in seconds.[2][5] It proved tactically valuable during the Klingon War, allowing rapid response to threats.[1] In later eras, it addressed fuel shortages caused by dilithium scarcity.[6]

However, limitations include navigational instability for long jumps, potential damage to the mycelial network, and risks such as containment failures or exposure to hazards like Hawking radiation.[1][7] Ethical concerns emerged from using sentient beings as interfaces, leading to its initial decommissioning.[1][3] The drive requires compatible pilots and periodic repairs, and overuse can cause burnout, necessitating shipyard maintenance.[1] In the mirror universe, a variant existed but was less efficient.[1]

### Scientific inspiration
The spore drive's concept is inspired by real-world fungal biology, particularly the book ''Mycelium Running'' (2005) by mycologist Paul Stamets, who shares his name with the fictional character.[4] It analogizes mycelial networks—underground webs of hyphae that transport nutrients and signals in ecosystems—to a cosmic infrastructure.[4] The fungus ''Prototaxites stellaviatori'' is based on an actual prehistoric species from 470 to 360 million years ago.[4]

Real mycelia, such as those of the honey mushroom (''Armillaria ostoyae''), form vast networks spanning hundreds of hectares and demonstrate efficiency in resource distribution, resilience, and adaptive behaviors like rerouting around damage.[4] Fungal ecologist Lynne Boddy has noted similarities between these networks and the show's depiction, though real ones are terrestrial and not interstellar, and praised the concept for raising awareness of fungi's ecological importance.[4]

### Reception
The spore drive has received mixed feedback. Some viewers and critics appreciate it as an innovative addition to ''Star Trek'' propulsion technologies, comparable to warp drive or wormholes in significance.[8] It has been highlighted for its uniqueness and role in enabling dynamic storytelling.[9]

Others criticize it for scientific implausibilities, such as relying on fungal networks that defy physics and biology, and for creating continuity issues within the franchise, as the technology is absent from later timelines without clear explanation.[3][10][11] Commentators have described it as unnecessary technobabble that undermines the show's serious tone.[3] Ethical elements, like the use of living interfaces, have sparked discussions on animal rights and human augmentation.[3] Despite these points, it remains a central feature in ''Star Trek: Discovery'' and related media, including the video game ''Star Trek Online''.[12]
