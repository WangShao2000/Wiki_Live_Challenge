### History

#### Early Development
Rail service to New Bedford began with the New Bedford & Taunton Railroad, which opened on July 1, 1840, connecting New Bedford to Taunton with two daily round trips.[2] Promoted by local investors including Joseph Grinnell and William Rotch, Jr., the line used rails imported from England and featured the Pearl Street Depot, an Egyptian Revival structure designed by Russell Warren.[3] This depot was replaced in 1886 by a larger stone facility at the foot of Pearl Street, designed by Henry Paston Clark, accommodating eight trains to Taunton and four to Fall River.[2]

In Fall River, the Fall River Branch Railroad opened in 1844 under Col. Richard Borden, linking the city to Myricks Junction in Berkley.[4] By 1846, it merged with other lines to form the Fall River Railroad, adding Boston connections and, in 1847, steamship links to New York via the Fall River Line.[4] In 1854, this merged into the Old Colony Railroad, forming the Old Colony and Fall River Railroad, which expanded networks to Providence, New Bedford, and Cape Cod.[4] By 1893, the system fell under the New York, New Haven and Hartford Railroad, creating a regional monopoly.[4]

A branch to Fall River from New Bedford was completed in 1876.[2] These lines fueled industrial expansion, transporting coal, textiles, and passengers, with depots serving as key hubs.[5]

#### Decline and Cessation
Rail usage peaked in the early 20th century but declined due to the Great Depression, bankruptcy proceedings, and rising automobile use.[4] The Fall River Line steamship service ended in 1937.[4] Attempts to abandon lines, including Fall River's Ferry Street station in 1938, were temporarily reversed by a 1939 U.S. Supreme Court decision.[4] Passenger service to Fall River, Taunton, and New Bedford ceased in 1958 under the New York, New Haven and Hartford Railroad, with tracks relegated to freight.[5][6] New Bedford's Pearl Street Depot was sold in 1959, marking the end of an era.[2] For over six decades, the region lacked commuter rail, relying on highways like Route 24 and Route 140.[4]

#### Revival and South Coast Rail
Efforts to restore service emerged in the 1980s, formalized in 1991 under Governor William Weld with a proposed extension from Stoughton to Fall River at an initial cost of $136 million.[4] Costs escalated to $600 million by 2002 and $3.4 billion by 2016, driven by scope changes including electrification.[4] Environmental concerns, such as impacts on Hockomock Swamp, prompted revisions in 1999.[4] The project spanned six governors, with delays under Paul Cellucci, Mitt Romney, Deval Patrick, and Charlie Baker.[4]

In 2017, a two-phase plan emerged: Phase 1, using diesel via the Middleborough/Lakeville Line at $1 billion, targeted for 2024; Phase 2, electric via Stoughton, for 2030 at over $3 billion.[4][7] Groundbreaking occurred in July 2019, with major construction in 2020, including bridge replacements, culvert installations, and wetland mitigation.[7] Test trains ran in June 2024, and service launched on March 24, 2025, at six new stations, renaming the line from Middleborough/Lakeville to Fall River/New Bedford.[7][8] This marked the first passenger rail to the area in 66 years, connecting the only three major cities within 50 miles of Boston without prior commuter access.[7]

### Route
The line originates at South Station, proceeding south via the Dorchester Branch to Braintree, then the Old Colony Main Line through Brockton to Middleborough. From Middleborough, it uses the upgraded Middleborough Secondary to East Taunton. The route then splits: the Fall River Secondary (14 miles) to Fall River Depot via Freetown, and the New Bedford Main (11 miles) to New Bedford via Church Street.[7][9] Tracks are shared with freight operations, and maximum speeds reach 79 mph.[7] The former Middleborough/Lakeville station now serves only seasonal CapeFLYER trains, with regular service at the new Middleborough station.[9]

### Operations
The MBTA operates the line with bi-level coaches and locomotives, refurbished for the extension.[7] Weekday service includes direct trains and shuttles, with transfers at East Taunton to optimize frequency.[9][10] For instance, a 6:22 a.m. shuttle from New Bedford connects to a 6:35 a.m. Fall River train to Boston.[9] Schedules indicate seat availability and allow early departures at certain stops.[10] Fares align with MBTA zones, with Zone 8 applying to new stations.[9] Bicycles are accommodated without restrictions during regular service.[7][9]

Ridership surged 33% post-launch by November 2025, contrasting with a system-wide decline.[1] However, initial months saw disruptions: 13 trains failed to complete trips on time, 30 were canceled, and dozens delayed due to crew shortages and mechanical issues.[11][12] State legislators investigated these in April-May 2025, with the MBTA attributing problems to training delays.[11][13] About 15 passengers were stranded by cancellations in the first weeks.[13]

### Economic and Social Impact
The line aims to enhance access to Boston's labor market, potentially generating economic development in southeastern Massachusetts.[14][15] Studies suggest improved commuting could boost reverse commutes and regional growth.[16] Post-launch, business groups noted transportation's role in opportunity, with ridership increases indicating demand.[1][17] However, some residents express concerns over rising housing costs and displacement in Fall River and New Bedford.[18][19] The project is projected to influence real estate markets, though benefits may be gradual.[19][20]

### Future Plans
The Full Build phase, in preliminary engineering as of 2025, would extend via the Stoughton Line, adding connections and electrification for efficiency.[7] This involves wetland mitigation and funding challenges, with no firm timeline.[7] Costs for the full project have drawn scrutiny, with Phase 1 at $1 billion and the total potentially exceeding $3 billion.[4][21]
