### USCGC Dione

USCGC *Dione* (WPC-107) was a 165-foot Thetis-class patrol boat of the United States Coast Guard. Launched in 1934 and commissioned shortly after the repeal of Prohibition, the vessel served for nearly three decades in roles including law enforcement, search and rescue, and wartime convoy escort. During World War II, *Dione* operated along the East Coast, engaging in anti-submarine operations and assisting in the surrender of German U-boats following Germany's capitulation in Europe. The cutter was decommissioned in 1963 after a career marked by routine patrols and occasional rescue missions. Its service history illustrates the Coast Guard's adaptation from anti-smuggling duties to broader maritime security responsibilities.

#### Design and Construction
The *Dione* was part of the Thetis-class patrol boats, a series of 18 steel-hulled vessels built between 1931 and 1934 to succeed the earlier 125-foot cutters.[1] These cutters, sometimes referred to as the "B" class or Argo class, were designed for offshore patrol to enforce U.S. laws, particularly those related to Prohibition.[3] However, Prohibition ended in 1933, shifting the class's primary focus to general law enforcement and search and rescue.

Built by the Manitowoc Shipbuilding Corporation in Manitowoc, Wisconsin, the *Dione* was launched on June 30, 1934.[1][2] It was commissioned on October 5, 1934, and initially assigned to Norfolk, Virginia.[1] The vessel's name derives from Dione, a figure in Greek mythology as the mother of Aphrodite by Zeus.[1]

Key specifications as of 1933 included:
- **Displacement**: 337 tons at full load (increasing to 350 tons by 1945).
- **Dimensions**: Length overall 165 feet; beam 25 feet 3 inches; draft 7 feet 8 inches (extending to 10 feet by 1945).
- **Propulsion**: Two Winton Model 158 6-cylinder diesel engines producing 1,340 brake horsepower, driving twin three-bladed propellers.
- **Performance**: Maximum speed of 16 knots; sustained speed of 14 knots for 1,750 statute miles; cruising speed of 11 knots for 3,000 miles; economic speed of 6 knots for 6,417 miles.
- **Complement**: Five officers and 39 enlisted in 1933, expanding to seven officers and 68 enlisted by 1945.
- **Armament**: In 1933, one 3-inch/23 gun and one 1-pounder. By 1941, this included one 3-inch/23 gun, a Y-gun, and two depth charge tracks. By 1945, armament comprised two 3-inch/50 guns (single mounts), two 20mm/80 guns (single mounts), two depth charge tracks, two Y-guns, and two Mousetrap anti-submarine projectors.
- **Electronics**: Initially equipped with wireless communication; by 1945, added SF radar and QCN-1 sonar.[1]

The construction cost was approximately $258,000.[1] The design prioritized endurance and seaworthiness for extended patrols in coastal and offshore waters.

#### World War II Service
At the outset of U.S. involvement in World War II, the *Dione* was assigned to the Eastern Sea Frontier (EASTSEAFRON) for coastal convoy escort duties out of Norfolk.[1] This role placed it in the midst of the Battle of the Atlantic, where German U-boats targeted Allied shipping along the American East Coast.

Notable actions included:
- On March 20, 1942, an aircraft from Elizabeth City Air Station dropped depth charges on a submarine contact detected by *Dione*, with unknown results.[1]
- On April 5, 1942, the cutter rescued one survivor from the torpedoed oil tanker SS *Byron D. Benson*.[1]
- On April 18, 1942, *Dione* dropped depth charges following a submarine attack on a tanker, again with no confirmed results.[1]
- During escort duties on June 24, 1942, it made an underwater contact and dropped depth charges without results; later that day, it responded to the torpedoing of SS *Nordland* and SS *Manuela*, conducting searches while other vessels aided survivors.[1]
- On June 25, 1942, another contact led to five depth charges, resulting in oil surfacing—potentially indicating damage to a submarine, though unconfirmed.[1]
- On June 27, 1942, it dropped four charges on a doubtful contact.[1]

In May 1945, as the war in Europe ended, *Dione* joined a U.S. Navy "surrender group" comprising six patrol vessels, including fellow Coast Guard cutters *Nemesis* (WPC-111) and *Argo* (WPC-100).[3] This group accepted the surrender of five German U-boats following Grand Admiral Karl Dönitz's broadcast on May 8, 1945, ordering all U-boats to capitulate.[3] Specifically, *Dione* escorted the Type IXC/40 submarine U-1228, which had surrendered at sea on May 9, 1945, into Portsmouth, New Hampshire, on May 17, 1945.[4][5] U-1228 had initially been directed to St. John's, Newfoundland, but proceeded to Portsmouth due to weather conditions.[6] This event marked one of the final U-boat surrenders in the Atlantic theater.[3]

By June 1945, *Dione* shifted to air-sea rescue duties in the 5th Coast Guard District.[1]

#### Post-War Service
Following the war, *Dione* returned to law enforcement and search and rescue operations out of Norfolk from 1945 to 1947.[1][6] Due to personnel shortages, it was placed in storage for four years.[1] Recommissioned in 1951, it was transferred to Freeport, Texas, in 1952, where it remained based until the end of its service.[1][6]

A notable post-war operation occurred from January 2 to 5, 1957, when *Dione* towed the disabled cutter *Iris* to New Orleans.[1]

#### Decommissioning
The *Dione* was decommissioned on February 8, 1963.[1][2] It was sold on February 24, 1964.[1][2] No records indicate its fate after sale.

#### Legacy
The *Dione*'s career spanned significant transitions in U.S. maritime operations, from the tail end of Prohibition to Cold War-era patrols. Photographs from its service, including WWII actions and crew activities, are preserved in Coast Guard archives.[1] Two surviving crew members from its early WWII days were honored by the Coast Guard Auxiliary in 2011.[1] The vessel's involvement in U-boat surrenders underscores the Coast Guard's contributions to the Allied victory in the Atlantic, though its role was more supportive than decisive compared to larger naval assets.
