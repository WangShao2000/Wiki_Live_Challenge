## Early life
Kaleigh Trace was born in 1987.[1] She spent part of her childhood in Nova Scotia, where she later attended university.[2] At age eight or nine, she was involved in a car accident that resulted in a permanent spinal cord injury, leading to mobility challenges such as difficulty walking without swaying or falling.[3][4] This injury shaped her experiences growing up, particularly during her teenage years in the mid-2000s, when she felt alienated from cultural ideals of desirability portrayed in media featuring able-bodied figures.[3] Trace has described her disability as influencing her sense of self, noting that it made her feel inherently undesirable in a society that often excludes disabled bodies from discussions of beauty and sexuality.[3][5]

She graduated from Dalhousie University in Halifax in 2009 with a Bachelor of Arts degree during an economic recession.[3][6] Trace has reflected on this period as one of uncertainty, with limited job prospects following her graduation.[6]

## Career
### Sex education
In 2010, at age 23, Trace began working at Venus Envy, a queer- and feminist-oriented sex shop and bookstore in Halifax.[3][7] Initially hired amid financial need, she sold sex toys and books while learning about feminist theory and sexuality.[3][8] Over five years, she transitioned into a sex educator, leading workshops on topics such as consent, oral sex techniques, and sexual health for diverse audiences, including students, bachelorette parties, and seniors' groups.[3][9] Trace has stated that her role helped her overcome initial feelings of being an imposter, especially as a queer person who was still coming out.[3] She educated herself through reading and practice, emphasizing experimental and playful approaches to sex that challenge assumptions about gender roles and dominance.[3]

Her disability informed her work, as she found that people were often more comfortable discussing vulnerabilities around her, allowing her to address insecurities and fears related to bodies and intimacy.[3] Trace conducted demonstrations, such as safe sex practices, for thousands of Dalhousie University students.[3] She has advocated for sex education rooted in respect and pleasure rather than power and shame, critiquing cultural norms that promote performative dishonesty—such as pretending to know one's desires or conforming to gendered expectations.[10] Trace worked at Venus Envy until her early thirties.[3]

### Therapy
In 2017, Trace moved to Toronto while commuting to the University of Guelph to pursue a Master of Science in Couple and Family Therapy.[3][11] She specialized in healing sexual trauma, nonmonogamy, and kink, viewing sex as both empowering and complex.[3][12] After completing her degree, she worked at a couples therapy clinic before opening her own private practice as a registered psychotherapist.[3][13] Trace focuses on relational dynamics, helping clients process shame, grow through vulnerability, and build connections.[14] She took a year off in 2022 following her cancer diagnosis but resumed part-time work in February 2024, describing it as a privilege to support others in relational healing.[10][14]

Trace has emphasized the importance of community and interdependence, stating that humans are relational beings who need one another, though this requires effort to navigate love and vulnerability.[14]

## Writing
Trace started a blog called *The Fucking Facts* to address gaps in sex education resources for disabled people, which were often centered on able-bodied experiences.[3][15] The blog documented her explorations of pleasure and joy, serving as a foundation for her writing career.[16]

Her debut book, *Hot, Wet, and Shaking: How I Learned to Talk about Sex*, was published in 2014 by Invisible Publishing.[17] It is a memoir and essay collection recounting her journey to self-acceptance as a disabled person, her experiences in sex education, and her embrace of queer sexuality.[3][18] The book includes humorous and candid anecdotes about navigating sex with a disability, such as managing bladder issues during intimate encounters.[19] It challenges ableist norms in sexuality, promoting the idea that bodies are inherently divine and deserving of pleasure regardless of ability.[10][20] Trace has described the book as an invitation to radical delight, written to fill a void in representations of queer, disabled sex.[21] The cover features her image, and the book involved national tours and media appearances.[3]

The memoir won the Evelyn Richardson Non-Fiction Award in 2015 and was shortlisted for an East Coast Literary Award.[22][23] A 10th-anniversary edition was released in 2024, with new essays, a foreword by Christa Couture, and reflections on her cancer diagnosis.[17][24] Trace has noted the jarring contrast between her joyful 27-year-old voice and her grieving 37-year-old perspective, but values the honesty that connects them.[10]

Trace's work has appeared in various publications, including personal essays on sex, disability, and life.[17] She has been interviewed on topics like vulnerability in sex and grief, often linking sex and death as experiences demanding full presence and connection.[10][21]

## Personal life
Trace identifies as queer, disabled, and femme.[25][26] She has discussed the intersections of these identities, viewing "queer" and "disabled" as labels of resistance against normative expectations.[16] Her disability, stemming from her spinal cord injury, affects mobility and has required adaptations in daily life and intimacy.[3][4] Trace has advocated for embracing bodily imperfections, celebrating strengths like arm mobility while acknowledging challenges.[10] She has critiqued societal ableism that assumes universal norms in sex education, sharing her own delayed discovery of orgasm due to a lack of inclusive guidance.[14]

In October 2022, Trace was diagnosed with rapidly progressing bladder cancer, which was deemed terminal.[3][27] She underwent emergency surgery and has been in ongoing treatment, including chemotherapy.[10] Initially given a two-year prognosis, she has responded well but continues to face recurrences.[10] The diagnosis coincided with the end of a six-year relationship, leading to a period of grief and reflection.[10][28] Trace has described using vulnerability—learned through sex—to approach death, fostering deeper connections.[10] She has hosted "sex and death salons" at book launches, exploring how both demand attention and invite fuller living.[10]

Trace has spoken about finding aliveness through sex and community amid her illness, noting that eros opposes death by demanding presence.[14] She values humor and absurdity in bodies, encouraging self-trust and playfulness in intimacy.[14]
