# 2025 Pennsylvania Supreme Court election

The 2025 Pennsylvania Supreme Court election was a retention election held on November 4, 2025, to determine whether three incumbent justices of the Pennsylvania Supreme Court—Christine Donohue, Kevin Dougherty, and David Wecht—would serve additional 10-year terms. All three justices, who identify with the Democratic Party, were retained by voters with approval rates between 61% and 62%. The election maintained the court's 5-2 Democratic majority, which has held since 2016. Although retention elections in Pennsylvania are typically low-profile and result in incumbent approval, the 2025 races attracted significant outside spending and partisan involvement due to the court's role in rulings on voting laws, redistricting, and other politically sensitive matters. Total spending exceeded $10 million, marking one of the most expensive judicial retention campaigns in the state's history.

## Background

The Pennsylvania Supreme Court is the highest court in the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania and consists of seven justices who serve 10-year terms. Justices are initially selected through partisan elections, but subsequent terms are decided by nonpartisan retention elections, where voters decide yes or no on retaining the incumbent without opposing candidates.[1][4] Retention requires a simple majority, and justices cannot actively campaign under the Pennsylvania Code of Judicial Conduct.[4] If a justice is not retained, the seat becomes vacant and is filled through a partisan election in the next odd-numbered year.[2]

The three justices up for retention in 2025—Donohue, Dougherty, and Wecht—were elected in the 2015 partisan election, which shifted the court to a Democratic majority for the first time in decades.[2][4] At the time of the 2025 election, the court had a 5-2 Democratic composition, with Chief Justice Debra Todd (Democrat), Justices Donohue (Democrat), Dougherty (Democrat), Wecht (Democrat), Sallie Mundy (Republican), Kevin Brobson (Republican), and one additional Democratic justice.[3] The court's decisions during the justices' terms included upholding mail-in voting laws, invalidating a congressional map drawn by Republican lawmakers, and addressing COVID-19 restrictions, which drew criticism from conservative groups.[2][8]

Retention elections have historically favored incumbents in Pennsylvania. Since the adoption of the current system in the 1968 state constitution, only one statewide justice, Russell Nigro (Democrat), was not retained, in 2005 amid public backlash over legislative pay raises.[2][4] In 102 retention elections nationwide since 2020, 98% of justices were retained.[4]

## Justices

### Christine Donohue

Christine L. Donohue (born December 24, 1952) is a justice of the Pennsylvania Supreme Court. She was elected in 2015 after serving on the Pennsylvania Superior Court from 2008 to 2015.[5] Prior to her judicial career, Donohue worked in private practice as a litigator from 1980 to 2007, handling state and federal cases. She also served on the Pennsylvania Court of Judicial Discipline and the Disciplinary Board of the Supreme Court of Pennsylvania.[5][37] Donohue authored the 2022 majority opinion upholding the state's mail-in voting law.[2] She is scheduled to retire in 2027 upon reaching the mandatory retirement age of 75.[2][3]

### Kevin Dougherty

Kevin M. Dougherty (born May 19, 1962) is a justice of the Pennsylvania Supreme Court. Elected in 2015, he previously served as a judge on the Philadelphia Court of Common Pleas from 2002 to 2015, including as administrative judge of the Family Division.[6] Dougherty has focused on behavioral health initiatives, establishing the Office of Behavioral Health in the courts and promoting sensory-friendly courtrooms.[2][47] His brother, John Dougherty, a former labor union leader, was convicted of bribery and embezzlement in 2023, though this did not directly involve the justice.[2]

### David Wecht

David N. Wecht (born May 20, 1962) is a justice of the Pennsylvania Supreme Court. He was elected in 2015 following terms on the Pennsylvania Superior Court (2012–2015) and the Allegheny County Court of Common Pleas (2003–2011).[7][41] Wecht graduated from Yale University and Yale Law School, and he has held adjunct teaching positions.[7] He authored a 2020 opinion upholding gubernatorial emergency powers during the COVID-19 pandemic.[2]

## Campaign

The 2025 retention elections were marked by unusual partisan activity and high spending, totaling at least $9.8 million in independent expenditures.[2] Republican groups, including the Republican State Leadership Committee and the Commonwealth Leaders Fund (linked to billionaire Jeff Yass), ran advertisements urging voters to reject the justices, labeling them "radical liberal judges" and criticizing court rulings on mail voting, redistricting, and the overturning of Bill Cosby's conviction on procedural grounds.[2][4][8][9] One mailer accused the court of "gerrymandering" maps to favor Democrats, a claim fact-checkers described as misleading because the court rejected a Republican-drawn map but did not directly create new districts.[8] Former President Donald Trump publicly called for voting no on the retentions.[10][33]

Democratic organizations, including the Democratic National Committee, Planned Parenthood, and Governor Josh Shapiro, supported retention through ads emphasizing the court's role in protecting reproductive rights and democracy.[2][3][10] The Pennsylvania Bar Association rated all three justices as recommended for retention, citing their fairness and openness to differing perspectives.[2] The campaign highlighted broader national trends in judicial elections, where state supreme courts have become focal points for issues like abortion and election integrity.[9][29]

No primaries were held, as the elections were retentions without challengers.[4]

## Results

The election occurred on November 4, 2025, with 3,780,930 ballots cast out of 8,906,882 registered voters, representing a turnout of approximately 42%.[1] All results were certified as official by the Pennsylvania Department of State.[1]

All three justices were retained, ensuring continuity on the court. Donohue's term will end early in 2027 due to age limits, leading to a partisan election for her seat.[2]
