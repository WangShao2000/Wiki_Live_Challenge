# Trichy assault rifle
## Development and Design
The Trichy assault rifle (TAR) is a 7.62 × 39 mm assault rifle developed by the Ordnance Factory Tiruchirappalli (OFT) in India. Initiated in the early 2010s, the project aimed to create an indigenous counterpart to the AK-47, reducing reliance on imported rifles[1][2]. An early version of the rifle was unveiled in 2011, when OFT officials announced a locally designed 7.62 mm weapon “matching the specifications of the AK-47”[3]. It was subsequently named the Trichy Assault Rifle to honor the factory’s contribution[4]. After successful field trials (including evaluation by the Indian Army) and iterative improvements, the rifle entered production by late 2016[5][6]. The first batch was delivered in March 2017 to the Chhattisgarh state police for use in anti-insurgency operations[5].
The Trichy assault rifle is a selective-fire weapon chambered for the 7.62 × 39 mm cartridge – the same ammunition used in Kalashnikov rifles[7]. It operates in semi-automatic and automatic (burst-fire) modes, with a cyclic rate of fire of approximately 600 rounds per minute[6][8]. The rifle’s effective range is around 300 meters, with a maximum effective distance of up to 500 meters for area targets[6][8]. Notably, unlike many AK-47 variants that use stamped sheet-metal receivers, the TAR is built with a fully machined (milled) steel receiver for improved strength and durability[9]. It supports a 30-round curved magazine and can mount accessories such as optical sights and under-barrel grenade launchers. OFT’s design explicitly allows integration of 40 mm UBGL units (including the 40×46 mm M6 launcher), enhancing the rifle’s versatility for firing grenades or tear-gas shells[10]. The rifle also features a foldable buttstock (in certain variants) and Picatinny rails on the fore-end for mounting scopes or night sights, according to user requirements[4][11].
## Variants
OFT produces the Trichy assault rifle in several configurations to meet different user needs. Initially, three main stock variants were offered: a model with a fixed (non-folding) butt, one with a side-folding buttstock, and one with an under-folding stock[12]. All versions share the same internal mechanism and caliber, and all can be equipped with the 40 mm under-barrel grenade launcher attachment[13]. In August 2021, OFT formally launched the under-folding stock variant (described as a “down-folding butt” model) to provide a more compact profile for troops on patrolling and guard duties[14][15]. By this time a fourth version with a telescoping (retractable) buttstock had also been developed, bringing the total to four stock options[16]. These folding and telescoping-stock models allow the weapon to be shortened for transport or close-quarters use, improving handling in tight spaces (e.g. inside vehicles or buildings). Apart from stock configuration, the core specifications remain consistent across variants.
### TriCa compact carbine
In 2021, OFT introduced a shortened carbine derivative of the rifle, nicknamed the “Baby TAR” or TriCa (Trichy Carbine). The TriCa is a compact assault carbine designed for close-quarters situations and for personnel who need a smaller weapon. It has a significantly reduced barrel length (about 210 mm or 8.3 inches, roughly half the standard AK-47 barrel) which cuts the effective range to roughly 150–175 m[7][17]. The TriCa retains the same 7.62×39 mm chambering and uses standard AK-type 30-round magazines, ensuring ammunition commonality with the full-size TAR[7]. To compensate for the shorter barrel, the weapon includes an improved flash suppressor and internal design tweaks to reduce muzzle flash and overheating when firing rapidly from the short barrel[18]. The TriCa is lightweight and highly portable – only 19 inches (49 cm) long with the stock folded, and weighing about 3.5 kg with a loaded magazine[19][17]. This makes it roughly 1 kg lighter than a full-size AK-47. According to OFT, the compact carbine is well-suited for special forces, vehicle and aircraft crews, police commando units, and security personnel guarding high-value installations[20][21].
After internal development during 2020, the TriCa underwent trials (including informal evaluations by Army units) where prototypes reportedly fired thousands of rounds without malfunctions[22][23]. It was officially released in mid-2021 and began attracting orders from law enforcement. For example, the Kerala Police ordered 92 TriCa carbines in 2022 and received their first consignment in May 2025[24][25]. Other agencies such as the Assam Forest Department and the Central Bureau of Narcotics have also placed orders for the weapon as of 2025[26]. The introduction of the TriCa adds a domestically-produced option for a compact 7.62 mm weapon, filling a niche for high-firepower yet concealable arms in India’s security forces.
## Service and Adoption
Although developed with military standards in mind, the Trichy assault rifle has been primarily adopted by India’s central armed police and state police forces rather than the regular Army. The first users were police units engaged in counterinsurgency: the Chhattisgarh state police received an initial batch of 200 rifles in early 2017 for anti-Naxalite operations[5]. Following its debut, multiple states – including Jharkhand, Rajasthan, and Madhya Pradesh – placed orders as they modernized their armouries, and the rifle was observed in field use during security operations by those forces[27].
At the national level, the Central Reserve Police Force (CRPF) – India’s largest paramilitary police force – conducted extensive trials of the TAR around 2017–2018. These tests involved evaluating accuracy, reliability, and environmental robustness (drop tests, mud and water immersion, extreme weather firing, etc.)[28]. The TAR reportedly passed a 15,000-round endurance test “without any single stoppage,” demonstrating its reliability in sustained fire[28]. Satisfied with performance, the CRPF first ordered a pilot batch of 100 rifles, and after positive field feedback (including use in varied terrains such as Jammu & Kashmir), the CRPF placed a bulk order for 6,167 rifles in 2018–2019[29][30]. The first installment of this order (500 rifles) was handed over to CRPF in March 2020[31][9].
Other central forces soon followed. The Border Security Force (BSF) and the Sashastra Seema Bal (SSB) together requisitioned about 17,000 Trichy rifles by 2020 to re-equip their units guarding India’s frontiers[32]. By that time, OFT reported that approximately 11,000 TAR units had already been delivered across various police and paramilitary organizations nationwide[31][9]. According to media reports in 2021, the cumulative production of Trichy rifles neared 20,000 units supplied to Indian security forces[33]. Notably, the rifle is being used to replace obsolete .303 Lee–Enfield bolt-action rifles and older 7.62 mm self-loading rifles in many police inventories[34][35]. For instance, in 2025 Kolkata Police announced the induction of new Trichy Assault Rifles to finally phase out their WWII-era .303 rifles as part of a modernization drive[2][36]. Senior officers there cited the TAR’s lighter weight (~3.5 kg, about 2 kg less than a .303) and its 500 m effective range with burst-fire as major advantages for urban policing[11].
The Indian Army, while having tested the Trichy assault rifle, did not formally procure it for standard infantry use – instead pursuing other rifle procurement programs (such as the import of SIG Sauer 7.62 mm rifles and co-production of AK-203 rifles with Russia). Thus, the TAR’s impact has been most visible among internal security forces. Its adoption by the Central Armed Police Forces (CAPFs) and state police units contributes to India’s Atmanirbhar (self-reliance) initiative in small arms, by providing a locally-made alternative to the many AK-style rifles that were previously imported for police and paramilitary needs[37][38]. In fact, the Trichy rifle’s design is heavily based on the Bulgarian Arsenal AR-M1 (a licensed AK-47 variant), thousands of which were procured by Indian agencies in the past[16][33]. The TAR now offers an indigenous substitute: each unit costs the government significantly less than an imported rifle (approximately ₹55,000 for a TAR vs. ₹75,000 for a new AK-203 in 2021)[33][38]. The introduction of the Trichy assault rifle and its carbine variant has therefore been a strategic step toward modernizing India’s police arsenals with Made-in-India firearms while reducing dependency on foreign suppliers.
