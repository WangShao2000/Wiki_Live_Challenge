# 2020 Jonesboro tornado
The 2020 Jonesboro tornado was a destructive EF3 tornado that struck the city of Jonesboro, Arkansas on March 28, 2020[1]. The tornado touched down in the southeastern part of Jonesboro around 5:00 p.m. local time and carved a path roughly 12.5 miles (20 km) long across the city[1]. It caused widespread damage to homes, businesses, and infrastructure, injuring 22 people but causing no fatalities[2][3]. Major landmarks damaged included The Mall at Turtle Creek – the city’s primary shopping mall – and the Jonesboro Municipal Airport[4]. Officials noted that the low casualty count was aided by timely warnings and the fact that many normally busy facilities were closed or sparsely occupied due to the COVID-19 pandemic[5][6].
## Meteorological background
On March 28, 2020, a significant severe weather outbreak was underway across parts of the Midwest and South. The Storm Prediction Center had forecast a risk of tornadoes that day, and several twisters were reported in Arkansas, Illinois, and Iowa[7]. In Arkansas, a supercell thunderstorm developed during the afternoon and first produced a weaker tornado (rated EF1) in Jackson County, southeast of Jonesboro[8]. This same storm intensified as it moved northeast toward Craighead County. The National Weather Service office in Memphis issued a tornado warning for the Jonesboro area by 4:47 p.m., and within minutes the storm generated a tornado on the south side of the city near Interstate 555[9].
As the tornado entered Jonesboro shortly before 5 p.m., it rapidly strengthened. Doppler radar indicated a strong velocity couplet as the storm approached the city, and observers captured video of a large funnel cloud moving through town[10][11]. The tornado tracked roughly north-northeast through southern and eastern sections of Jonesboro, remaining on the ground for an estimated 15 to 20 minutes. The National Weather Service rated the tornado EF3, with peak winds around 140 mph (225 km/h)[12][13]. The damage swath reached up to about 500–600 meters in width at its peak intensity[14]. This was the strongest tornado to affect Arkansas since the April 27, 2014 outbreak, which included an EF4 tornado in Vilonia[15].
## Impact and damage
The tornado caused severe and widespread damage across Jonesboro. It initially struck a commercial area on the city’s south side, heavily damaging The Mall at Turtle Creek[16]. The nearly empty mall – normally busy on a Saturday – suffered major structural damage, with numerous stores destroyed or left in ruins. The twister then crossed residential neighborhoods and an industrial corridor as it moved toward the northeast. Dozens of houses were flattened or lost their roofs and exterior walls, and vehicles were tossed into piles of debris[4]. At the Jonesboro Municipal Airport, the tornado reached its maximum intensity (EF3) – hangars were obliterated, several small airplanes were mangled, and an anemometer recorded a wind gust of 74 mph before failing[4]. Just north of the airport, railcars were pushed off the tracks as a freight train was derailed by the winds[17]. In total, approximately 300 buildings in Jonesboro were damaged or destroyed by the tornado[18]. Power lines and trees were downed across the city, causing widespread outages and blocking roads with debris[19][20].
Despite the extensive destruction, no one was killed in the Jonesboro tornado. About 22 people were reported injured, and most of those injuries were minor[21][2]. Only two people required hospitalization, and both sustained non-life-threatening injuries[21]. The lack of fatalities was considered remarkably fortunate, given that the tornado struck a densely populated area on a weekend afternoon[18]. City officials attributed the low number of casualties in part to the COVID-19 pandemic timing: many businesses had closed early or were entirely shut down, resulting in far fewer people at places like the mall when the storm hit[5][6]. Jonesboro’s mayor Harold Perrin noted that under normal circumstances “a lot more people” would have been in the tornado’s path at the mall and elsewhere, potentially leading to a much worse outcome[22]. The local E-911 director described the situation as a “blessing in disguise” in terms of preventing injuries[23][24]. Advanced warning also played a role – the National Weather Service warning issued about 10 minutes before touchdown gave many residents time to seek shelter after hearing sirens and alerts[25][26].
## Aftermath and response
In the immediate aftermath of the tornado, authorities in Jonesboro implemented emergency measures to assist victims and keep residents safe. A citywide curfew was enacted on the evening of March 28 to keep people off the streets and allow first responders to conduct search and rescue operations[19][27]. Emergency crews and volunteers fanned out to the hardest-hit areas to check for anyone trapped in damaged buildings. Fortunately, no fatalities or life-threatening injuries meant the response focused on securing hazards and providing shelter. The Jonesboro police and local relief organizations opened four emergency shelters to accommodate those displaced by the tornado[28][29]. The Arkansas Army National Guard delivered cots and supplies to support these shelters overnight[30]. Residents were urged to continue observing pandemic-related social distancing even as they sought shelter and assistance, complicating the usual relief efforts[31][32].
On March 29, Arkansas Governor Asa Hutchinson declared a state disaster for the Jonesboro area, mobilizing state resources for recovery and formally requesting federal assistance[33]. In a statement, the governor remarked that he had not seen such tornado devastation in the state during his tenure, comparing it to prior major disasters[34]. Since the tornado struck amid the COVID-19 crisis, state officials noted that businesses destroyed by the storm would be eligible for federal disaster loans already in place for the pandemic[35]. Damage assessments in the days following the event indicated that the tornado had inflicted massive property losses. Early surveys by the Red Cross found dozens of homes completely demolished (55 destroyed in just the initial 35% of the damage assessment)[36]. Overall, local officials estimated “several hundred million dollars” in total damage costs to the Jonesboro area[37]. Governor Hutchinson stated that the damage likely met the threshold for a federal disaster declaration, and federal aid was later approved to support rebuilding efforts.
Reconstruction in Jonesboro proceeded in the months and years after the tornado, though full recovery took time. Many damaged homes and businesses were rebuilt or repaired, but some facilities never returned. In particular, The Mall at Turtle Creek, which had been a centerpiece of the city’s retail community, was permanently closed as a result of the tornado’s destruction[38]. Several of the mall’s retailers later relocated to other shopping centers or rebuilt at new locations in the city, but the mall structure itself was not restored. By the one-year anniversary of the disaster, most of the debris had been cleared and many affected neighborhoods were on the mend, even as the community coped with the dual challenges of tornado recovery and the ongoing pandemic. City leaders praised the efficient warning systems and community response that helped prevent loss of life, and Jonesboro’s experience in 2020 has since been cited as a case study in effective emergency preparedness during compounding disasters[26][21].
