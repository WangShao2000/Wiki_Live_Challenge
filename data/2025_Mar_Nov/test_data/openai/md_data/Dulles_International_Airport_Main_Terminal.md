# Dulles International Airport Main Terminal
Dulles International Airport Main Terminal is the central passenger terminal building at Washington Dulles International Airport in Dulles, Virginia, United States. Designed by Finnish-American architect Eero Saarinen and opened in 1962, the terminal is famous for its distinctive curved roof – a cable-supported concrete shell suspended between outward-leaning concrete piers – which gives the building a dramatic, soaring form[1]. It was one of the first airport terminals in the United States built specifically for jet air travel and introduced an innovative mobile lounge system to transport passengers between the terminal and aircraft, instead of using traditional gate concourses[2]. Originally 600 feet (180 m) long and 200 feet wide, the terminal was designed to allow expansion and was extended to 1,240 feet in 1996 to accommodate growing passenger traffic[3]. The Dulles main terminal is widely regarded as an icon of mid-20th-century modern architecture; it received the American Institute of Architects First Honor Award in 1966 and was listed on the U.S. National Register of Historic Places in 1978, recognizing its historical and architectural significance[4][5].
## History
Planning and construction: In the 1950s, rapid growth in air travel prompted the U.S. government to plan a new international airport for the nation’s capital[6]. The Washington Airport Act of 1950 authorized the project, and in 1958 President Dwight D. Eisenhower selected a 10,000-acre site in Fairfax and Loudoun Counties, Virginia, for the new airport[7][8]. That same year, the Civil Aeronautics Administration (predecessor of the FAA) commissioned architect Eero Saarinen, in collaboration with the engineering firm Ammann & Whitney, to design the airport’s main terminal and control tower[9]. Saarinen sought to capture “the soul of the airport” in his design and later called the Dulles terminal “the best thing I have ever done”[4]. Construction of the terminal began in September 1958 and was completed in late 1962[10]. The new Washington Dulles International Airport was formally dedicated by President John F. Kennedy on November 17, 1962, and it opened for commercial flights two days later on November 19, 1962[11][12]. The terminal was originally a compact two-level building, 600 ft long and able to serve around 6 million passengers per year[13][14]. Its striking modern design and the airport’s unique mobile lounges immediately impressed travelers and dignitaries at the opening, symbolizing America’s entry into the Jet Age[15].
Early operations and expansions: In its early years, Dulles Airport saw lower traffic than expected, leading critics to dub it a “white elephant,” but usage grew steadily by the 1970s as the jet age progressed[16]. The main terminal itself underwent several modifications over time. The first significant upgrade was completed in 1982, when additional passenger waiting areas were added to the upper level and a new baggage handling area was built on the lower level to cope with increased activity[17]. In 1989–1991, a separate International Arrivals Building (designed by Skidmore, Owings & Merrill) was opened adjacent to the main terminal to expand customs and immigration facilities for growing international flights[18]. A major extension of the main terminal was carried out from 1995 to 1997, in accordance with Saarinen’s original modular design. On October 1, 1996, the expanded terminal opened – its length doubled from 600 ft to about 1,240 ft and its floor area increased from about 500,000 sq ft to over 1.1 million sq ft[3]. This expansion, led by Saarinen’s successor architects and SOM, extended the terminal at both ends while retaining the building’s architectural integrity and profile. The iconic concrete control tower that originally rose from the center of the terminal (193 ft tall) remained in service until 2007, when a new, taller control tower was constructed a mile away; the historic tower atop the main terminal is now inactive but preserved as part of the landmark structure[19][20]. In the 2000s and 2010s, the main terminal’s functionality was further enhanced by new transit connections: the underground AeroTrain people-mover system began operations in 2010, linking the terminal to midfield concourses, and in November 2022 a station on Washington Metro’s Silver Line opened at Dulles, providing direct rail service to the airport[21][22]. Throughout these changes, the original Saarinen terminal has remained the focal point and architectural centerpiece of Dulles Airport.
## Design and Architecture
Concept and structure: The Dulles main terminal’s design was groundbreaking for its time, combining bold aesthetic vision with functional innovation. Saarinen conceived the terminal as a swooping concrete form that evokes the drama of flight – often likened to a wing or a “huge continuous hammock suspended between concrete trees,” as he described it[1]. The roof of the building is a graceful curved shell supported by a system of steel tension cables draped between a row of outward-angled concrete piers on each side. These Y-shaped piers (approximately 65 feet tall along the front façade and 40 feet tall on the airside) hold the roof’s weight in balance, creating a clear span inside without interior columns[23][24]. The result is a vast, open interior space uninterrupted by supports – an architectural expression of openness and movement. The terminal’s profile is lowest on the airfield side and rises higher on the front side, a design that makes the building appear to hover above the ground when seen from a distance[25]. Glass curtain walls stretch between the piers, allowing natural light and views of the airfield. Saarinen deliberately elevated the terminal on a platform (with a service basement below) and separated passenger levels by function: departing travelers enter on the upper level (ticketing and departures), while arrivals and baggage claim are on the lower level[26][27]. A dual-level roadway in front of the building serves each level, streamlining traffic flow.
Interior and features: The main hall of the terminal was designed to be spacious and uncluttered, enhancing travelers’ experience of the architectural space. In Saarinen’s original layout, airline ticket counters were placed in a single central row, and ample open lounge space was provided on either side[28]. Notably, the Dulles Terminal pioneered the use of mobile lounges – large wheeled passenger transport vehicles – as part of its design. Passengers would proceed from the ticket counters directly to mobile lounge docking gates at the rear of the terminal, where the massive lounge vehicles (each 54 ft long, carrying up to 102 passengers) would drive right up and attach to the building[29][30]. These mobile lounges then ferried passengers across the ramp to aircraft parked on remote hardstands. This system allowed Dulles to operate without the long finger concourses common in other airports of the era[31][30]. While the mobile lounge concept was not widely adopted elsewhere, it was an integral part of Dulles’s design to minimize walking distances and could flexibly serve aircraft of any size. The terminal also originally incorporated a control tower protruding above its roofline at the center, emphasizing the building’s symmetry and Jet Age futurism. Amenities and interiors were designed in a mid-century modern style; for example, renowned designers Charles and Ray Eames provided seating furniture for the terminal’s waiting areas[32]. Landscape architect Dan Kiley collaborated on the approach roads and parking, creating a grand processional entrance that frames the terminal’s dramatic silhouette[33].
Materials and style: In terms of materials, the terminal features concrete for its roof shell and piers, large expanses of glass, and metal accents – a palette that was modern in the 1960s and helped convey a clean, functional elegance[34][25]. The aesthetic is often classified as Expressionist modernism or Neo-futurist. Saarinen’s Dulles design has been noted for its sculptural quality and its harmony of form and function. Critics have praised how the building’s form encapsulates the excitement of air travel; the opening day press described it as “futuristic in concept as the jet air age it will serve” and applauded the terminal’s sweeping lines and sense of movement[35]. The American Institute of Architects, in granting its First Honor Award in 1966, cited the Dulles Terminal for conveying “the free and graceful movement that we associate with flight”[36]. Over the decades, the terminal’s design has remained timeless, and even as new facilities were added, the Saarinen building continues to be the symbolic heart of Dulles Airport.
## Legacy and Recognition
Upon its completion, the Dulles main terminal was hailed as an architectural milestone and a symbol of the Jet Age. In 1966 the terminal received the First Honor Award from the AIA, one of the highest honors in American architecture, with jurors praising its imaginative form and functional clarity[4][36]. The terminal also earned Saarinen posthumous acclaim, as he had died in 1961 before the project was finished – his colleagues Kevin Roche and John Dinkeloo saw the design through to completion[37]. As passenger volumes grew, there was early recognition of the building’s historical importance: in May 1978, just 16 years after opening, the Dulles Terminal was listed on the National Register of Historic Places[5]. This designation underscored the terminal’s significance as one of the first major works of modern airport architecture to be preserved for its cultural value.
The influence of the Dulles main terminal can be seen in later airport designs around the world. Notably, Terminal 1 of Taipei’s Taoyuan International Airport (opened in 1979) was directly modeled after Saarinen’s Dulles design, adopting a similar swooping roof and profile[38]. The Dulles terminal’s elegant integration of structure and form has inspired architects such as Renzo Piano and Santiago Calatrava in their own airport projects, and it remains a case study in airport planning for its forward-looking concepts (like mobile lounges and centralized passenger processing)[39][40]. Architects and historians have often referred to the Dulles Terminal as a “Jet Age landmark” and even likened it to a modern-day Parthenon for its monumental presence and harmony of design[1]. Today, the terminal is cherished not only as a working transportation facility but also as a mid-century masterpiece. Its image – the iconic profile of the wing-like roof against the sky – has become synonymous with Dulles Airport and the advent of modern air travel.
## In Popular Culture
The distinctive Dulles main terminal has made numerous appearances in film and popular culture, often to establish a Washington, D.C. setting. One of the earliest on-screen appearances was in the Cold War thriller Seven Days in May (1964), which included scenes at the then-new airport. The terminal is perhaps best known in pop culture for its role in the action movie Die Hard 2 (1990), which is set at “Dulles International”; several exterior scenes feature the Saarinen terminal (though interior scenes were partly filmed elsewhere). The airport and its terminal also appear in films such as Airport (1970), The Package (1989), The X-Files (1998), Forces of Nature (1999), and Body of Lies (2008), among others[41]. Through such appearances, the Dulles terminal – with its unmistakable design – has become an enduring visual icon representing modern aviation and Washington’s gateway to the world.
