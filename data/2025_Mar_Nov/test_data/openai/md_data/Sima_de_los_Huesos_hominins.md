# Sima de los Huesos hominins
Sima de los Huesos hominins refers to a population of Middle Pleistocene human ancestors discovered at the Sima de los Huesos (“Pit of Bones”) site in the Sierra de Atapuerca, northern Spain. The remains date to approximately 430,000 years ago and comprise at least 28 individuals from a single population[1][2]. This makes Sima de los Huesos the largest and one of the oldest known collections of hominin fossils ever found at one site[3]. These hominins are characterized as early Neanderthals or “proto-Neanderthals” – they exhibit a mosaic of Neanderthal-like anatomical features in the face, teeth, and jaws, combined with more primitive traits in the braincase[2][4]. Genetic evidence has confirmed that the Sima de los Huesos people belonged to the Neanderthal lineage, although their mitochondrial DNA shows an unexpected link to the Denisovans of Asia[5][6]. The site’s remarkable fossil assemblage has provided key insights into the evolution of Neanderthals and has sparked debate over how and why so many bodies came to rest at the bottom of a deep cave shaft[7][8].
## Discovery and Site
Sima de los Huesos is a deep chamber within the Cueva Mayor–Cueva del Silo cave system of the Atapuerca Mountains in Burgos Province, Spain[1]. The chamber lies about 30 meters underground, at the base of a vertical shaft approximately 13 meters (43 ft) deep and only a few meters wide[9][10]. Access to this “Pit of Bones” is difficult and requires descending the narrow chimney-like shaft from an upper cave passage. The site is part of the larger Sierra de Atapuerca UNESCO World Heritage area, known for rich paleoanthropological finds spanning over a million years of human prehistory.
Systematic excavations at Sima de los Huesos began in 1984 under Spanish paleoanthropologist Juan Luis Arsuaga and colleagues[11][12]. Over the ensuing decades, researchers recovered thousands of fossil bones from the chamber. Due to the cave’s depth and narrow access, excavations are challenging: archaeologists must haul sediment and fossils up the shaft in difficult conditions. Nevertheless, the site has yielded an unprecedented trove of hominin remains as well as animal fossils. The first human bones were found in the 1980s, but major discoveries continued into the 1990s and 2000s as teams expanded the excavation. By the 2010s, Sima de los Huesos had produced more identifiable human fossils than any other single Paleolithic site in the world[13].
## Fossil Assemblage
The Sima de los Huesos deposit contains more than 6,500 hominin bone fragments representing at least 28 individuals, alongside a large accumulation of animal remains[14][13]. The human fossils include skulls, jaws, teeth, and virtually all major bones of the body, often broken and scattered but exceptionally well-preserved by the cave sediments. Notably, parts of about 17 distinct skulls have been recovered, several of them nearly complete[15][16]. The individuals include both males and females and an age range from adolescents to mature adults (roughly At least one nearly complete pelvis and numerous vertebrae, limb bones, and hand/foot bones have also been found, allowing researchers to reconstruct body size and anatomy in detail.
In addition to the human fossils, the bone layer contained remains of many carnivorous animals. Fossil bones of cave bears (Ursus deningeri) are especially abundant – over 160 bear individuals are estimated at the site[14]. Other carnivores found include extinct species of lions, wildcats, wolves, foxes, and lynx[17]. Unlike typical predator den sites, however, the Sima de los Huesos assemblage has a curious absence of herbivore prey animals[18][8]. Most of the hominin and animal bones were not found in natural articulation, and some show gnaw marks from scavengers, indicating that carnivores did chew on a few of the corpses[19][20]. However, detailed taphonomic studies found that only about 4% of the bones (human or animal) have tooth marks, suggesting the cave was not primarily a carnivore lair or feeding site[20].
One striking aspect of the assemblage is the presence of a single stone tool amid the thousands of bones. This lone artifact is a finely made hand axe of red quartzite, nicknamed “Excalibur.” It was found near the human remains and is of Acheulean typology (Mode 2)[21]. No other stone tools, debris, or signs of habitation were recovered in the pit. The uniqueness of the Excalibur hand axe – in raw material and quality – and its isolated context have led to speculation that it may have been a deliberate offering or grave good, possibly the world’s oldest-known funerary offering[22][21]. This interpretation remains conjectural but highlights the unusual context of Sima de los Huesos as more than a typical living site.
## Anatomy and Classification
The human fossils from Sima de los Huesos exhibit a combination of anatomical traits that position them on the evolutionary line leading to Neanderthals. They are often described as “proto-Neanderthals” or early members of the Neanderthal lineage[23][2]. For example, the faces show pronounced mid-facial projection and double-arched brow ridges, similar to classic Neanderthals[2]. The jawbones and teeth are robust: the mandibles are thick with retromolar spaces, and the anterior teeth show heavy wear, suggesting use of teeth as tools – a known Neanderthal behavior[2][24]. Dental studies have found that Sima dentition has inner enamel and dentine patterns virtually identical to later Neanderthals[25][26], reinforcing their close relationship. In contrast, other parts of the skeleton retain more primitive features. The cranial vaults (braincases) are shaped more like earlier humans, with somewhat smaller brain capacity. The average cranial volume of the Sima hominins is around 1,230 cubic centimeters, which is slightly below that of later Neanderthals (∼1,410 cc) and modern humans (∼1,350 cc)[27]. Postcranially, they were short and stocky: estimates suggest males stood about 1.70 m tall and weighed around 77 kg (170 lbs), while females were roughly 1.58 m and 57 kg (125 lbs)[27]. This body build aligns with the cold-adapted, robust physique associated with Neanderthals, even though these individuals lived during a temperate phase between glacial periods.
When the first Sima de los Huesos fossils were studied in the 1990s, researchers initially classified them as Homo heidelbergensis, a broad species category for mid-Pleistocene humans in Europe[26][16]. H. heidelbergensis was named for a roughly 600,000-year-old jaw from Germany and was thought to be a likely ancestor of Neanderthals. The Atapuerca team initially “lumped” the Sima remains into this species due to some primitive characteristics and their intermediate age[16]. However, as more bones were recovered, distinctive Neanderthal-like traits became clear. In 2014, Juan Luis Arsuaga and colleagues published an influential study examining 17 skulls from Sima de los Huesos, and concluded that these fossils did not fit within H. heidelbergensis[28][29]. The Sima hominins showed many derived features shared with Neanderthals (especially in the face, jaw, and teeth) that were absent in H. heidelbergensis fossils from elsewhere. At the same time, they differed from other known fossils of similar age (such as those from Ceprano and Arago in Europe) and from later “classic” Neanderthals. Because of this unique combination, Arsuaga’s team suggested that the Sima population might warrant designation as a new species, or at least a separate population within the Neanderthal lineage[30][31]. As of the mid-2020s, no new species name has been formally assigned – the Sima de los Huesos hominins are generally considered early Neanderthals in a broad sense, occupying a position just before the fully developed Neanderthal form of the later Ice Age.
Paleoanthropologists describe the pattern of traits in the Sima fossils as evidence of mosaic evolution. That is, different anatomical features evolved at different rates: key Neanderthal characteristics in the dental and facial region appeared early (by 430,000 years ago), while other features like an enlarged brain and very robust body form evolved later[32][33]. The Sima skull study found that the face, teeth, and jaw show a suite of Neanderthal-derived adaptations likely related to intensive biting and chewing (for example, using front teeth as a “third hand”)[4][24]. In contrast, the smaller braincase and certain limb bone features remained more primitive. This supports the idea that Neanderthal ancestors gradually accrued their distinctive anatomy piecemeal, rather than all at once. The Sima de los Huesos population, living roughly midway between the time of H. erectus and classic Neanderthals, provides a rare snapshot of this evolutionary process in action.
## Genetic Evidence
Sima de los Huesos is notable for yielding the oldest hominin DNA yet recovered. The cool, sealed cave sediments helped preserve genetic material for nearly half a million years. In 2013, researchers managed to extract and sequence mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) from a hominin thigh bone found in the pit[5][6]. The results were surprising: the mtDNA did not match what was expected for a Neanderthal ancestor. Instead, the Sima mtDNA showed a closer affinity to the mtDNA of the Denisovans – an extinct human lineage known from Asia – even though the Sima hominins’ skeletal features clearly align with Neanderthals[5][6]. This unexpected finding suggested a complex scenario in which the mitochondrial lineage in these European hominins might have originated from an ancestral population common to Denisovans, or was later replaced in Neanderthals by gene flow from another group.
Subsequently, in 2016, scientists from the Max Planck Institute reported the sequencing of nuclear DNA from Sima de los Huesos fossils, including fragments of the genome from a femur and a tooth[34][5]. Although the recovered nuclear DNA comprised only about 0.1% of the full genome, it was sufficient to establish evolutionary relationships[35]. The nuclear DNA conclusively showed that the Sima de los Huesos individuals were early Neanderthals, genetically closer to later Neanderthals than to Denisovans or modern humans[5][34]. In other words, despite the Denisovan-like mtDNA, the overall genome places them on the Neanderthal branch after the split from the modern human lineage. These fossils lived around the time that the Neanderthal and Denisovan lineages were diverging from each other, which might explain the mixed signal. One hypothesis is that the Sima de los Huesos hominins initially carried an ancestral mtDNA lineage (common to Denisovans), and that Neanderthals subsequently acquired a different mitochondrial lineage later—perhaps through interbreeding with an African population that migrated into Europe[5][36]. This scenario would reconcile the genetic data with the anatomy, indicating the Sima population are indeed Neanderthal ancestors with an archaic mtDNA signature.
The success of DNA recovery from Sima de los Huesos was a breakthrough for paleogenetics. At roughly 430,000 years old, these are by far the oldest hominin DNA sequences ever obtained, eclipsing the next-oldest (Neanderthal DNA from ~120,000-year-old specimens) by over 300,000 years[35]. The achievement required advanced techniques to retrieve highly fragmented, degraded DNA from non-permafrost bones[5][37]. The findings have provided direct genetic confirmation of the evolutionary hypothesis that the Sima de los Huesos group falls on the Neanderthal lineage. They also highlight that our picture of hominin relationships can be complex – for example, the unexpected Denisovan link hints at previously unknown population interactions in the Middle Pleistocene. Ongoing research on ancient DNA from Sima de los Huesos and other sites may further clarify how Neanderthals, Denisovans, and modern humans each inherited different pieces of their ancestry.
## Deposition and Possible Mortuary Practices
Why the remains of so many hominins (along with numerous carnivores) accumulated in a remote cave chamber is one of the most debated aspects of Sima de los Huesos. Several hypotheses have been proposed, and the true circumstances may never be fully known. The traditional explanation is that the Sima was a natural trap: humans and animals could have fallen down the vertical shaft in sporadic events over time, dying upon impact or being unable to escape the deep pit[8]. According to this scenario, the shaft functioned like a death trap or natural pitfall. This would be analogous to other fossil sites (such as natural sinkholes or caves) where animal bones accumulate after accidental falls. The geology of the site – a steep shaft leading to a sloping “ramp” and chamber – is consistent with a natural drop into the pit[9][8]. Supporting this, many of the Sima bones were found in a mud-breccia matrix with evidence of mudflows, which could have helped transport or mix the bones after deposition[8]. The presence of so many cave bear remains in the same pit strongly suggests that animals were routinely tumbling into the hole over centuries. Bears likely fell during hibernation explorations, as evidenced by scratch marks on the shaft walls from trapped bears clawing to escape[10][38]. By this view, the human individuals might also have entered the pit by accidental falls or even been washed in during flood events.
However, certain aspects of the Sima de los Huesos assemblage are difficult to explain with a purely accidental scenario. One observation is that all the hominin fossils appear to be of a similar age (roughly 430,000 years) and potentially deposited around the same time, rather than representing many separate incidents over tens of thousands of years[39]. Moreover, the demographic profile of the humans is non-random: studies of the remains indicate an overrepresentation of adolescents and young adults, with fewer very young or old individuals than might be expected in a natural death sample[40]. Out of the ~28 individuals, only one was under about 10 years old and none were elderly; most were in their teen or adult prime years[40]. If the cave were simply a long-term trap or cemetery, one might expect a broader range of ages including more children, since child mortality was high in prehistory. The relatively selective age distribution hints that the assemblage might not result from random accidents alone.
Another hypothesis suggests periodic carnivore activity: perhaps large carnivores (like cave bears or hyenas) dragged human corpses into the cave. Yet this is not strongly supported by the evidence. As noted, there are few bite or gnaw marks on the human bones[20], and crucially, no remains of common prey herbivores were found that would indicate a den where carnivores fed regularly[18]. It appears unlikely that any predator specialized in hunting humans to bring into the cave. The absence of herbivore bones (deer, bovids, etc.) and the deep location argue against the cave being a regular carnivore den.
The most provocative interpretation – advocated by the lead excavators – is that Sima de los Huesos represents an intentional disposal of the dead by other humans, essentially a form of mortuary behavior. Juan Luis Arsuaga and some colleagues propose that the 28 individuals did not arrive by chance, but were deliberately dropped or deposited in the pit after death[10][21]. This would make the site the oldest known example of funerary practice or body disposal by hominins. The idea is supported by the lack of habitation debris and the singular hand axe Excalibur possibly serving as a ritual offering[21]. Proponents note that deliberate body disposal would neatly explain why so many individuals of one group ended up together, and why their bones are largely of similar age and condition. It could represent a social practice where this cave shaft was used as a place to lay the dead – a kind of natural tomb.
Skeptics of the mortuary hypothesis caution that it is difficult to prove intent in the deep past. They argue that natural processes could still account for much of the evidence: for instance, a catastrophic event or disease could have killed many members of a group whose bodies later found their way into the shaft via flooding or earth movements. Yet, some physical evidence does hint at foul play or intentionality. One skull (specimen Cranium 17) from Sima de los Huesos was found to have two penetrating fractures on the forehead, injuries that forensic analysis indicates were likely caused by blows from a blunt object[41][42]. The two wounds are similar in size and shape, suggesting repeated intentional trauma rather than an accidental fall. This 2015 analysis (by Nohemi Sala and colleagues) argued that the individual died from these injuries – potentially making it one of the earliest identified cases of homicide in human history[41]. Furthermore, the study contends that the presence of a murder victim in the assemblage is consistent with bodies being intentionally cast into the pit (whether by perpetrators hiding their crime, or by a group ritually disposing of their dead)[43]. If humans were indeed disposing of bodies in the shaft – whether as a form of simple corpse removal or as a ritualistic act – it would push back the origins of mortuary practices to a remarkably early date, over 200,000 years before the earliest widely accepted burials.
In sum, the deposition of the Sima de los Huesos hominins remains an open question. Current evidence has made some scenarios (like a long-term carnivore den) unlikely[20], while lending some support to the idea of deliberate accumulation[44][42]. The “Pit of Bones” may have been a natural trap that multiple humans and animals fell into, possibly exacerbated by occasional flooding that swept in remains. Alternatively, it might represent a planned disposal site used by these early humans to place their deceased. Both perspectives find some backing in the site data, and they are not mutually exclusive (for example, a deliberate disposal could have occurred in a pit that was already known as a dangerous natural trap). Ongoing excavations (as much of the deposit is still unexcavated) and further analyses may provide more clues. Whatever the case, Sima de los Huesos offers an extraordinary glimpse into the lives—and deaths—of a Middle Pleistocene human community.
## Significance
The discovery of the Sima de los Huesos hominins has been hugely significant for paleoanthropology. It provides an unparalleled collection of fossils from a single population of archaic humans, allowing scientists to study variation and development within a group 430,000 years ago[45][46]. The site has yielded insights into the anatomical evolution leading toward Neanderthals, showing which traits appeared early and which came later in the lineage (supporting the idea of modular, regional evolution of Neanderthal features)[32][33]. Because the fossils are so well-preserved and numerous, researchers have been able to examine growth patterns, calculate demographic profiles, and even diagnose pathologies or injuries on these ancient humans, building a detailed picture of their biology and lifeways.
Sima de los Huesos also anchors the timeline of human evolution in Europe. The precise dating of the deposit (~430 ka, achieved by multiple techniques) makes these the oldest known hominins clearly on the Neanderthal lineage[47]. This helps calibrate molecular clocks and evolutionary scenarios, confirming that the Neanderthal branch and the modern human branch had already split by this time[48]. The genetic evidence from Sima further illuminates this period, hinting at population dynamics (like possible interbreeding events or population replacements) in the mid-Pleistocene that were previously only conjectured. In particular, the unexpected Denisovan link from the mtDNA has spurred new hypotheses about how widespread and interconnected populations were across Eurasia at that time[5][6].
Finally, the Sima de los Huesos site has raised provocative questions about the cognitive and cultural capabilities of early humans. If the accumulation of bodies was indeed intentional, it suggests that hominins hundreds of thousands of years ago may have had concepts of death, group identity, or ritual far earlier than assumed. Even if the mortuary interpretation is not conclusively proven, the very consideration of it expands the discussion of cognitive archaeology into much deeper time. The possible murder evidence from Cranium 17 likewise provides a window into social behavior (and conflict) in these ancient communities[41]. In essence, Sima de los Huesos is not just a trove of bones, but a rich source of information about the morphology, genetics, and behavior of our distant relatives. Ongoing research at this site continues to refine our understanding of where the Sima people fit in the human family tree and how they lived and died in the deep past.
