# 2025 Pennsylvania Supreme Court Election
## Lead
The 2025 Pennsylvania Supreme Court election refers to the judicial retention election held on November 4, 2025, for three sitting justices of the Pennsylvania Supreme Court: Christine Donohue, Kevin M. Dougherty, and David N. Wecht. Voters were asked to vote “Yes” or “No” on whether each justice should be retained for another ten-year term on the state’s highest court. All three justices won retention by a comfortable margin, each receiving roughly 63% “Yes” votes[1]. The result preserved the court’s existing 5–2 majority of justices elected as Democrats[2], thwarting an effort by Republican-aligned groups to unseat the justices and alter the court’s ideological balance[3]. The 2025 retention election drew unprecedented attention and campaign spending for a normally low-profile judicial vote, reflecting the high stakes perceived by both major parties[4][5].
## Background
Pennsylvania’s constitution requires appellate judges and justices to run in nonpartisan retention elections at the end of each 10-year term, rather than facing challengers in a contested race[6]. Voters simply choose “Yes” (to retain) or “No” (to remove) for each judge on the ballot. If a justice is retained, they earn another ten-year term; if not, the seat becomes vacant. In the event of a vacancy, the governor may appoint a temporary replacement with two-thirds approval of the state Senate, and a partisan election for a full term would be held at the next municipal election (in this case, 2027)[7]. Since the modern retention system was adopted in 1968, only one Pennsylvania Supreme Court justice has ever lost a retention vote: Justice Russell Nigro was removed by voters in 2005 amid public anger over a government pay raise scandal[8]. Retention races in Pennsylvania have traditionally attracted little controversy or spending, and judges almost always win re-election by wide margins[1][9].
The 2025 retention election was unique because three state Supreme Court justices appeared on the ballot simultaneously – an unusually high number. Donohue, Dougherty, and Wecht had all been initially elected to the Supreme Court in 2015 during a rare three-seat contest that flipped the court’s partisan control from Republican to Democratic[10][11]. Since 2015 the court has had a Democratic-leaning majority (5–2 by 2025), which has handed down significant decisions on politically charged issues such as mail-in voting rules, congressional redistricting, COVID-19 policies, and abortion access[12][13]. These rulings made the court a focus of partisan interest. Heading into 2025, Republicans saw an opportunity to “break” the Democratic majority by voting “No” on retention and thus opening up seats for potential GOP replacements[14][3]. If even one justice were not retained, a statewide election for that seat would occur in 2027, giving Republicans a chance to win a new seat on the court. Had all three been removed, the court could have been left evenly split between Democratic and Republican-aligned justices in the interim, a scenario observers warned would risk deadlocks on important cases during the 2026–2028 election cycle[15][16]. This high-stakes context set the stage for an unusually intense retention campaign.
## Campaign
Although judicial retention elections in Pennsylvania are officially nonpartisan and typically low-key, the 2025 Supreme Court retention race became highly politicized and broke spending records[4][5]. Both major parties and outside interest groups treated it as a proxy battle for control of the court. Republican and conservative organizations launched a coordinated “vote no” campaign aimed at removing the three justices. Their messaging highlighted controversial court decisions and portrayed the retention vote as a rare chance to impose accountability. For example, ads funded by GOP-aligned groups and wealthy donors (such as billionaire Jeffrey Yass) attacked the justices for “radical” liberal rulings: they noted the court’s role in overturning Bill Cosby’s sexual assault conviction on due-process grounds and in rejecting a Republican-favored congressional district map as unconstitutional[17][18]. Some anti-retention mailers urged voters to “Term Limit the Court”, framing a “No” vote as a way to oust longtime incumbents and reset the judiciary[17]. This rhetoric tapped into popular sentiment for term limits and dissatisfaction with government, according to political analysts[19].
On the other side, Democratic officials, labor unions, and progressive groups mobilized a “vote yes” effort to defend the justices. Supporters argued that retaining Donohue, Dougherty, and Wecht would preserve an independent judiciary and protect civil rights. Democratic messaging emphasized issues like abortion rights, voting rights, and fair elections, warning that conservative activists were trying to “buy” the court and roll back protections[20][21]. Pennsylvania’s Democratic governor, Josh Shapiro, and national Democratic figures openly campaigned for a “Yes” vote on retention[22][23]. Even former President Barack Obama weighed in with support for retaining the justices, while former President Donald Trump urged Pennsylvania voters to reject them[24]. This unusually high-profile involvement underscored the national significance that both sides attached to the normally obscure retention contests.
Campaign spending shattered previous records for judicial retention elections in the state. In past cycles, retention races saw at most a few hundred thousand dollars in spending[25], but in 2025 the spending ran into the tens of millions. By Election Day, over $16 million had been poured into advertising and voter outreach for the Supreme Court retention votes, predominantly by pro-retention (Democratic) groups[5][26]. The Democratic side outspent the anti-retention campaign roughly 4-to-1, according to news reports, funding television ads, mailers, and get-out-the-vote operations statewide[27]. Republican committees (such as the Republican State Leadership Committee) and allied PACs still invested significant resources in digital ads and mail, but were ultimately outmatched in spending and ground game[26][28]. All three justices were also endorsed by the Pennsylvania Bar Association, which recommended voters retain them and described each as fair and qualified[29]. The convergence of national partisan attention, large donations from special interests, and active campaigning by the judges themselves made this retention election “historic” in its level of politicization. One local media outlet noted that 2025’s battle “dealt a blow to the idea that retention votes are nonpartisan affairs,” as it came to resemble a typical partisan contest in all but name[30][31].
## Results
The retention election was held on November 4, 2025, alongside Pennsylvania’s municipal general elections. Turnout was higher than usual for a judicial off-year election, reflecting the intense interest. On election night, the Associated Press called the Supreme Court retention races in favor of all three justices by around 10 p.m., less than two hours after polls closed[32][33]. Unofficial returns showed a clear mandate to retain each justice. Christine Donohue, Kevin Dougherty, and David Wecht each received approximately 63–64% “Yes” votes to 36–37% “No” votes statewide, margins of roughly 26–28 percentage points in favor of retention[1]. These margins are in line with the traditionally strong public support that incumbent judges enjoy in retention elections[1]. All three justices were thus elected to serve additional ten-year terms on the Supreme Court, which will extend into the mid-2030s.
The outcome was hailed by Democrats as a major victory for judicial independence. Gov. Josh Shapiro lauded the voters for “sending a resounding message” in favor of retaining the justices, saying they would continue to uphold the rule of law and protect constitutional rights on the court[23]. Democratic Party leaders noted that the result maintains the court’s 5–2 liberal majority through upcoming election cycles[2][34]. With all three justices remaining in office, the Pennsylvania Supreme Court’s ideological balance was unchanged, preventing any immediate shift on hot-button issues such as election oversight and redistricting. Voters essentially rejected the Republican effort to “flip” the court, opting for stability on the bench[35].
On the other hand, conservatives expressed disappointment at failing to unseat any justice. Some Republican strategists argued that the politicization of judicial races would continue and vowed to contest the open seat that is expected in 2027. Justice Christine Donohue will reach Pennsylvania’s mandatory judicial retirement age of 75 in 2027, which will require her to leave the court before her new term is complete[36][37]. That impending vacancy is slated to be filled by a partisan election in November 2027, offering the GOP another chance to gain a seat on the court. Until then, the Supreme Court’s composition will remain the same. The avoidance of any immediate vacancies also spared Pennsylvania’s judicial system from potential disruption. Had one or more justices been removed, their seats would have stood vacant until the 2027 election (barring a difficult interim appointment agreement), possibly leading to deadlocked 3–3 decisions on high-profile cases[15][16]. By winning retention, the three justices ensured continuity and a full complement of seven justices on the bench.
The 2025 Pennsylvania Supreme Court retention election is likely to be remembered for its partisan overtones and record spending. It demonstrated that even nominally noncompetitive judicial elections can become major political battlegrounds when control of a high court is at stake[3][21]. In the end, Pennsylvania voters decisively chose to retain all the justices, reaffirming the historical trend that sitting judges are rarely removed at the ballot box. The court’s liberal majority was preserved heading into the 2026–2028 period, and any further shifts will depend on future judicial elections and retirements. As only the second statewide retention election in Pennsylvania to attract such intense scrutiny (after 2005’s Nigro vote), the 2025 contest may influence how political parties approach judicial retention races in the future[8][31].
