# Sarabande (collection)
Sarabande is the Spring/Summer 2007 women’s collection created by British fashion designer Alexander McQueen[1]. Its runway show was staged in Paris at the Cirque d’Hiver on 6 October 2006 during Paris Fashion Week[2]. The collection’s title comes from a sarabande by composer G. F. Handel (famous as the theme from Stanley Kubrick’s film Barry Lyndon) that was played by a live orchestra during the show[3]. Sarabande is noted for its romantic historical inspirations and its exploration of decay and fleeting beauty, epitomized by a dramatic finale in which a gown made of real flowers gradually shed its petals as the model walked[4][5]. The collection was met with critical acclaim for balancing theatrical spectacle with exquisite craftsmanship, and it is often cited as one of McQueen’s standout works[6][7].
## Background and inspiration
Alexander McQueen conceived Sarabande with an emphasis on fragility, impermanence, and the cycle of life and death[8][9]. He described the collection as conveying “the idea that nothing is permanent, that beauty disappears”[10]. In an interview, McQueen revealed that his mood at the time was “darkly romantic” and that he found inspiration in art depicting decay, such as photographer Sam Taylor-Wood’s time-lapse images of rotting fruit[9]. The show’s title and central musical motif were taken from Handel’s Sarabande (as featured in Barry Lyndon, 1975), reflecting McQueen’s interest in 18th-century aesthetics[11][2]. His show notes also cited the paintings of Spanish artist Francisco Goya and the life of Marchesa Luisa Casati as influences[12]. These references informed the collection’s blend of romance and menace: McQueen drew parallels to the beauty and brutality of a bullfight, noting that there is “a certain beauty in the act but savagery in the death”[13]. He was also inspired by contemporary art – for example, Marc Quinn’s installation of frozen flower sculptures – which led to the use of real flowers that would wither and fall, dramatizing the theme of transient beauty[8]. Overall, Sarabande channeled historical opulence and decadence through a dark, poetic lens, in line with McQueen’s recurring fascination with the interplay between life and decay[14][9]. (McQueen later summed up this ethos bluntly: “Things rot... I used flowers because they die.”[4])
## Design and style
A runway look from the Sarabande collection, featuring an 18th-century-inspired silhouette embellished with silk organza and flowers. The Sarabande collection consisted of 46 looks in total[15]. McQueen’s designs interwove historical fashion elements with delicate feminine details. The dominant silhouette drew from 19th- and early 20th-century styles – for example, fitted Edwardian-era jackets with flared bell sleeves and off-the-shoulder necklines, and gowns evoking Spanish infanta dresses with exaggerated hip structures[16][17]. Many outfits featured tightly corseted bodices that accentuated the waist and hips, creating an hourglass shape, paired with flowing skirts. These skirts were often covered in layers of fine lace or tulle, enhancing the collection’s ethereal, “ghostly” quality[18][19]. McQueen’s color palette for Sarabande was subdued and vintage-inspired: predominantly dusty gray, ivory, and faded rose-pink tones, which added to the sense of faded elegance and “decaying grandeur” noted by critics[20][14].
Floral motifs were a signature throughout the collection, symbolizing both vitality and decay. Many garments were literally embellished with flowers – both artificial silk flowers and real blooms. For example, one pale mauve dress and jacket ensemble was trimmed with clusters of hydrangea and rose appliqués around the neckline, sleeves, and even tucked into pannier-style side hoops[21]. The penultimate look featured lavish sprays of faux flowers overflowing from the garment’s seams, foreshadowing the grand finale to come[21]. In contrast to the elaborate gowns, McQueen also included a few pared-down, modern pieces for balance. Notably, the collection showcased sleek grey tailored trousers paired with airy tunic tops – one tunic constructed from asymmetric tiers of dusty-pink lace, and another styled as an elongated T-shirt gilded with gold leaf accents[22]. These more contemporary looks demonstrated McQueen’s ability to integrate wearability without breaking the collection’s romantic narrative. Overall, Sarabande’s fashion design was lauded for its intricate craftsmanship – from hand-worked lace and embroidery to the careful placement of flowers – and for revisiting McQueen’s favorite historical references in a softer, more refined manner[16][23]. Fashion historian Kate Bethune later described the result as “fragile beauty undercut with a sense of decaying grandeur,” calling Sarabande both “darkly romantic and quintessentially feminine” in its aesthetic[14].
## Runway presentation
The Sarabande runway show itself was staged as an immersive period drama. It took place in the circular arena of the Cirque d’Hiver in Paris, with the audience seated in the round[24]. At the center of the dimly lit space stood a lone crystal chandelier draped in cobweb-like tulle, casting a sepulchral glow over the scene[2]. A live chamber orchestra was positioned under the chandelier, performing baroque music that set the tone of faded splendor. In particular, the orchestra played a custom arrangement of Handel’s Sarabande, the mournful Baroque piece that inspired the collection’s name[3]. As models paraded around the circular wooden runway, the combination of the 18th-century music, the dusty atmosphere, and the historic venue created what one reviewer called a “hauntingly beautiful spectacle”[25].
McQueen choreographed the show in a restrained, elegiac manner to complement the clothes. The models walked at a deliberate pace, allowing petals and bits of lace to occasionally drift off trailing embellishments. All the while, the classical musicians played steadily, reaching subtle crescendos as each set of looks concluded[26]. The show built towards an unforgettable finale. For the final passage, model Tanya Dziahileva (wearing the collection’s showpiece gown) slowly entered the arena dressed in an ivory silk-organza wedding dress completely covered in fresh flowers[27][5]. This gown was embellished from bodice to hem with hundreds of real blossoms – including roses, hydrangeas, and peonies – interwoven with silk flower petals[28]. Crucially, the flowers had been frozen and attached shortly before the model stepped out, so as she circled the runway under the warm lights, the blooms began to thaw and detach[8]. Petals started to flutter to the floor behind her, creating a visibly “dying” train of flowers. Viewers gasped as the dress seemingly withered in real time, a poetic visualization of the collection’s theme of transient beauty[29][26]. By the time the model completed her turn, a carpet of fallen petals trailed her path. In the final moments, the orchestra swelled to a climax and then fell silent, and the chandelier lights dimmed to darkness as the model exited – a theatrical ending that gave the impression of the gown (and show) living and expiring before the audience’s eyes[30][31]. The dramatic effect left a deep impression; some spectators even assumed the timing of the petals was a deliberate special effect, though McQueen later revealed it was an organic accident of the fresh flowers coming loose[28]. The Sarabande presentation, with its circular stage and operatic flourish, has since been regarded as one of McQueen’s most artful runway productions[3][32].
## Reception and legacy
Upon its debut, Sarabande received widespread praise from the fashion press. Critics commended McQueen for achieving a harmonious balance between spectacle and substance in this collection[7]. Vogue’s review noted that McQueen had “resolved [the] conflicting tensions” between his signature theatrical showmanship and the need to highlight the clothes themselves, resulting in a presentation that showcased “all his romantic, historicist accomplishments” without tipping into excess[33]. In the same review, Vogue’s Sarah Mower lauded the collection as “one of his best” as a refinement and summation of McQueen’s strengths[23]. Many reviewers highlighted the poetic motif of decay: author Katherine Gleason described Sarabande as “romantic, nostalgic, even elegiac,” noting how McQueen translated “the beauty inherent in death and decay” for his audience[14][34]. The show’s haunting finale in particular was widely discussed and admired. Industry observers and fellow designers acknowledged the falling-petals gown as an iconic McQueen moment that encapsulated his ability to fuse fashion with performance art. Over time, Sarabande has frequently been cited in retrospective analyses as one of the most memorable collections of McQueen’s career[32][3].
The legacy of Sarabande extends beyond the runway. The famous “flower dress” from the finale was carefully preserved after the show – with its fresh flowers replaced by replicas – and later appeared as a centerpiece in museum exhibitions. It was prominently featured in Alexander McQueen: Savage Beauty, the landmark 2011 retrospective at the Metropolitan Museum of Art, where it was displayed as a prime example of McQueen’s “darkly romantic” creativity[35][9]. (On the Met exhibit label, McQueen’s quote “I used flowers because they die” was used to explain the piece’s meaning[36].) In 2019, the house of Alexander McQueen mounted a special installation called “Roses” at its London flagship store, celebrating McQueen and his successor Sarah Burton’s floral designs. The Sarabande finale gown – regarded as a “legendary” fashion creation – was included as a star attraction alongside a newer rose-inspired dress by Burton[37][38]. Visitors could watch archival video of the original 2007 show, witnessing again how the petals fell off the dress in motion[39].
Notably, Sarabande also inspired McQueen’s philanthropic efforts. In 2007, the designer established the Sarabande Foundation, a charitable trust to support young artists and designers, and he named it after this Spring 2007 collection[40]. (The foundation’s mission of fostering creative talent was perhaps a nod to the regenerative cycle symbolized in Sarabande.) McQueen continued to reference floral and life-cycle motifs in subsequent collections, but Sarabande remains a touchstone for its particularly poignant execution of these themes. Years after its unveiling, fashion scholars and fans still highlight Sarabande for its blend of technical mastery and emotional impact – a collection in which Alexander McQueen’s artistry literally bloomed and died on the catwalk, leaving an indelible mark on fashion history.
