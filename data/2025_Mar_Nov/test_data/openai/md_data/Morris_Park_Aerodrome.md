# Morris Park Aerodrome
The Morris Park Aerodrome was a short-lived airfield in the Morris Park section of the Bronx, New York City, that operated from 1908 to 1909. Established by the Aeronautical Society of New York on the grounds of the former Morris Park Racecourse, it is often cited as the first organized flying field in the United States[1]. In November 1908 the aerodrome hosted what has been described as the nation’s first public aviation meet, drawing an estimated 20,000 spectators to witness early experiments in powered flight[2][3]. Though only in use for a brief period, Morris Park Aerodrome played a notable role in the dawn of aviation before falling into disuse and being overtaken by later airfields on Long Island.
## Historical Background
Racetrack origins: The aerodrome was built on the site of the Morris Park Racecourse, a prominent horse racing track opened in 1889 by businessman John Albert Morris. The track gained renown for hosting events such as the Belmont Stakes until 1904, when horse racing ceased there[4]. After the racetrack’s closure, the property lay largely idle, with its grandstand and oval still intact, making it an open expanse suitable for large gatherings and experiments.
Conversion to airfield: In mid-1908, the newly formed Aeronautical Society of New York arranged to lease the defunct racetrack from the city for use as an aviation testing ground[5]. The society cleared portions of the course to accommodate flying machines; organizers even removed the inner rail and fences around the track to create an “unobstructed straightway for a flight of at least one kilometer,” according to The New York Times[6]. The repurposed venue soon became known as the “Morris Park Aerodrome,” one of the earliest sites in the world dedicated specifically to heavier-than-air aviation activities.
Early exhibitions: In September 1908, preparations were underway for the first flying demonstrations. Inventors from around the world were invited to try out their experimental aircraft at Morris Park[7]. Among them was Wilbur R. Kimball, an early helicopter pioneer, who assembled his contraption (described at the time as a “heliocopter”) at the field in hopes of achieving vertical flight[8]. These efforts culminated in a public Aeronautical Exhibition and Tournament held on November 3, 1908, which is regarded as the first public air show in aviation history[1]. Crowds of New Yorkers flocked to the grandstand and infield to catch their first glimpse of powered flying machines[2]. The meet featured an eclectic array of devices – from multi-propeller helicopters and motorized biplanes to unpowered gliders – built by various tinkerers and aviation enthusiasts. The excitement of the day outpaced its technical successes: none of the heavier-than-air craft managed a sustained flight off the ground at that event, although a few made brief hops or glides[9][10]. (One prize-winning airplane design, in fact, could not fly at all, despite impressing onlookers with its appearance[10].) The large turnout and press coverage of this 1908 exhibition underscored public fascination with the nascent era of flight, even if practical flying remained elusive at the time.
Notable participants: Several significant early aviators and inventions were associated with Morris Park Aerodrome. Wilbur R. Kimball’s helicopter experiments in 1908 were among the first attempts at rotorcraft flight in America[7]. Balloonist Thomas Scott Baldwin also frequented the field around this time with his lighter-than-air airships (Baldwin’s dirigible California Arrow had used a Curtiss engine a few years prior), reflecting the overlap of balloon and airplane interests. Most prominently, Glenn H. Curtiss – who would become one of the leading pioneers of American aviation – became involved with the Aeronautical Society’s activities in 1909. The Society commissioned Curtiss to build a powered airplane for its members, and he delivered a biplane known as the Golden Flyer (or Gold Bug). After initial tests at Curtiss’s upstate workshop, this aircraft was brought to New York for trials at Morris Park[11]. Curtiss himself performed demonstration flights at the aerodrome in the summer of 1909, giving New York City residents their first live experience of a piloted airplane in controlled flight[1]. On June 26, 1909, in an event organized by the Aeronautical Society, Curtiss successfully flew the Society’s new biplane at Morris Park – marking the first ever airplane flights within New York City[1]. His exhibition flights, including circling the old racetrack, were conducted before thrilled onlookers and garnered significant media attention[12]. Curtiss’s achievements at Morris Park helped prove that sustained, controlled flight was possible and captured the imagination of the city’s populace.
## Operations and Decline
Timeline of events: Morris Park Aerodrome’s period of operation was brief but eventful. Following the inaugural meet in late 1908, the Aeronautical Society of New York used the site through 1909 for ongoing experiments, training, and occasional exhibitions. In June 1909 the Society staged a second major public air show at the aerodrome (coinciding with Curtiss’s flights)[1]. By this time, technical progress had advanced enough that actual powered circuits of the field could be demonstrated. Glenn Curtiss’s flying displays in June–July 1909 – which included take-offs, laps around the track, and safe landings – gave Morris Park the distinction of hosting the first controlled airplane flights seen by the New York public[1]. These displays also represented one of the earliest instances of an aircraft flying a closed-course circuit in front of spectators in America. The Aeronautical Society, which had purchased Curtiss’s Golden Flyer for these events, thus facilitated what is noted as the first private sale of an airplane in the United States (occurring in 1909) to enable its members’ activities[11].
Despite the enthusiasm generated, the aerodrome’s practical limitations became increasingly apparent as the year went on. The Morris Park grounds – essentially a one-mile horse track surrounded by urbanizing neighborhoods – proved too small and constraining for sustained aviation use[13]. Early aircraft required wide open spaces for takeoff and especially for unplanned emergency landings, something the tight confines of the racetrack could not provide. Glenn Curtiss later noted that while the Society had hoped to make Morris Park a permanent center for air meets and experiments, “the grounds proved too small,” and there was a need for a less obstructed location with “plenty of open country” to ensure safety[13]. The challenges of flying from a relatively cramped field in the Bronx (surrounded by trees, structures, and populated streets) meant a higher risk of accidents and mishaps. Indeed, minor crashes did occur: for example, a glider flight by Lawrence J. Lesh ended in a rough landing that broke the pilot’s ankle, after onlookers encroached on his landing area[14]. Although no fatal incidents were recorded at Morris Park, the hazards were evident.
End of operations: By late 1909, attention had shifted to more suitable flying grounds outside the city. At the recommendation of Curtiss and others, the Aeronautical Society moved its base of operations to Long Island, where the flat expanse of the Hempstead Plains offered an ideal flying field[13]. In 1910 the Society established new facilities at Mineola, Long Island, including a dedicated hangar, effectively replacing Morris Park as New York’s aviation hub[5][13]. Once the Society’s two-year lease expired, the Morris Park Aerodrome was abandoned as an airfield. Its brief tenure ended less than a year after it began, as the era of aviation exhibitions moved on to larger venues (such as Belmont Park, which hosted a major international air meet in 1910, and later airfields like Hempstead and Garden City).
Several local factors hastened the closure. In 1910, a major fire gutted the old Morris Park racetrack structures, destroying the grandstand and other facilities that had been repurposed for the aerodrome[15]. Not long after, the property’s ownership changed hands. The City of New York auctioned off the Morris Park land in 1913 to real estate developers, paving the way for residential development of the area[16]. By that time, any remaining aviation activity had long since ceased. The combination of a lack of space, safety concerns, the destruction of on-site infrastructure, and the lure of more expansive airfields elsewhere meant the Morris Park Aerodrome quickly became obsolete.
## Legacy and Current Status
Though it existed only briefly, Morris Park Aerodrome holds a noteworthy place in aviation history. It introduced powered flight to the New York City public and is remembered as the venue for the first outdoor aviation meet in the United States[2][1]. Aviation historians often highlight the aerodrome’s November 1908 exhibition as a milestone event – essentially America’s first air show – which demonstrated the potential of aircraft to a wide audience even before the Wright brothers’ feats were widely appreciated domestically[17][1]. The successful flights by Glenn Curtiss at Morris Park in 1909 further cemented the site’s legacy as a proving ground for early American aviation achievements. Curtiss’s experiences there (and subsequent shift to Long Island) reflected the broader transition of early aviation from ad-hoc city locations to purpose-built airfields in open areas.
After the aerodrome’s closure, the site was lost to urban development. The former racetrack grounds were subdivided and became part of the growing Morris Park residential neighborhood in the Bronx. Today, streets and houses cover what was once the airfield; no visible trace of the old track or aviation facilities remains. A portion of the area has been preserved as public green space – Loreto Playground, for instance, now occupies the spot where the racetrack’s grandstand once stood, although it features no commemoration of the aviation past. The only reminders of the aerodrome era are historical accounts and archival materials. One unique artifact is a large oil painting titled “Fledglings,” created by cartoonist Rudolph Dirks after he attended the 1908 air meet. Dirks was so inspired by the spectacle of the early flying machines and the captivated crowd that he painted a six-foot panorama of the scene, filled with whimsical depictions of turn-of-the-century New Yorkers marveling at gliders and contraptions on the infield[18]. This artwork, considered on par with contemporary European paintings of early flight, was later donated to the Smithsonian’s National Air and Space Museum, where it preserves the memory of the Morris Park exhibition in vivid color[3].
In the Bronx itself, the aviation legacy of Morris Park went largely unmarked for decades. However, local historians and community members have advocated for recognition of this forgotten chapter. In 2014, an archivist from the area proposed installing a historical plaque or marker to honor the neighborhood’s role in aviation history[19]. The Bronx borough historian has likewise noted the significance of Morris Park’s 1908–1909 experiments, emphasizing that “with something this important, we should think about marking it”[20]. As of the mid-2020s, these efforts have raised awareness even if an official marker is still pending. The story of the Morris Park Aerodrome – a pioneering airfield that briefly turned a Bronx racetrack into the stage for humanity’s first flights – remains an intriguing footnote in the annals of early flight, illustrating both the bold vision of aviation’s pioneers and the rapid pace of change that quickly rendered their first venues obsolete.
