# Archaeology of North Korea
## Introduction
The archaeology of North Korea encompasses the discovery and study of prehistoric sites, ancient kingdoms, and historical monuments within the territory of the Democratic People's Republic of Korea (DPRK). After Korea’s partition in 1945, North Korean archaeologists developed their own interpretations of the past, often aligned with the state’s nationalist Juche ideology. Major excavations have revealed that the Korean Peninsula’s human history extends to the Paleolithic era – with the first Korean Paleolithic site uncovered in North Korea in the 1960s[1]. North Korea is also home to numerous Goguryeo kingdom tombs with vividly painted murals, as well as medieval sites from the Goryeo dynasty around Kaesong. Several of these cultural sites (such as the Complex of Goguryeo Tombs and the Kaesong historic monuments) are recognized on the UNESCO World Heritage list[2]. Archaeological research in North Korea is conducted mainly by state institutions and often serves to affirm national legends and historical claims. International access to North Korean archaeology is limited, but joint projects – for example, with South Korea at Kaesong – have demonstrated the potential for collaboration in preserving Korea’s shared heritage.
## Historical Development
After 1948, North Korean archaeology began to diverge from that of South Korea, shaped by socialist scholarship and Korean nationalist narratives. Early efforts focused on recovering the heritage of ancient Korean kingdoms on northern soil, as well as asserting an indigenous, continuous development of Korean civilization. In 1963, North Korean researchers made a landmark discovery of Paleolithic stone tools at Gulpo-ri in North Hamgyong Province, the first time a Paleolithic site had ever been formally excavated in Korea[1]. This find (nearly simultaneous with similar discoveries in South Korea) proved that humans inhabited the Korean Peninsula in the Pleistocene, overturning earlier assumptions that Korea lacked Paleolithic human presence. Throughout the 1960s–70s, the DPRK established archaeological units under the Academy of Social Sciences, and launched excavations of key ancient sites such as the royal palace of Goguryeo in Pyongyang. North Korea’s main archaeological journal, Joseon Gogo Yeongu (“Research on Korean Archaeology”), became the vehicle for publishing findings and promoting official historical interpretations.
North Korean archaeology has from the outset been intertwined with politics. Scholars in Pyongyang adopted elements of Marxist historiography (periodizing history into stages) combined with an ethnocentric emphasis on Korea’s unique cultural achievements. Under Kim Il-sung and Kim Jong-il, archaeological narratives were adjusted to support state ideology. For example, until the early 1990s North Korean historians downplayed the legend of Dangun (the mythical founder of the first Korean kingdom, Gojoseon). However, in 1993 North Korean archaeologists announced they had excavated the Mausoleum of Dangun near Pyongyang, purportedly unearthing Dangun’s 5,000-year-old remains. This dramatic (and internationally disputed) claim prompted a major shift in North Korea’s official history: the regime began venerating Dangun as the true ancestor of the Korean nation. Following the Dangun tomb excavation, North Korean academia revised the ancient timeline – now asserting that Gojoseon was founded around the 30th century BCE with its capital in Pyongyang, and that it grew to encompass the entire Korean Peninsula and beyond. In 1998, DPRK historians introduced the “Taedong River culture” theory, positing that the Pyongyang area fostered an independent Neolithic and Bronze Age civilization which gave rise to Gojoseon. Outside scholars note that these interpretations serve to reinforce North Korea’s political legitimacy by placing the cradle of Korean civilization in its territory.
## Prehistoric Sites and Discoveries
Archaeological evidence shows that North Korea was inhabited by humans from the Lower Paleolithic onwards. Since the discovery of Gulpo-ri in 1963, numerous prehistoric sites have been identified. In the 1970s, excavations at Komunmoru Cave in Sangwon County yielded Paleolithic stone tools, and the site of Ryonggok Cave near Pyongyang produced Late Pleistocene human fossils (Homo sapiens) that have been studied by Korean and foreign paleoanthropologists. In recent years, North Korean archaeologists have reported significant Paleolithic finds at several locations. Notably, since 2010 excavations at three cave sites – Tongam-dong Cave and Hyangmok-ri Cave in the Pyongyang area, and Limgyong Cave – as well as an open-air site at Sinpung-ri, have uncovered a wide range of Paleolithic stone tools, animal bones, and even human remains. These include Lower Paleolithic tool assemblages at Tongam-dong, early Upper Paleolithic artifacts at Hyangmok-ri, and a late Upper Paleolithic industry at Sinpung-ri. At Limgyong Cave, fossils of Homo sapiens sapiens dated to the late Upper Paleolithic were found, marking the first discovery of early modern human remains in North Korea. The absolute dating of sediments and bones from these sites (using methods like optically stimulated luminescence and radiometric analysis) has provided valuable data, placing human habitation in what is now North Korea to at least ~40,000 years ago.
North Korea also possesses numerous Neolithic (New Stone Age) sites characterized by Jeulmun (comb-pattern) pottery and early evidence of agriculture. Excavations have uncovered prehistoric village remains including pit-houses and shell middens along coasts and river valleys. For instance, at the site of Jitam-ri in Pyongan Province (Middle Neolithic period), archaeologists found comb-pattern pottery fragments and carbonized millet grains, indicating some of the peninsula’s earliest farming communities (circa 4000–3000 BCE). The Neolithic cultures of North Korea show connections to the broader Northeast Asian Neolithic, but North Korean scholars emphasize indigenous development. Large prehistoric dolmens (megalithic tombs dating to the Bronze Age, c. 1st millennium BCE) are found in the northwest (particularly Pyongyang and Hwanghae provinces), demonstrating that the Korean megalithic tradition also extends into the DPRK. Bronze Age remains, such as Liaoning-type bronze daggers, ornaments, and pit tomb burials, have been excavated in sites along the Cheongcheon and Taedong rivers. These are generally associated with the culture of ancient Gojoseon. North Korean archaeologists often interpret Bronze Age sites in their territory as evidence that a proto-Korean civilization was flourishing independently in the north, contemporary with Chinese Bronze Age cultures. This viewpoint contrasts with that of most outside historians, who place Gojoseon’s center in southern Manchuria and see cultural exchange with neighboring regions; however, the DPRK stresses an autonomous development leading directly into the Korean Iron Age.
## Archaeology of Ancient Kingdoms
Much of North Korea’s archaeological work has focused on the remains of early Korean states and kingdoms, especially Goguryeo (Koguryo) and Goryeo. Goguryeo (traditionally 37 BCE – 668 CE) was one of the Three Kingdoms of Korea and controlled northern Korea and parts of Manchuria. North Korea contains a great concentration of Goguryeo archaeological sites, as the kingdom’s capital was relocated to Pyongyang in 427 CE. From the 1950s onward, DPRK teams excavated Goguryeo fortresses, palaces, and tombs to illuminate this heritage. A major project was the excavation of Anhak Palace in Pyongyang, the royal palace of Goguryeo’s capital city. Excavations from 1958 to 1969 at the Anhak Palace site revealed the foundations of 53 buildings arranged within a walled compound measuring 622 m on each side. Archaeologists identified gates, courtyards, stone pavements, and remnants of drainage systems, confirming descriptions of Goguryeo’s capital in historical records. Other Goguryeo-era fortifications in North Korea include the Taesong Mountain Fortress (encircling much of modern Pyongyang, thought to predate the capital move) and the remains of cities and fortresses in the border region with China. These sites illustrate Goguryeo’s advanced military architecture – massive stone walls expertly fitted, often along mountain ridges, to defend against invaders. Indeed, North Korean scholars take pride in Goguryeo’s reputation as a fiercely independent kingdom that withstood multiple invasions.
Perhaps the most renowned Goguryeo relics in North Korea are the painted tombs. Over the years, archaeologists have documented around 30 individual Goguryeo tombs in the Pyongyang and Nampo areas that contain mural paintings[2]. These underground chamber tombs, dating from the 3rd through 7th centuries CE (late Goguryeo period), are decorated with vivid wall paintings depicting daily life, mythological creatures, and celestial motifs of Goguryeo culture. Notable examples include the Anak Tomb No.3 (famous for its murals of a Goguryeo noble couple and retainers) and the Kangso Great Tomb (believed to be the tomb of King Pyongwon, featuring the Azure Dragon and other guardian deities painted on the ceiling). The artistry and themes of these murals provide key insights into Goguryeo’s beliefs, social structure, and interactions – for instance, scenes of hunting, dancing, and elaborate costumes. North Korean authorities have taken steps to preserve these tombs; in 2004, the Complex of Koguryo Tombs in the DPRK was inscribed as the country’s first UNESCO World Heritage Site[2]. This World Heritage listing recognizes the unique cultural value of the tomb art and the fact that, aside from ruins, the painted tombs are among the only surviving remains of Goguryeo’s civilization[3]. (Comparable Goguryeo tombs are also found across the border in China’s Ji’an region, reflecting the kingdom’s broad extent.) North Korean and foreign conservators have cooperated via UNESCO in monitoring the condition of the murals and controlling humidity and pests at the tomb sites[2].
North Korea’s archaeological narrative for the Goguryeo period occasionally conflicts with those of neighboring countries. A notable example is the treatment of the Lelang Commandery, a Han Chinese colonial prefecture (108 BCE – 313 CE) that was established around Pyongyang after the fall of Gojoseon. Mainstream historians recognize Lelang’s remains in and around Pyongyang (including Chinese-style tombs and artifacts) as evidence of Han Chinese presence. However, North Korean scholars have long denied that these relics are foreign, instead reinterpreting them as belonging to Korean states (such as Gojoseon or Goguryeo) in order to minimize the idea of Chinese rule in Korean history[4]. This stance, which echoes the views of early 20th-century Korean nationalist historians like Sin Ch’ae-ho, means that Han-era sites in the DPRK are often relabeled in North Korean research. For instance, what outside archaeologists identify as a Han Chinese-administered Lelang cemetery might be claimed by North Korean publications to be an ancient Korean tomb cluster. The issue of Lelang is part of a wider historiographical dispute – North Korean and South Korean nationalist historians vs. Chinese scholars – over whether Goguryeo (and other early states in Manchuria/Korea) are considered strictly Korean or part of Chinese history. In practice, North Korean archaeology downplays or “Koreanizes” any evidence of historical Chinese presence on its soil, consistent with the country’s political emphasis on sovereignty and cultural purity.
In addition to Goguryeo, North Korea contains important sites from the Goryeo dynasty (918–1392 CE) and the early Joseon dynasty (1392–1910). The city of Kaesong in southern North Korea was the capital of the Goryeo kingdom for over four centuries and is rich in archaeological heritage. Within and around Kaesong are the ruins of palaces, temples, and tombs from the Goryeo era. One of the most significant is Manwoldae, the main royal palace of Goryeo, which was destroyed in the 14th century. Excavations at the Manwoldae palace site have been carried out in phases since the 1990s. North Korean archaeologists, joined in several seasons by South Korean experts, have uncovered the layouts of large palace halls, stone bases of columns, building foundations, wells, and drainage systems. Artifacts such as roof tiles, ceramics (including exquisite celadon pieces), metal ornaments, and everyday utensils from the palace have also been recovered. These finds help researchers reconstruct the grandeur of Goryeo’s court and the architectural style of its palace buildings. The Historic Monuments and Sites in Kaesong, a collection of a dozen Goryeo-era sites (including Manwoldae, the Kaesong city walls and gates, Namdae Gate, Songgyungwan Confucian academy, and several royal mausoleums), were recognized as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2013. Among the Goryeo royal tombs in North Korea, the Tomb of King Kongmin (the 31st king of Goryeo) is notable – it consists of twin mounds with stone statues of officials and tigers, and is a popular stop for the limited number of tourists who visit Kaesong.
Relics of the Joseon dynasty (which succeeded Goryeo) are less abundant in the North compared to the South, since the Joseon capital and most royal burials are around Seoul. However, two early Joseon royal tombs are located in the Kaesong area of North Korea. These are the graves of kings who had ties to Kaesong (which was the predecessor dynasty’s capital). North Korea maintains these tombs, though they are not part of the UNESCO World Heritage listing of Joseon royal tombs (which only covers sites in South Korea). Additionally, remains of Kaesong Castle and other Joseon-era military outposts exist near the Demilitarized Zone, and occasional archaeological surveys have been done on those. Overall, the bulk of North Korea’s medieval archaeology centers on Goryeo sites, while Joseon material is comparatively limited.
## Archaeological Practice and Ideology
Because of North Korea’s closed nature, archaeological research and cultural heritage management are conducted almost entirely by domestic institutions, with results only selectively shared internationally. The Institute of Archaeology (under the Academy of Social Sciences) and the National Heritage Protection Bureau are key bodies that oversee digs, conservation, and interpretation of sites. North Korean archaeologists are trained within the country’s universities and tend to approach Korea’s ancient past through a lens that mixes socialism and nationalism. The state’s official ideology of Juche (“self-reliance”) strongly influences historical interpretation – emphasizing independent, indigenous development of Korean culture and downplaying external influence. For instance, North Korean publications assert that everything from the invention of metallurgy to the formation of kingdoms happened “autonomously” on Korean soil, a viewpoint that elevates national pride. These works often lack detailed data or omit context that might suggest foreign connections, which has led foreign scholars to question their objectivity. South Korean and Western experts have limited direct access to verify North Korean finds; most evidence comes through state media reports, the North’s academic journals, or the occasional academic exchange.
A clear example of archaeology serving propaganda occurred in 2012, when North Korea’s state news agency KCNA reported that archaeologists had “reconfirmed” the lair of a unicorn ridden by an ancient Korean king[5]. According to the KCNA dispatch, the History Institute of the DPRK Academy of Social Sciences identified a cave called Kiringul near Pyongyang as the legendary unicorn lair of King Tongmyong (the 1st-century BCE founder of Goguryeo). This peculiar announcement – quickly met with international skepticism – was interpreted as an attempt to mythically bolster Pyongyang’s status as an ancient capital[6]. (In Korean tradition, the unicorn-like Kirin was a symbol associated with wise rulers; North Korean officials appeared keen to link Kim Jong-un to Tongmyong’s legacy of greatness[6].) Similarly, the 1993 Dangun’s tomb project can be seen in a political light: it not only aimed to solidify the concept of a single Korean lineage originating in the North, but also occurred at a time when North Korea was seeking ideological consolidation after the Cold War. Some outside historians have described North Korea’s archaeological claims – such as 5,000-year continuity of the Korean minjok (ethnic nation) in the Taedong River basin – as forms of nationalist mythology. Nonetheless, these claims are presented domestically as scientific and irrefutable, taught in history textbooks and propagated in museums.
Despite the heavy ideological framing, North Korean archaeologists do employ standard field methods (excavation, stratigraphic recording, preservation techniques) and have made genuine contributions to Korean archaeology. For example, the discovery of early Paleolithic sites in North Korea expanded knowledge of human habitation in East Asia. North Korea has also preserved a number of sites well due to relatively low development pressure and intentional safeguarding. The Kaesong area monuments, for instance, remain in a good state, and Goguryeo tomb murals in the DPRK are often found in crisper condition than those across the border in China. In recent years, the DPRK has engaged with UNESCO and international experts for technical assistance in site preservation, digital documentation, and combating threats to heritage (like looting or environmental damage). In 2014, North Korea opened a new National History Museum in Pyongyang which showcases archaeological finds (from stone tools to medieval artifacts) in a state-curated narrative.
## International Collaboration and Recent Developments
Given political isolation and sanctions, North Korea’s archaeological cooperation with other countries has been limited but not nonexistent. In rare instances, foreign archaeologists have participated in excavations or surveys in the DPRK. One notable collaboration has been with South Korea: starting in 2007, archaeologists from both North and South cooperated on a series of joint excavations at the Manwoldae palace site in Kaesong. This project, born of a cultural agreement during a period of warmer inter-Korean relations, marked the first time since Korea’s division that teams from both sides worked together on an archaeological dig. Over multiple rounds (2007–2008 and 2014–2015), about 80 South Korean experts joined North Korean researchers in uncovering the Goryeo palace’s remains. Although occasional differences in methodology and tensions arose, the joint project was generally successful; both sides gained knowledge, and the North benefited from modern equipment and techniques introduced by the South. The excavation findings – including building layouts and artefacts – were published in combined reports, symbolizing a small bridge in the academic divide between the two Koreas. Plans to resume the Manwoldae excavation have been discussed whenever political conditions improve, as both nations acknowledge the site’s historical significance for Korean heritage as a whole.
North Korean archaeologists have also cooperated with Japan on specific discoveries. In 2010, for example, a team of North Korean and Japanese researchers jointly excavated a large Goguryeo tomb at Tongsan-dong in Pyongyang[7]. The tomb, which dates to around the 5th century CE, featured a corridor connecting antechamber and burial chamber, and well-preserved murals showing warriors on horseback and processional scenes[8][9]. The skeletal remains of a man and two women were found in the tomb, along with grave goods such as gold and silver ornaments, pottery (including a rare tiger-shaped vessel), bronze coins, and iron nails from a coffin[10]. Japanese archaeologists noted the murals’ style was comparable to previously known Goguryeo tombs (like the UNESCO-listed Tokhung-ri Tomb in Nampo)[10]. The discovery was considered important for understanding Goguryeo’s links with neighboring cultures, and North Korea announced intentions to nominate the new tomb for UNESCO World Heritage status[10]. This collaboration was facilitated through a Japanese academic association and shows that, on occasion, the DPRK is open to foreign expertise when it aligns with showcasing Korea’s ancient glory. There have also been scholarly exchanges with Chinese archaeologists, especially concerning Goguryeo and Balhae sites in the border areas, although North Korea remains cautious to assert its own narrative in those joint studies (given Chinese claims on Goguryeo history).
Modern technology is slowly finding its way into North Korean archaeology. Remote sensing via satellite imagery has been used by South Korean and Western researchers to survey inaccessible North Korean regions for potential sites. For instance, high-resolution Google Earth images have helped identify the likely locations of unknown tombs and ruins in the DPRK. North Korean publications occasionally report new findings – for example, a 2020 excavation at Hyangmok-ri Cave (publicized in state media) which claimed to contain cultural layers from the Paleolithic through Bronze Age, thus “proving” a continuous occupation of the site and lending support to the official notion of an unbroken Korean lineage in that area. Outside analysts interpreted this as the DPRK using archaeological data to reinforce national identity. Indeed, archaeologist Hyeong Woo Lee noted that the Hangmok-ri discovery was presented in North Korea as evidence of an “undiluted bloodline” of the Korean people extending tens of thousands of years.
In summary, the archaeology of North Korea is characterized by significant ancient remains and a rich record of human history, yet is uniquely colored by the country’s political agenda. From the Paleolithic tool-makers of Komunmoru, to the mural artists of Goguryeo, to the Confucian scholars of Kaesong – the DPRK’s archaeological sites span the full breadth of Korean civilization. North Korea’s efforts to research and preserve these sites demonstrate a genuine respect for cultural heritage, even as the interpretation of that heritage is tailored to bolster national narratives. As diplomatic conditions evolve, there is cautious optimism that more collaboration and transparency could emerge. Increased academic exchange, joint heritage preservation projects, and archaeological tourism (on a limited scale) might eventually allow North Korea’s hidden historical treasures to be better understood by the world. For now, our knowledge relies on the pieces of the past that have been unearthed and the stories that Pyongyang chooses to tell through them – a past proudly claimed as both authentically Korean and profoundly rooted in the soil of North Korea.
