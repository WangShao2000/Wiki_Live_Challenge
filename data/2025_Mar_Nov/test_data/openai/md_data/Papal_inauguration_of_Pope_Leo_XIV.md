# Papal inauguration of Pope Leo XIV
The Papal inauguration of Pope Leo XIV was held on May 18, 2025, in St. Peter’s Square at the Vatican City. This solemn Mass marked the official public commencement of the pontificate of Pope Leo XIV, who had been elected ten days earlier as the 267th pope of the Catholic Church[1][2]. Pope Leo XIV (born Robert Francis Prevost) is the first American-born pope in history[3]. He chose the regnal name Leo XIV in tribute to Pope Leo XIII, signaling an intention to continue the Church’s commitment to social justice; Leo XIV explicitly linked his choice to Leo XIII’s 1891 encyclical Rerum Novarum on workers’ rights, drawing parallels to modern challenges such as the rise of artificial intelligence[4][5]. The inauguration liturgy combined ancient papal rites and symbols with new inclusive elements, including the involvement of laypeople in the traditional obedience ceremony, reflecting a vision of a church rooted in tradition yet broadened in participation[6][7]. Approximately 200,000 people attended the open-air Mass, including delegations from around the world and numerous heads of state and religious leaders, underlining the global significance of the event[8][9].
## Background
Pope Leo XIV’s election followed the death of his predecessor, Pope Francis, on April 21, 2025, at the age of 88[10]. Francis’s passing on Easter Monday led to the convening of a papal conclave of the College of Cardinals in early May 2025. On May 8, 2025, after several rounds of balloting, Cardinal Robert F. Prevost – an American-born Augustinian friar who was serving as Prefect of the Dicastery for Bishops – was elected as the new Pope[11]. Stepping onto the balcony of St. Peter’s Basilica that evening, the new pope was introduced with the name Leo XIV. This choice made him the first man in over 120 years to take the name “Leo,” the last being Leo XIII (who reigned until 1903)[4].
In an address to the cardinals shortly after his election, Leo XIV explained that he took his papal name in honor of Leo XIII. He cited Leo XIII’s landmark social encyclical Rerum Novarum (1891) – which addressed the rights and conditions of workers during the Industrial Revolution – as a major inspiration for his own mission[5]. Leo XIV noted that the Church’s social teaching must continue to respond to new “revolutions,” comparing the 19th-century industrial upheaval to contemporary developments in artificial intelligence and their impact on human dignity and labor[12]. By invoking Leo XIII’s legacy, the new pope signaled his commitment to uphold and update Catholic social doctrine in the face of modern challenges. Vatican observers remarked that choosing the name Leo underscored the new Pope’s focus on social justice and a “church of the poor,” much as Leo XIII had championed the rights of the oppressed in his time[13][14].
Leo XIV’s election was an historic moment for the United States: he became the first pope from the U.S., a milestone that many had long considered unlikely[3]. His elevation was seen as a continuation in many ways of the pastoral and reforming spirit of Pope Francis. Upon his first public appearance, Leo XIV echoed Francis’s call for a “missionary Church” that reaches out to the marginalized and works for peace[15][16]. The new pope’s background bridged cultures – an American by birth who spent decades in Peru as a missionary bishop – which observers noted might help him heal divisions between different constituencies in the Church[17][18]. These themes of unity, inclusion, and global mission would soon be evident in the planning of his papal inauguration Mass.
Soon after his election, the Vatican announced that the Mass of Inauguration of the Petrine Ministry of Pope Leo XIV would take place on Sunday, May 18, 2025, in St. Peter’s Square[19]. Although by canon law a pope’s authority begins immediately upon accepting his election, the public installation Mass serves as a ceremonial inauguration of the new Bishop of Rome’s pastoral ministry[20][21]. The date of May 18 allowed time for international dignitaries and pilgrims to arrive in Rome following the conclave and Pope Francis’s funeral, and it fell during the Easter season – a fact reflected in some of the liturgical choices for the ceremony (such as the singing of the Regina Caeli hymn instead of the Angelus)[22][23]. The Office for the Liturgical Celebrations of the Supreme Pontiff released the order of service beforehand, highlighting that the inauguration Mass would “combine ancient rituals, papal symbols and global participation in a celebration of continuity and communion”[24][1]. Notably, Vatican officials indicated there would be certain modifications compared to the last papal inauguration in 2013: for example, instead of all cardinals individually pledging obedience to the new pope, a representative group of clergy, religious and laity would perform this act on behalf of the entire People of God[6][7]. This change, first implemented under Pope Benedict XVI’s liturgical guidance, was intended to streamline the rite and to visibly include the broader Church in the ceremony.
## Ceremony
On the morning of May 18, 2025, St. Peter’s Square was filled to capacity with faithful well before the ceremony began. By 8:30 am, the atmosphere was festive and anticipatory; pilgrims, clergy, and Vatican guests from all over the world had gathered, many carrying national flags and banners welcoming the new Pope[25][26]. It was estimated that about 200,000 people were present in and around the square for the Mass[8]. At approximately 9:15 am, Pope Leo XIV emerged from St. Peter’s Basilica in the popemobile to greet the crowds[27]. He toured through the square, waving and smiling, to the applause and cheers of those assembled. After making a circuit among the people, the Pope disembarked and entered the basilica briefly to prepare for the liturgical procession.
The inauguration rite began inside St. Peter’s Basilica, at the Altar of the Confession near the tomb of Saint Peter. Accompanied by the Eastern Catholic patriarchs and major archbishops, Pope Leo descended to the crypt shrine of St. Peter’s tomb and spent a few moments in prayer there[28]. In a sign of reverence and continuity with the first pope, he placed incense in a thurible and incensed the tomb – the “Trophæum Apostolicum” monument marking Peter’s resting place[29]. Notably, the liturgical symbols of the papacy had been placed near the tomb prior to the ceremony: the pallium, the Fisherman’s Ring, and the Book of the Gospels[30][31]. After praying, Leo XIV and the Eastern prelates ascended from the crypt, and two deacons reverently retrieved the three symbolic items.
A solemn procession then formed to move from the basilica out to the altar setup in the square. As the Pope, the deacons, and assisting clergy processed through the central door, the choir intoned the ancient hymn Laudes Regiæ[32]. This is a special litany invoking Christ and the saints – particularly the patron saints and previous popes of Rome – to intercede for the new pontiff. The procession made its way to the raised altar platform on the steps of St. Peter’s, passing under a tapestry hung above the basilica entrance that depicted the biblical scene of the miraculous catch of fish (a visual theme of Peter’s apostolic mission chosen for the day)[33]. Pope Leo XIV, vested in white and gold Eucharistic vestments, took his place at the altar, flanked by cardinals and bishops, as the congregation’s singing of the litany resounded across the square[34].
At approximately 10:00 am, the Mass formally began. Pope Leo opened with the sign of the cross greeted in Latin – “In nomine Patris et Filii et Spiritus Sancti” – and a sprinkling of holy water over himself and the people, a rite of aspersion especially fitting for a Sunday in the Easter season[35][23]. The penitential rite was omitted (as is customary in such festive liturgies during Eastertide), and the Gloria was sung. The opening prayer (Collect) made reference to St. Peter’s role as the rock upon which the Church is built, setting the tone for the scriptural readings to follow[23][36].
Liturgy of the Word: The readings chosen for the inauguration Mass all highlighted St. Peter’s ministry and the pope’s role as his successor. The First Reading was taken from the Acts of the Apostles (Acts 4:8–12) and was proclaimed in Spanish[37]. In that passage, Peter boldly testifies that Jesus Christ is “the stone rejected by the builders, which has become the cornerstone.” The Responsorial Psalm (Psalm 118) was sung in Italian, repeating the line “The stone rejected by the builders has become the cornerstone” as a refrain[37]. The Second Reading, in Spanish, was chosen from the First Letter of Peter (1 Pt 5:1–5, 10–11), in which the Apostle exhorts church elders to shepherd the flock willingly and humbly, reminding them that “when the chief Shepherd appears, you will receive the unfading crown of glory.” This reading underscored the connection between Peter’s own exhortations and the mission about to be undertaken by his successor in Rome[38].
For the proclamation of the Gospel, an especially symbolic ritual was observed. The Gospel passage was John 21:15–19, wherein the risen Jesus asks Peter three times “Do you love me?” and commands him, “Feed my lambs… Tend my sheep.” In a gesture expressing the universality of the Church, the Gospel was chanted in two languages: first in Latin (the liturgical language of the Roman Church) and then in Greek (the ancient language of the Eastern Churches and the New Testament)[39][40]. This dual proclamation of the Gospel – alternating Latin and Greek – has become a tradition in modern papal inaugurations, symbolizing the unity of East and West under the pope’s pastoral care[39][40]. Pope Leo XIV listened to the Gospel reading while standing and holding the pastoral staff. After the Gospel reading concluded, he silently blessed the open Book of the Gospels, which was then carried to a place of honor[40].
Investiture with the Pallium and Ring: Following the Gospel, the principal rites specific to the inauguration took place in view of the congregation, before the Mass continued. First, Pope Leo XIV was invested with the pallium, the white woolen stole symbolizing a metropolitan archbishop’s authority and, for the Bishop of Rome, his pastoral authority over the universal Church[41][42]. The pallium was imposed on the Pope’s shoulders by an attending Cardinal-Deacon, reflecting the traditional role of the senior deacon of the College of Cardinals in such ceremonies[43]. (In this case, Cardinal Dominique Mamberti, the Protodeacon, had earlier announced Habemus Papam at the conclave and likely fulfilled the pallium role.) The pallium bestowed on Leo XIV was a narrow band of white lamb’s wool decorated with six black crosses, with one end draping in front and one in back; it recalls the image of the Good Shepherd carrying a sheep and signifies the pope’s duty to “feed His sheep” in unity with all bishops[41][42].
Next, the Fisherman’s Ring was presented to the Pope. A Cardinal-Bishop (the highest order of cardinals) approached and placed the ring on Leo XIV’s right hand[43]. This gold Petrine ring bears the image of St. Peter hauling a fishing net, along with the new pope’s name in Latin around the edge[44]. The Fisherman’s Ring, used historically as a papal seal, symbolizes the pontiff’s role as fisher of men and successor to Peter’s apostolic mission[44]. On this occasion, one of the cardinals participating in the rite was Cardinal Luis Antonio Tagle of the Philippines – himself a cardinal-bishop and a prominent leader from the global South – who had the honor of sliding the ring onto Pope Leo’s finger[45]. As the ring was conferred, many in the crowd cheered, aware that this was the visible seal of the papacy being bestowed. Alongside the pallium and ring, a third senior prelate (from the order of Cardinal Priests) then offered a solemn prayer for the Holy Spirit to strengthen the new Pope in his mission[43][46]. This prayer invoked Christ, the “Shepherd and Bishop of our souls,” asking that Leo XIV be given wisdom and charity in guiding the Church[47].
These rites concluded with Pope Leo XIV lifting the Book of the Gospels and using it to bless the assembly, a gesture symbolizing the Word of God at the heart of the Church’s life[48]. At that moment, the choir sang out the acclamation “Ad multos annos!” (Greek for “Many years!”) in hope for a long and fruitful pontificate[48]. The newly installed Pope was visibly moved during these investiture ceremonies, appearing prayerful and humble as he assumed the liturgical emblems of his office[49].
Act of Obedience: In a departure from earlier papal inaugurations, the act of obedience to the new pope was performed not by the entire College of Cardinals, but by a diverse group of twelve individuals representing the People of God. After the pallium and ring were given, twelve representatives – chosen to reflect a broad cross-section of the worldwide Church – approached Pope Leo XIV one by one[50]. This group included cardinals, bishops, priests, a deacon, men and women religious, and lay faithful from various continents[6][7]. Each representative knelt before the Pope, kissed his ring or clasped his hand, and professed their loyalty and prayerful support on behalf of all the Church’s members. This symbolic rite of obedience underlined the unity between the pope and the faithful of every state in life, emphasizing that the Bishop of Rome serves the whole people of God, not only the hierarchy. It was a notable change from the 2013 inauguration (for Pope Francis), where only six cardinals performed the obedience gesture – the inclusion of laity in 2025 manifested the more synodal, inclusive vision that had been encouraged in recent years[6][7].
With the investiture and obedience rites completed, the Mass continued. Pope Leo XIV then went to the microphone to deliver his homily – his first major sermon as pope – which outlined the spiritual priorities of his nascent pontificate (see Homily section below). The congregation listened in attentive silence, broken at times by applause when the Pope stressed themes of unity, peace, and charity.
After the papal homily, the Nicene Creed (Profession of Faith) was sung in Latin by all present. The Prayer of the Faithful (Universal Prayer) followed, with intentions announced in a variety of world languages, reflecting the global scope of the Church[51]. According to the prepared liturgy, petitions were offered in Portuguese, French, Arabic, Polish, and Chinese, praying for, respectively: the Church’s mission, the new Pope, civil authorities and the common good, those who suffer or are oppressed, and the unity and peace of the human family[51]. Each intercession was followed by a response in Latin.
Liturgy of the Eucharist: The offertory gifts of bread and wine were then brought forward by lay people representing different continents and placed on the altar. During the offertory, a Latin hymn “Tu es Petrus” (“You are Peter”) or “Tu es pastor ovium” (“You are the shepherd of the sheep”) was sung, linking Peter’s mandate to the Eucharistic sacrifice about to be celebrated[52]. Pope Leo XIV proceeded to celebrate the Eucharistic Prayer; he chose to use the Roman Canon (First Eucharistic Prayer), the most traditional form, underscoring continuity with the liturgical heritage of the Roman pontiffs[52]. A multitude of cardinals, bishops, and priests – estimated at over 100 concelebrants – joined him around the altar or from their places in the sanctuary. At the moment of consecration, the bells of St. Peter’s rang out and the vast crowd fell silent in prayer.
Communion was distributed to the faithful in the square by hundreds of priests and deacons, as the choir sang hymns in Latin and Italian. After Communion, the Pope offered a final prayer that God would strengthen the Church in charity and protect him and all the flock entrusted to his care[53].
Concluding Rites: Before the conclusion of the Mass, Pope Leo XIV made brief remarks of thanks and greeted the assembly (this was in place of the usual post-Communion Angelus address given by popes at regular papal Masses). Since it was the Easter season, the Pope then led the recitation of the Regina Cæli (“Queen of Heaven”), the Marian anthem traditionally said instead of the Angelus between Easter and Pentecost[22]. Following the Regina Cæli, Pope Leo imparted the solemn Apostolic Blessing to all in attendance and to “all men and women of good will” watching or listening through media. In this blessing, he invoked imagery of the Church as a vine in God’s vineyard, praying, “May the Lord, who has planted this vine, watch over it and make it grow, and may His face shine upon you”, a paraphrase of Psalm 80 used in papal liturgies[22]. The crowd responded with a hearty “Amen” and cheers, officially concluding the inauguration Mass at around noon local time.
As the bells of St. Peter’s Basilica pealed joyfully, the choir and congregation sang the Easter hymn “Regina Cæli, lætare, Alleluia.” The newly inaugurated Pope lingered on the platform for a moment, gazing out over the cheering throng and waving. He then descended the steps, greeted some of the front-row attendees, and was escorted back into St. Peter’s Basilica. The liturgical ceremonies on the square had ended, but an additional protocol aspect was yet to take place inside the basilica (see Aftermath below).
## Homily
During the inauguration Mass, Pope Leo XIV delivered a homily that outlined the guiding principles of his papacy, centering on the themes of love, unity, and mission. Early in his sermon, he humbly described himself as coming “with fear and trembling” to assume Peter’s role, and said he wished to be “a brother… the servant of [the people’s] faith and joy” on a common journey in God’s love[54][55]. He then emphasized two “dimensions” that Jesus entrusted to St. Peter – love and unity – which would also define Leo’s own ministry[55][56].
Pope Leo XIV stressed his desire for a unified Church that can be a force for reconciliation in the world. “Brothers and sisters, I would like that our first great desire be for a united Church, a sign of unity and communion, which becomes a leaven for a reconciled world,” he proclaimed[57][58]. Noting that humanity in the present time suffers “too much discord, too many wounds caused by hatred, violence, prejudice” and exploitative economics, the Pope urged Catholics to be agents of healing and harmony[59]. “For our part, we want to be a small leaven of unity, communion, and fraternity within the world,” he said, “We want to say to the world, with humility and joy: Look to Christ… in the one Christ, we are one”[59]. He extended this call to ecumenical and interreligious outreach, inviting “our sister Christian churches, those who follow other religious paths, and all people of good will” to join in building a world “where peace reigns.”[60][61] Such words signaled that Leo XIV sees the unity of Christians and cooperation with other faiths as integral to his mission of peacemaking.
The Pope repeatedly returned to the primacy of love as the core of the Gospel message. Partway through the homily, he declared with emphasis: “Brothers and sisters, this is the hour for love!”[62]. He explained that “the heart of the Gospel is the love of God that makes us brothers and sisters”, and that only by being rooted in God’s love can the Church fulfill its role as the Body of Christ[62]. Pope Leo drew inspiration from his namesake Leo XIII, at one point quoting Leo XIII’s observation (from Rerum Novarum 21) asking whether, “if [true charity] were to prevail in the world, would not every conflict cease and peace return?”[62]. By citing this line, Leo XIV connected the pursuit of social peace in the modern era with the consistent teaching of the Church on charity and justice.
In practical terms, the Pope spoke about a “missionary Church” that must not remain self-referential or divided into factions. “We are called to offer God’s love to everyone, to achieve a unity that does not cancel out differences but values each person’s history and culture,” he said[63]. He warned against the Church becoming closed in “small groups” or feeling superior, instead urging a spirit of service and openness[63]. Leo XIV underlined that the authority of the Church, and of the Pope himself, lies in the charity of Christ and in self-giving service: “It is never a question of capturing others by force or by propaganda… but always and only of loving as Jesus did,” he noted, echoing the model of Peter who was asked to love “more” and feed Christ’s sheep[64][65].
Toward the conclusion of the homily, Pope Leo XIV made specific appeals for peace in troubled regions of the world. He invoked the ongoing conflicts and humanitarian crises, mentioning in particular the war in Ukraine, strife in the Middle East (including Gaza), and turmoil in Myanmar, and he implored that a “just and lasting peace” take root in those lands[66][67]. These references showed continuity with Pope Francis’s constant advocacy for peace in those areas. Leo XIV prayed that the Church, through its witness of unity and charity, could help bridge divides: “Unity and peace with all is the goal of the Church… let us walk together as one people, brothers and sisters, toward a world of peace,” he urged in closing[68][69]. The homily was met with warm applause, especially when the Pope spoke about unity in the Church and reaching out to the peripheries. Vatican commentators described the address as setting a tone of pastoral warmth and continuity – “reminiscent of Francis in its call for a church of open arms,” noted one media outlet – while also emphasizing Leo XIV’s personal motto of love as the remedy for a divided world[70][71]. The line “this is the hour for love” quickly became the most quoted phrase from the homily in news reports, capturing the new Pope’s message in a single declaration.
## Attendees and Dignitaries
The papal inauguration of Leo XIV drew a large and diverse audience, including both ordinary pilgrims and high-ranking dignitaries from around the globe. St. Peter’s Square and the surrounding area were completely filled, with estimates of about 200,000 people in attendance at the Mass[8]. Among the throngs were clergy, religious, and lay faithful representing all parts of the international Catholic community. Many attendees waved national flags – observers noted banners from countries as varied as Peru (where Leo XIV had served as a bishop), Samoa, the Philippines, Poland, the United States, and countless others[26]. Priests and seminarians from Roman colleges were present in their cassocks, and numerous Catholic orders of nuns and friars sent members in their distinctive habits, adding to the colorful tableau.
About 200 official delegations from foreign states and international organizations attended the inauguration, reflecting the diplomatic importance of the papacy on the world stage[9]. Dozens of heads of state, heads of government, royalty, and ministers were present in the VIP sections. Notable attendees from Europe included King Felipe VI and Queen Letizia of Spain, who sat alongside other royals such as Prince Albert II and Princess Charlène of Monaco, Grand Duke Henri of Luxembourg and his spouse Maria Teresa, and Princess Victoria of Sweden[72]. Many European countries sent their top political leaders: for example, Andrzej Duda, the President of Poland, Marcelo Rebelo de Sousa, the President of Portugal, Friedrich Merz, the Chancellor of Germany, and Ursula von der Leyen, President of the European Commission, were all in attendance in the front rows of the state delegations[73][74]. The presence of such figures underscored the significance that governments attach to relations with the Holy See. From the Pope’s home country, the United States, a high-profile delegation was sent, led by Vice President J. D. Vance and Secretary of State Marco Rubio (both of whom are Catholic)[75]. Their attendance was notable not only because Leo XIV is the first American pope, but also because it marked an occasion of unity beyond domestic politics – members of both major U.S. political parties praised the election, and the delegation signaled U.S. respect for the papacy. Other Western Hemisphere leaders present included Justin Trudeau, the Prime Minister of Canada (represented in some reports by Mark Carney as a special envoy), Santiago Peña of Paraguay, Dina Boluarte of Peru, and the vice-presidents of countries like Brazil, Argentina, and Cuba[76][77].
Representatives from the Middle East, Africa, and Asia also attended in significant numbers. The President of Israel, Isaac Herzog, and Prince Ghazi bin Muhammad of Jordan were among those in the Middle Eastern delegation, as were officials from predominately Muslim nations such as Bahrain, Iran, and others, highlighting the interfaith respect for the Pope’s inauguration[78][79]. From Africa, heads of state or senior ministers of countries including Nigeria, Senegal, Congo, Uganda, and Zimbabwe attended; for instance, Nigerian President Bola Tinubu was present, as was the Vice President of Zimbabwe and the Prime Minister of Mozambique[80][81]. The Prime Minister of Australia, Anthony Albanese, led the delegation from Oceania, and many Asian countries sent envoys as well: notable were Tarō Asō, a former Prime Minister representing Japan, and Chen Chien-jen, the former Vice President of Taiwan (himself Catholic), representing Taiwan’s leadership[82][83].
Also in attendance were leaders of international organizations and Christian churches. The event saw the presence of delegations from the Orthodox Churches, including, reportedly, a representative of the Ecumenical Patriarch of Constantinople (continuing the historic gesture from 2013 when Patriarch Bartholomew attended Pope Francis’s inauguration). High-ranking clergy from other denominations – such as the Archbishop of Canterbury’s envoy from the Anglican Communion, and moderators from Protestant and Orthodox communities – were seated prominently, as were representatives of non-Christian faiths (for example, rabbinical representatives, Muslim imams, and other religious figures)[84]. Their attendance emphasized the Vatican’s role in global interreligious dialogue and the hopes for cooperation under the new pope.
Overall, the congregation for Leo XIV’s installation Mass was among the largest for any papal event in recent years, comparable to the turnout for Pope Francis’s inauguration. The mix of flags, national costumes, and religious habits in St. Peter’s Square highlighted the Catholic Church’s diversity and global reach. The United Nations Secretary-General (or his representative) and numerous ambassadors accredited to the Holy See were also on hand. After the Mass, some world leaders commented on the occasion; for example, the President of the United States issued a statement calling the event “a testament to the enduring significance of the papacy for peace and dialogue,” while European and Latin American leaders praised Leo XIV’s homily message of unity. Many attendees, from heads of state to ordinary pilgrims, described being deeply moved by witnessing the spiritual and historical pageantry of a papal inauguration up close.
## Aftermath and Subsequent Events
Following the conclusion of the inauguration Mass, Pope Leo XIV did not immediately depart the scene. In keeping with recent custom, the new Pope went back inside St. Peter’s Basilica to personally greet and receive the delegations of rulers and religious leaders who had attended. A brief reception line was organized in the nave of the basilica, near the Altar of the Confession, where the Pope exchanged handshakes, hugs, or a few words with each delegation in turn[85][86]. This post-Mass greeting is an act of diplomatic and ecumenical courtesy rather than a liturgical rite. For about 40 minutes, Leo XIV met heads of state, including kings, presidents, and prime ministers, thanking them for coming. In a notable encounter, he had a short private conversation with President Volodymyr Zelenskyy of Ukraine, who had traveled to the Vatican for the inauguration amidst the ongoing war in his country[87]. The Pope, who has been vocal about seeking peace in Ukraine, reportedly assured President Zelenskyy of the Holy See’s prayers and support for efforts toward a just peace[66]. No lengthy bilateral meetings were held that day (consistent with Vatican protocol to avoid formal diplomacy during a strictly religious event), but these brief exchanges set a positive tone for Leo XIV’s relationships with world leaders.
After greeting all the delegations, Pope Leo XIV returned to the Apostolic Palace. Unusually, the Pope did not make another circuit in the popemobile after Mass (Vatican officials had announced in advance that a second tour was not planned)[85][88], likely due to the extensive interaction he already had and security considerations with many VIPs present. Nonetheless, as he left, he spontaneously stopped to bless and kiss a few babies and to clasp the hands of some disabled individuals in the front rows, drawing cheers from the remaining crowd[89]. By early afternoon, St. Peter’s Square gradually cleared as the faithful departed, many jubilant at having witnessed the historic event.
In the days that followed the inauguration, Pope Leo XIV carried out additional ceremonies to formally take possession of his roles in the Diocese of Rome. On May 20, 2025, he visited the Basilica of St. Paul Outside the Walls in Rome, one of the four major papal basilicas, for a prayer service with the Benedictine community there[90]. On May 25, 2025, Pope Leo XIV officially took possession of the Archbasilica of St. John Lateran, which is the Cathedral of Rome and the traditional seat of the Bishop of Rome[91]. In a solemn enthronement ritual at St. John Lateran, the Pope sat in the Cathedra Romana (the bishop’s chair) and the Lateran Chapter (the clergy of the cathedral) welcomed him, symbolizing the moment he assumes pastoral governance of the Diocese of Rome. That same day he also made a visit to the Basilica of St. Mary Major, where he prayed before the icon of Salus Populi Romani (a beloved image of the Virgin Mary) and entrusted his pontificate to the maternal care of the Blessed Virgin[90]. These events rounded out the inaugural period of the new papacy.
Leo XIV’s inauguration was widely seen as a success, blending grand tradition with a few innovative touches. Vatican commentators noted that the inclusion of laypeople in the obedience rite and the multilingual prayers highlighted the Pope’s commitment to a more inclusive and international Church[7][51]. Many faithful were struck by the sight of a pope from the New World ascending the throne of Peter and by his clear message of love and unity. The first American pope’s installation Mass thus entered the annals of Church history as a moment of both continuity – carrying forward rituals dating back centuries – and change, marking new milestones (such as the American papacy) and hopeful directions for the Catholic Church’s future under Leo XIV’s leadership.
