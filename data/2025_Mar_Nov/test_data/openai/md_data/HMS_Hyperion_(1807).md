# HMS Hyperion (1807)
HMS Hyperion was a 32-gun fifth-rate frigate of the Royal Navy, launched in 1807 and broken up in 1833[1]. The ship was built to a design based on the captured French frigate Magicienne (launched 1778 and taken by the Royal Navy in 1781)[2]. Intended as an 18-pounder armed frigate, Hyperion served during the Napoleonic Wars and the War of 1812, notably capturing an American privateer in 1814. After the wars, she was employed in suppressing the slave trade, anti-piracy patrols, and coastguard anti-smuggling duties, before being decommissioned in 1831 and subsequently dismantled.
## Design and Construction
Hyperion was ordered in June 1805 and built under contract by shipbuilder William Gibson at the Kingston shipyard in Hull, Yorkshire. Her design was overseen by Sir John Henslow, and it closely followed the lines of the French 36-gun frigate Magicienne (1778) which had been captured by Britain in 1781[2]. Hyperion was launched on 3 November 1807 and then fitted out for service at Chatham Dockyard, with work completed by April 1808.
As a fifth-rate frigate, Hyperion was rated for 32 guns and was armed primarily with 18-pounder long guns on her main deck[2]. She measured approximately 978 tons burthen and had an overall gundeck length of about 144 feet (44 m). The ship’s complement was around 250 officers and men (254 in 1808) when fully manned.
## Napoleonic Wars (1808–1815)
After commissioning in 1808 under Captain Thomas Charles Brodie, Hyperion joined the Royal Navy’s Mediterranean fleet. Upon completing her outfitting, she sailed for the Mediterranean on 29 June 1808 to take part in naval operations against Napoleonic France. In this theater the frigate served in patrolling and convoy escort roles. On 14 January 1810, Hyperion was reassigned to the West Indies, departing Britain for the Jamaica Station, where she protected British interests and shipping in the Caribbean during the ongoing wars.
In May 1811, Captain William Pryce Cumby (noted for his role at Trafalgar) took command of Hyperion[3]. Under Cumby’s command, the frigate was active in the Atlantic during the War of 1812 between Britain and the United States. By mid-1812, Hyperion had been sent to North American waters, sailing for Newfoundland in May 1812 just as hostilities with the U.S. began. She operated off the Canadian coast and Atlantic trade routes, safeguarding convoys and pursuing American privateers. In early 1814, Hyperion was attached to a convoy carrying British troops and militia reinforcements to the continent – in March–April 1814 she escorted transports in the Bay of Biscay destined for the Garonne River, supporting the Duke of Wellington’s campaign in southwestern France.
On 3 June 1814, Hyperion achieved a notable success when she captured the United States privateer brig Rattlesnake, which was armed with 16 guns. The captured Rattlesnake was sent into Plymouth as a prize. For the remainder of 1814 and into 1815, Hyperion continued patrolling in the English Channel and the approaches to the Atlantic. After the Napoleonic Wars concluded, the ship was paid off into ordinary (reserve) at Portsmouth in August 1815.
## Post-war Service (1818–1833)
With the return of peace, Hyperion underwent a refit and was recommissioned in 1818. The frigate was assigned to overseas duty on Britain’s colonial stations. In 1819–1820 Hyperion deployed to the South American station, calling at Rio de Janeiro in October 1819 and rounding Cape Horn to Valparaíso, Chile by mid-1820. During this period, the Royal Navy was enforcing the abolition of the slave trade. The Admiralty authorized Hyperion’s officers (along with other ships on the South America Station) to detain Portuguese or Spanish vessels suspected of illegal slave trafficking under international treaties.
In late 1821, Hyperion carried Lord Charles Somerset, the newly appointed Governor of the Cape of Good Hope, from England to South Africa. After delivering the governor at Cape Town, the ship was intended to continue onward to the East Indies once relieved on the Cape station. By 1822 Hyperion was operating in the West Indies: in December 1822 her crew seized the schooner General Montillo, a pirate vessel, and brought its crew to Kingston, Jamaica, where they were jailed on charges of piracy.
The frigate returned to British waters by 1825 and thereafter was employed in home waters on “Coast Blockade” duty. From 1825 to 1831, Hyperion served as part of the Coast Blockade (a naval anti-smuggling patrol) based at Newhaven on the Sussex coast. In this role, the ship’s officers and crew worked to intercept contraband being smuggled by sea. Hyperion seized numerous smuggling vessels and illegal cargoes (including shipments of spirits and tea), for which prize money and rewards were periodically distributed to her crew. Captain William James Mingaye commanded Hyperion during her later years on this station.
By early 1831 the Coast Blockade was wound down and Hyperion was finally withdrawn from service. The frigate was paid off and laid up at Portsmouth in May 1831, ending her active career.
## Fate
After over two decades of service, HMS Hyperion was disposed of by the Navy. In June 1833, the worn-out frigate was broken up at Portsmouth Dockyard.
