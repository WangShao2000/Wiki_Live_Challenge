# Dorfromantik (board game)
Dorfromantik: The Board Game (German: Dorfromantik – Das Brettspiel, lit. "village romance") is a 2022 cooperative tile-placement board game designed by Michael Palm and Lukas Zach, and published by Pegasus Spiele[1][2]. It is an adaptation of the acclaimed 2021 video game Dorfromantik by Toukana Interactive[3]. In the game, one to six players work together to place hexagonal landscape tiles and fulfill specific tasks in order to score points while building an idyllic countryside[4]. The board game has no strict losing condition; players achieve a final score each session and can embark on a multi-session campaign to unlock additional content and challenges[5][6]. Dorfromantik: The Board Game was highly praised and won the 2023 Spiel des Jahres (Game of the Year) award, marking the first time a video game adaptation has won that prestigious board game award[7].
## Gameplay
Dorfromantik: The Board Game is a fully cooperative, family-friendly tile-laying game. The components include a set of hexagonal landscape tiles showing different terrain types (such as forests, fields of grain, villages, railway tracks, and rivers) and a set of task tiles which indicate goals to achieve[8][9]. At the start of each game, a few task tiles are placed face-up on the table; each task might require forming a contiguous area of a certain terrain type or other specific configurations (for example, connecting a cluster of forest tiles of a given size)[8]. On each turn, the active player draws and places a single tile, with the group collectively deciding how to position it. If fewer than three task tiles are currently in play, the player must draw a task tile to add a new goal; otherwise, they draw a landscape tile to extend the map[8]. All tiles placed must adhere to simple placement rules – for instance, tracks and rivers cannot abruptly end against incompatible terrain types on adjacent tiles[9].
As the players expand the landscape, they aim to complete the task tiles’ requirements and create coherent features. Points are scored for each task tile fulfilled by the end of the game, as well as bonus points for other achievements: completing large areas marked with flag symbols and building the longest continuous railway line and the longest river on the board[10]. The game continues until the supply of landscape tiles is exhausted. There is no way to lose; every game ends with a final score rather than a win/lose outcome[5]. Players are encouraged to replay cooperatively to improve their high score.
A major feature of Dorfromantik: The Board Game is its campaign system. After each session, the collective score is recorded on a campaign progress track (often depicted as a meandering path on a campaign sheet)[11]. Reaching certain score thresholds allows the group to open one of five sealed boxes included in the game, each containing new game components such as additional tiles, new task cards, or other rule tweaks[11][6]. These legacy-like elements gradually introduce fresh challenges and scoring opportunities, increasing the game’s complexity and difficulty over multiple plays[6]. The campaign is replayable; the game can be reset so that new groups of players can start from the beginning, or players can choose to start over and explore different paths of unlocking content[6]. A complete campaign yields a fully expanded set of tiles and tasks, but even after all content is unlocked the game remains playable as a high-score challenge with all components available.
The game supports 1 to 6 players (a solo mode is possible by a single player making all decisions) and is recommended for ages 8 and up[12]. A typical session lasts around 30–60 minutes[13], though a full campaign spans several sessions. The cooperative nature of the gameplay means players discuss and plan moves together. The absence of any competitive element or timer gives Dorfromantik a relaxed, “zen” puzzle quality similar to its digital counterpart[14]. Reviewers have noted that this calm, low-pressure experience makes the game inviting to a broad audience, including those new to board gaming or fans of the original video game[15].
## Development and release
The board game Dorfromantik was developed by Pegasus Spiele under license from Toukana Interactive, the creators of the PC video game. Michael Palm and Lukas Zach, a German board game design duo known for titles such as The Dwarves and Bang! The Dice Game, were responsible for translating the video game’s concept into a tabletop format[16]. Notably, the video game’s original art style by illustrator Paul Riebe was retained in the board game, providing visual continuity between the two mediums[17]. The designers and Toukana’s team opted to make the board game a cooperative experience in line with the video game’s laid-back, single-player nature, rather than introducing competitive mechanics[18]. They also implemented a “resettable campaign” structure – drawing inspiration from the video game’s progression system of unlocking new tiles and biomes – to keep players engaged across multiple plays[19].
Pegasus Spiele officially announced Dorfromantik – The Board Game in mid-2022, describing it as a “zen” cooperative strategy puzzle and confirming it would feature a campaign with sealed content[20][6]. The game debuted at the Essen Spiel trade fair in October 2022, where Pegasus released it to the public for the first time[2]. It was made available in both German and English editions, among other languages, by Pegasus and its partners, with a recommended retail price around €35[21]. The initial reception at Essen was positive, as many players noted the board game successfully captured the charm of the digital version’s peaceful landscape-building gameplay. By the end of 2022, Dorfromantik: The Board Game had begun appearing in retail stores in Europe and North America[14][22].
In developing the adaptation, Palm and Zach incorporated the core tile-placement mechanics of the video game (placing hexagonal pieces to form continuous terrains) and its scoring objectives, while simplifying certain aspects for tabletop play. For example, unlike the endless play of the video game, the board game uses a finite stack of tiles to impose a natural end to each session[23][5]. Because players cannot earn additional tiles during play (whereas the video game awards extra tiles for completing tasks), the designers balanced the difficulty to ensure that most tasks can be completed within the fixed tile supply, and any remaining tasks simply go unfinished when the game ends[24]. This design choice means that Dorfromantik on the tabletop becomes a relaxed optimization puzzle with no risk of failure – reinforcing the “feel-good” atmosphere emphasized by the Spiel des Jahres jury[15]. The board game’s rulesheet credits the Toukana Interactive team for the original concept, but Palm and Zach are credited as the board game’s authors, reflecting their role in adapting the gameplay to a cooperative multiplayer format[17].
## Reception
Upon release, Dorfromantik: The Board Game received a warm critical response, and its popularity surged after it earned Germany’s Spiel des Jahres 2023 award (announced in July 2023)[1]. The Spiel des Jahres jury praised Dorfromantik as a “co-operative, feel-good game” that “takes the pressure out of everyday life”, noting that it provides new goals each play without ever pushing players out of their comfort zone[25]. The jurors highlighted how even as tasks become slightly more challenging over time, the game remains gentle and relaxing, making it suitable for families and casual gamers. Dorfromantik’s win was historic for the award – it became the first board game based on a video game to be crowned Game of the Year, and also the first winner with legacy-style campaign elements (i.e. unlockable content) as part of its design[7]. These milestones were seen as a sign of the board gaming hobby’s evolving scope, bringing in ideas from digital games and innovative mechanics in a genre traditionally dominated by standalone, non-digital designs[7].
Reviewers in the board gaming press have largely lauded Dorfromantik: The Board Game. Justin Bell of Meeple Mountain described it as a game that “combines an incredibly simple teach and infinite replayability”, thanks to its flexible modes (solo, multiplayer, one-off or campaign play) and gradually expanding content[26]. He noted that the straightforward rules make it easy to introduce to new players, yet the scoring challenges and unlockables keep it engaging over many sessions. David W., writing for The Daily Worker Placement, declared Dorfromantik “one of the best ports of a videogame ever” in board game form, praising how it preserves the strategic puzzle feel of the digital version and delivers a smooth, accessible experience for a broad audience[27]. He commended the high quality rulebook and components, and suggested the game has potential to attract video gamers into the board game hobby by virtue of its recognizable theme and cooperative play[28]. Several outlets have also favorably compared Dorfromantik to the classic tile-layer Carcassonne, noting that both games involve building landscapes from tiles, though Dorfromantik is fully cooperative and focuses on collective high scores rather than competition[13].
Critics have offered a few caveats. Because the game is cooperative with open information, some groups might experience the “quarterbacking” or Pandemic effect – where one dominant player directs others’ moves in a collaborative game[29]. The lack of a fail state means Dorfromantik is more of a relaxed activity than a tense game, which may not appeal to players seeking a challenge or direct player interaction. Nonetheless, most reviews regard these aspects as minor trade-offs for the intended calm tone of the game. By the end of 2023, Dorfromantik: The Board Game had become a commercial success in Europe, buoyed by the Spiel des Jahres win, and it introduced many new players to both tabletop gaming and the original video game. The game’s crossover success has been cited as an example of the growing synergy between video games and board games, as well as the continued popularity of cooperative and campaign-style board games[7].
## Expansions and spin-offs
Following the success of the base game, Pegasus Spiele and the designers expanded the Dorfromantik board game series with additional content and standalone titles. In August 2023, a small add-on titled The Great Mill (Die große Mühle) was released as a mini-expansion for Dorfromantik: The Board Game. It introduced an acrylic windmill figure and two new task cards, adding a minor gameplay element that can be mixed into the base game[30]. Later in 2023, Pegasus launched Dorfromantik – The Duel, a standalone spin-off that reimagines the peaceful gameplay as a light competitive experience[31]. Dorfromantik – The Duel is designed for two players (or two teams) who each use identical sets of tiles; on each turn both sides place the same tile simultaneously, striving to complete tasks and arrange their landscape more optimally than the opponent[31]. Despite the head-to-head format, The Duel retains the core tile-placement and task completion concepts of the original, and even includes two new task types that can optionally be added into the cooperative base game[30].
In 2024, Pegasus Spiele released Dorfromantik – Sakura, a standalone cooperative sequel with a fresh theme inspired by Japanese scenery[32]. Dorfromantik – Sakura applies the same fundamental mechanics of tile placement and campaign progression, but features new landscape elements like cherry blossom orchards, rice paddies, and stone pathways in place of the European countryside of the original game[32]. The Sakura edition offers a new set of scenarios and tasks, demonstrating how the Dorfromantik system can be rethemed and expanded upon while maintaining its relaxing gameplay.
Continuing the line, in late 2025 Pegasus Spiele introduced Dorfromantik – Light Luggage, a travel-friendly version of the game packaged in a compact magnetic-clasp box[33][34]. Light Luggage distills the cooperative campaign experience into a smaller format for one to four players, using scaled-down tiles and a high-score log in lieu of a sprawling campaign map[35][36]. Players unlock new components by surpassing score thresholds, similar to the base game’s campaign, and the box even includes a few new materials that owners of the original board game can integrate as additional content[36]. This portable edition aimed to make Dorfromantik more accessible for travel and quick play sessions, without losing the core charm of peaceful landscape building.
As of 2025, the Dorfromantik board game series encompasses the original cooperative board game and its mini-expansion, a two-player competitive variant, a sequel with a different setting, and a compact edition. The ongoing support and spin-offs underscore the game’s popularity and the versatility of its design in appealing to different audiences.
