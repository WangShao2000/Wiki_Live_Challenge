# Voiceverse NFT plagiarism scandal
## Background and Context
Voiceverse NFT was an NFT-based voice technology startup that launched in early 2022. Backed by members of the popular Bored Ape Yacht Club NFT collective, the company marketed itself as providing “the world’s first Voice NFTs” – unique AI-generated voices that users could own and use for creating content[1]. The premise was that a voice actor would record a voice model, mint it as a non-fungible token, and the NFT purchaser would gain perpetual access to that AI voice for use in videos, games, podcasts, and other media. Voiceverse claimed this system would allow creators to “own & invest in the IPs they create” with the voice and even proposed paying royalties to the original voice actors[2][3].
The Voice NFT concept emerged amid a broader NFT boom but immediately raised ethical and practical concerns. Critics pointed out that selling AI-generated voices could undercut human voice actors’ job opportunities, since studios might reuse an NFT voice in place of hiring talent[3]. Others noted the risk of misuse – for example, using a purchased voice to make someone appear to say offensive or fraudulent things. Additionally, like other NFT projects of the time, Voiceverse faced skepticism over the vague utility of its product and the environmental impact of NFTs (which at the time often relied on energy-intensive blockchains)[3]. These factors set the stage for controversy when Voiceverse began promoting its service.
## Troy Baker’s Involvement and Initial Backlash
On January 14, 2022, Troy Baker – one of the video game industry’s most famous voice actors, known for roles such as Joel in The Last of Us – announced he was partnering with Voiceverse NFT. In a tweet that morning, Baker wrote: “I’m partnering with @VoiceverseNFT to bring new tools to new creators … allowing everyone a chance to own & invest in the IP’s they create. … We all have a story to tell. You can hate. Or you can create. What’ll it be?”[4][5]. Baker’s endorsement instantly drew widespread backlash from fans and fellow creators. The negative reaction was nearly universal[6]. Many in the gaming and voice acting community were alarmed that a prominent voice actor was supporting a technology that could potentially automate his own profession’s work[6]. Others took offense at the phrasing “You can hate or you can create,” viewing it as an antagonistic dismissal of any criticism. Within hours, Baker’s tweet was heavily “ratioed” (receiving far more critical replies than likes) and became a lightning rod in the ongoing debate over NFTs in gaming.
Baker quickly responded to the outcry. About nine hours after his initial post, he apologized for his wording, admitting the “hate/create” line “might have been a bit antagonistic”[7][8]. He tweeted that he had “heard” the feedback and was sorry for “accusing anyone of ‘hating’ just by simply disagreeing” with him[9][10]. However, at that point he still had not withdrawn from the partnership. Meanwhile, Voiceverse NFT’s official Twitter account attempted its own damage control. The company posted a thread explaining that their Voice NFTs were meant to “open up a new stream of opportunities for voice actors, not replace them,” and promised that the original voice providers would receive royalties from NFT sales[11][12]. These assurances did little to quell the skepticism. As media outlets noted, the sentiment around Baker’s announcement remained overwhelmingly negative – combining general NFT backlash with fears that Voiceverse’s “voice cloning” concept could exploit artists and degrade the voice acting industry[6][3].
## Plagiarism Allegations
Just as the partnership controversy was unfolding, Voiceverse NFT became embroiled in an even more damaging scandal – an accusation of plagiarism involving its core technology. On the same day as Baker’s announcement, an AI researcher known by the pseudonym “15” (creator of the free text-to-speech platform 15.ai) came forward with allegations against Voiceverse. The 15.ai service, launched in 2020 at MIT, allows users to generate speech in the voices of various fictional characters for non-commercial purposes. The developer, Fifteen, had explicitly forbidden any commercial or NFT use of outputs from 15.ai – in fact, in December 2021 they publicly stated, “I have no interest in incorporating NFTs into any aspect of my work. Please stop asking.”[13].
On January 14, 2022, Fifteen announced on Twitter that they had evidence Voiceverse was “actively attempting to appropriate my work for their own benefit.” Digging through server logs, Fifteen discovered that some voice lines used in Voiceverse’s promotional material were generated using the 15.ai platform[14]. In other words, instead of using an original AI voice as advertised, Voiceverse had apparently taken audio output from 15.ai and repackaged it as part of an NFT demo, without credit or permission. The example in question involved an animated NFT character (an image of a cat from the “Chubbiverse” collection) speaking with a voice that was supposed to demonstrate Voiceverse’s capabilities. Fifteen’s logs indicated that this voice audio had actually been produced by 15.ai’s system, then slightly modified (pitch-shifted) to sound different[14].
When confronted publicly, Voiceverse NFT’s initial reaction on Twitter was defensive. The company’s account posted a now-deleted sarcastic retort to 15.ai’s claim, which provoked further criticism[15]. However, as evidence mounted, Voiceverse quickly reversed its stance and issued a formal apology. In a statement posted to Twitter, Voiceverse admitted that a voice clip from 15.ai had been used in their project: “We are extremely sorry about this. The voice was indeed taken from your platform, which our marketing team used without giving proper credit.”[16]. The company insisted that the core Voiceverse team (specifically a partner NFT project called “Chubbiverse”) had “no knowledge of this” and blamed the incident on an overzealous marketing staff. They pledged, “We will make sure this never happens again.”[16].
The response from 15.ai’s creator was swift and blunt. Fifteen replied publicly to Voiceverse’s apology with a terse message: “Go f yourself.”*[13] (expletive uncensored in original). This profane retort underscored the bad faith and outrage felt by the developer. Not only had Voiceverse plagiarized content from a free tool, but it had done so in pursuit of profit through NFTs – directly violating the ethos of 15.ai. Commentators noted that Voiceverse’s actions were an affront to the 15.ai project’s stated principles, given that Fifteen had gone out of their way to keep their AI voices non-commercial and accessible**[17]. The scandal also illustrated a novel twist on plagiarism in the digital age: an AI company stealing from another AI system, rather than from human-created work, raising questions about intellectual property and ethics between AI platforms[18][19].
## Fallout and Aftermath
The plagiarism revelations compounded the backlash against Voiceverse NFT. Over the next two weeks, the project’s public reputation crumbled. Troy Baker, who initially had doubled down despite criticism, ultimately decided to distance himself. On January 31, 2022, Baker announced that after “careful consideration” he would no longer continue the partnership with VoiceverseNFT[20][21]. He apologized again to fans, acknowledging that his earlier approach had been misguided: “Intentions aside, I’ve heard you and apologize for accusing anyone of ‘hating’ just by simply disagreeing with me.”[21]. Baker’s reversal was headline news in gaming media, given his stature in the industry. Voiceverse, for its part, claimed that the decision to part ways was mutual[22], but the damage to the project’s credibility was already done.
Following these events, Voiceverse NFT’s operations slowed significantly. The scandal tarnished the project’s image in both the NFT community and the AI research community. By late 2022, Voiceverse had largely faded from public view, and there were few signs of it achieving the ambitious “voice metaverse” vision it had touted. The company behind Voiceverse, a San Francisco-based AI startup called LOVO Inc., shifted focus back to its core business of AI voice synthesis (rebranding some services under the name “Genny”). However, controversies continued to follow. In May 2024, LOVO was sued by a group of voice actors in New York, who alleged that the company had illegally cloned their voices and used them in its AI products without permission[23]. The class-action lawsuit accused LOVO of fraud, deceptive business practices, and violating the actors’ publicity rights by creating and selling “AI voices” modeled on real performers[23][24]. As part of the legal case, the plaintiffs highlighted prior incidents of unethical behavior by the company – including the Voiceverse NFT plagiarism scandal – to establish a pattern of misuse of others’ voice work. The lawsuit survived initial challenges; as of mid-2025 a U.S. federal judge allowed several of the voice actors’ claims to proceed in court[25].
In retrospect, the Voiceverse NFT episode became a cautionary tale at the intersection of NFT hype and artificial intelligence. It showcased the risks of attempting to monetize creative AI outputs without proper respect for creators’ rights or the technology’s limitations. The scandal is often cited alongside other early 2020s NFT controversies as an example of “what not to do” in the Web3 and AI space[26][6]. For the voice acting community, it served as a rallying point against unfettered AI appropriation of human artistry. And for observers of technology, it foreshadowed further debates about AI plagiarism – raising questions such as whether content generated by one AI system can be owned or stolen by another. As the Plagiarism Today blog noted in a 2025 retrospective, “Voiceverse didn’t just plagiarize; it failed at the most basic task it was supposed to do” – ironically undermining its own premise by relying on someone else’s AI work[27][28]. The Voiceverse NFT plagiarism scandal thus remains a notable milestone in the evolving conversation about intellectual property, ethics, and accountability in artificial intelligence and digital art.
