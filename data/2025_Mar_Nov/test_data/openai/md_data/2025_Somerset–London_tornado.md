# 2025 Somerset–London tornado
## Summary
In the late evening of May 16, 2025, a long-track EF4 tornado struck parts of southern Kentucky, causing severe damage in and around the cities of Somerset and London. The tornado traveled approximately 55–60 miles across Russell, Pulaski, and Laurel counties with estimated peak winds of 170 mph[1]. It was a large, rain-wrapped nocturnal tornado that devastated several communities along its path. Nineteen people were killed and over a hundred injured, making it the deadliest tornado of 2025 in the United States[2]. Hundreds of structures were destroyed or damaged, particularly in Laurel County, and the event is noted as one of the most intense tornadoes ever recorded in eastern Kentucky[3].
## Meteorological background
On May 16, 2025, a widespread severe weather outbreak impacted parts of the Ohio Valley and Appalachia. Multiple rounds of storms occurred throughout the day. An initial wave of thunderstorms in the morning and early afternoon produced large hail (up to baseball-size), damaging winds, and heavy rain across Kentucky[4]. After a lull in activity by late afternoon that allowed the atmosphere to become highly unstable[5], a second round of intense supercell thunderstorms moved in during the evening. These supercells tracked eastward from the Midwest into Kentucky[6]. One long-lived supercell, originating in Missouri, produced several brief tornadoes and wind damage as it crossed western and central Kentucky[6]. During the late evening, this supercell spawned its most destructive tornado in southern Kentucky. The overall storm system on May 16–17 generated more than two dozen tornadoes across Missouri, Kentucky, Illinois, and Indiana, resulting in at least 28 fatalities in three states (including 19 in Kentucky)[7][8].
## Tornado track and characteristics
Satellite imagery reveals the tornado’s path (brown scar) through forested terrain between Somerset and London, Kentucky. The tornado touched down at approximately 10:27 p.m. EDT on May 16 in far eastern Russell County, Kentucky, about 4 miles southeast of the town of Russell Springs[9]. Initially, it cut a relatively narrow path of damage (rated in the EF2–EF3 range) as it moved eastward. Entering Pulaski County, the tornado struck the southern edge of the city of Somerset, where it caused significant damage to homes and obliterated the Southern Kentucky RECC electric cooperative headquarters[9][10].
As the tornado continued east of Somerset, it intensified rapidly to EF4 strength near the community of Mount Victory in Pulaski County[11]. At peak intensity, winds were estimated near 170 mph and the damage swath expanded to a maximum width of about 1,700 yards (nearly 1 mile)[1][12]. The tornado tore through the rugged Daniel Boone National Forest, snapping and debarking thousands of trees along a clearly visible track[12][13]. Radar evidence indicated debris was lofted at least 27,000 feet into the air by the tornado’s updraft at its most violent stage[12].
Crossing into Laurel County, the tornado maintained strong intensity. It narrowly missed central London, instead ravaging the southern and eastern outskirts of the city. The Sunshine Hill subdivision just southwest of London suffered near-total destruction, with many homes leveled or swept from their foundations[2]. The tornado crossed Interstate 75 and struck the London-Corbin Airport (south of downtown London), where buildings were damaged and a helicopter was destroyed[14]. The twister finally lifted about 6 miles southeast of London around 11:55–11:56 p.m. EDT, after being on the ground for roughly 89 minutes[15]. In total, its continuous path length was measured at just under 60 miles from the touchdown point in Russell County to where it dissipated in Laurel County[1].
## Damage and casualties
Extreme damage in the Sunshine Hill area of Laurel County after the tornado, with homes reduced to debris. The Somerset–London tornado inflicted catastrophic damage along its path, especially in Laurel County. Entire neighborhoods were obliterated, and large swaths of forest were flattened. The Sunshine Hill community on the southwest side of London saw the most severe devastation and loss of life[2]. In many cases, frame houses were completely destroyed with debris strewn far from foundations, consistent with high-end EF4 intensity.
Overall, the tornado caused 19 fatalities and widespread injuries. According to official reports, 17 of the deaths occurred in Laurel County (primarily in the London area), with one person killed in Pulaski County and one in Russell County[2]. This death toll made it the single deadliest tornado in the history of the National Weather Service’s Jackson forecast office, which covers eastern Kentucky[3]. In addition, over one hundred people were injured by the tornado. Local hospitals treated dozens of patients; at least 11 individuals suffered critical or serious injuries[16]. The Sunshine Hill neighborhood alone accounted for a large share of the casualties as many residents were caught without adequate shelter late at night. Damage to infrastructure was immense: in Laurel County, authorities reported 817 homes affected, including 280 homes completely destroyed and nearly 200 others with major damage[17]. Among the victims was a local fire department major in Pulaski County who died after being injured during storm response efforts[18].
Property damage extended across three counties. In Somerset, numerous homes and businesses on the city’s south side were heavily damaged or destroyed[19]. The tornado demolished several rural farmsteads and mobile homes in its path through Pulaski and Laurel counties, and high-tension transmission lines were downed. The London-Corbin Airport saw hangars severely damaged and aircraft wrecked[15]. In the aftermath, aerial and satellite imagery clearly showed a continuous scar of treefall and debris stretching from near Somerset to southeast of London[20]. Early estimates indicated many tens of millions of dollars in losses, though an exact damage tally was not immediately available.
## Aftermath and response
Kentucky Governor Andy Beshear declared a state of emergency in the region, and seven counties along with the city of London issued local emergency declarations in the wake of the tornado and associated storms[21]. Emergency crews and volunteers began search-and-rescue operations through the night of May 16–17, combing through rubble for survivors[22]. The Kentucky National Guard deployed about 70 soldiers to assist local authorities in Laurel and Pulaski counties, providing security patrols and helping with rescue and cleanup efforts[23]. State troopers and National Guard units enforced overnight curfews in the hardest-hit areas to prevent injuries and secure property[24]. Temporary shelters were opened for displaced families at facilities like churches and state parks. Neighboring communities and relief organizations sent resources, and donation drives were organized to aid survivors.
On May 20, just four days after the disaster, another round of severe storms threatened the same region. With portions of Laurel County still in ruins, officials ordered precautionary evacuations for some heavily damaged neighborhoods ahead of the new storms[25]. This added urgency for installing tarps and clearing debris as quickly as possible. Fortunately, the subsequent weather did not produce any tornadoes in the affected area, and recovery work continued uninterrupted.
Governor Beshear requested federal disaster assistance, and on May 24, the U.S. President approved a major disaster declaration for Kentucky to support recovery from the mid-May tornado outbreak. This declaration made federal funding and relief programs available in several counties, including Laurel, Pulaski, and Russell[26]. FEMA opened Disaster Recovery Centers in London and Somerset to help survivors apply for aid and temporary housing[27][28]. The American Red Cross and other charities provided food, supplies, and counseling to impacted families.
In the weeks following the tornado, community response efforts were widespread. First responders, utility crews, and volunteers (including local high school students and church groups) worked together to clear debris and begin rebuilding[29][30]. By early June, power and water service had been restored to most of the area, and reconstruction of homes was underway. The resilience of the affected communities was highlighted by officials, and a memorial event was held to honor the 19 lives lost. The 2025 Somerset–London tornado will be remembered as one of the most destructive and deadly natural disasters in Kentucky’s recent history.
