# Edwardsville Amazon warehouse collapse
The Edwardsville Amazon warehouse collapse was a deadly structural failure that occurred on December 10, 2021, when a tornado struck an Amazon delivery warehouse in Edwardsville, Illinois, United States, resulting in the building’s collapse with six worker fatalities and multiple injuries.[1] The tornado was rated EF3 and tore off the warehouse’s roof, causing its concrete walls to fall inward.[2] The disaster, which took place during a major tornado outbreak, raised concerns about the safety of large warehouses during severe weather. It prompted investigations by government agencies and led to lawsuits and legislative reforms aimed at improving warehouse emergency procedures.[3][7] The Edwardsville facility was subsequently rebuilt (with some structural enhancements but without a dedicated storm shelter) and reopened in 2024.[6]
## Background
The Amazon warehouse, known by the company as the DLI4 delivery station, was located in the Gateway Commerce Center industrial park near Edwardsville. The facility spanned approximately 1.1 million square feet and employed over 100 workers prior to the disaster.[6] It had been constructed in 2018 and was leased by Amazon in 2020.[5] On December 10, 2021, a severe weather outbreak across the Midwest produced numerous tornadoes, including the storm that struck Edwardsville. The National Weather Service determined that the Edwardsville tornado was an EF3 with peak winds of around 150 mph (240 km/h).[2] The tornado first touched down at 8:27 p.m. CST and intensified as it hit the Amazon warehouse shortly after 8:30 p.m. that night.[2]
## Collapse
As the tornado struck the warehouse, it ripped off large sections of the roof and caused the thick concrete walls to collapse inward.[1][2] Employees and delivery drivers inside were caught by surprise and sought shelter wherever possible, including in restrooms.[1] The most severe destruction occurred on the south end of the building, which suffered a catastrophic failure. Six individuals were killed when that portion of the structure gave way.[2] The victims included five contract delivery drivers and one maintenance worker who were at the site.[3] At least one other person was seriously injured, and a number of others sustained minor injuries. Approximately 45 people who had been in the facility survived without major harm, having either evacuated or reached safer areas in time.[1]
Local emergency responders converged on the scene to search for survivors amid the rubble. Rescue crews worked through the night; by the following day, authorities announced that everyone had been accounted for and they transitioned to recovery operations.[1] Illinois Governor J. B. Pritzker visited the site and vowed that the incident would be investigated, stating that “everyone assumes they will be safe at work... We don’t think that they will never come home.”[1] Amazon’s CEO Andy Jassy said the company was “heartbroken over the loss” of its employees and pledged to support the Edwardsville community, a sentiment echoed by Amazon founder Jeff Bezos.[1]
## Investigations and findings
Multiple investigations were launched in the aftermath of the collapse to determine whether building standards or emergency procedures contributed to the fatalities. The U.S. Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) inspected the site and reviewed Amazon’s safety protocols. In April 2022, OSHA reported that the company’s severe weather emergency plan met minimal federal safety guidelines and that there were no violations of workplace safety regulations, since no specific rule required tornado shelters at the time.[3] However, OSHA identified several shortcomings in the implementation of the emergency plan and issued a Hazard Alert Letter with recommendations for improvement.[3] Investigators found that some workers did not know the location of the designated shelter areas in the warehouse and instead had sheltered in a bathroom on the south side that was not a reinforced safe area.[3] Notably, one of the employees who died, 46-year-old Larry Virden, had texted his partner shortly before the collapse that “Amazon won’t let us leave,” suggesting that he and others were instructed to stay on site during the tornado warning.[4] OSHA’s investigation also revealed that an on-site megaphone, meant to be used to activate emergency alerts, was locked in a cage and not accessible to managers when the tornado struck.[4]
According to a report by a structural engineer who examined the wreckage, a number of steel support columns appeared to have no bolts or welds anchoring them to the foundation, but only a bead of caulk around the base – a condition likened to “a peg coming out of a hole.” This suggested a violation of building codes if true, and could have made the building more vulnerable to collapse. Amazon responded that these findings were preliminary and that the warehouse had been built in 2018 “in compliance with all applicable building codes” and had passed city inspections when Amazon leased it in 2020.[5] The company maintained it was premature to suggest any structural defects, noting that a full forensic investigation was still ongoing at that time.[5]
## Lawsuits and criticism
The incident also provoked criticism of Amazon’s safety practices from employees, public officials, and industry observers. In the weeks after the disaster, more than 20 members of Congress – including Senator Elizabeth Warren and Representatives Cori Bush and Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez – sent letters to Amazon’s leadership demanding information about the company’s emergency protocols and why workers were not evacuated earlier.[4] When Amazon’s initial response was deemed unsatisfactory, the lawmakers accused the company of having a “wholly inadequate safety culture,” pointing to discrepancies between Amazon’s statements and OSHA’s findings.[4]
Families of the deceased workers also sought accountability through legal action. Beginning in early 2022, multiple wrongful death lawsuits were filed against Amazon and the firms involved in constructing the warehouse.[5] The first suit, brought by the family of 26-year-old delivery driver Austin McEwen, alleged that Amazon had failed to provide proper shelter or evacuation for employees despite advance tornado warnings, and that “when you knew that this facility was not equipped with those resources and those safeguards, there was nothing stopping Amazon from evacuating.”[5] The lawsuit further claimed that Amazon prioritized fulfilling orders over worker safety, given the absence of any FEMA-rated safe room in the facility.[5] Additional suits from other victims’ families followed, and some expanded to include the building’s construction contractor as a defendant for alleged design flaws. As of late 2024, at least three lawsuits were ongoing, with one case scheduled for mediation and a possible trial in 2025.[6]
Amazon has defended its actions and emergency procedures in the face of these criticisms. The company stated that site managers did follow the emergency action plan, directing employees to shelter-in-place once the tornado warning was issued, and it credited those efforts with preventing further loss of life.[1] Amazon also noted that at the time there was no law requiring purpose-built storm shelters in warehouses, and that the majority of similar warehouse facilities lack such shelters. Company officials indicated that decisions on installing a tornado safe room in the Edwardsville building were up to the property owner (since Amazon was the tenant), and as of 2024 Amazon had not added storm shelters at its other U.S. sites, focusing instead on improving training and monitoring for severe weather.[6]
## Policy reforms
The deadly collapse also triggered a broader push to strengthen building standards and emergency planning for tornadoes. In Illinois, state lawmakers created a Warehouse Safety Task Force in 2022 to examine these issues (involving experts in engineering, emergency management, and labor, as well as local officials).[6] The task force’s final report, released in early 2025, recommended several measures – including mandating tornado shelters or safe rooms in large warehouses, requiring site-specific emergency plans and drills for employees, and improving building code enforcement and inspector training.[8]
Many of the task force’s recommendations were soon codified into law. In August 2025, Governor J. B. Pritzker signed the Warehouse Safety Tornado Preparedness Act, a legislative package developed in response to the Edwardsville tragedy.[7] The law requires all warehouse operators in Illinois to prepare a comprehensive tornado emergency plan and to train workers on emergency procedures.[7] It also mandates that any new warehouses built in the state include a designated storm refuge area or shelter designed to withstand tornado-strength winds.[7] Additionally, the law stipulates that municipal building inspectors must obtain a state certification (through the International Code Council) by 2027 to ensure consistent enforcement of safety codes.[7] The bill passed the Illinois legislature with bipartisan support. Lawmakers from the Edwardsville area described the reforms as a necessary step to protect warehouse employees, noting that workers “should have a safe and secure place to work, where foreseeable hazards are addressed… as the frequency and severity of tornadoes increases.”[7]
At the federal level, the Edwardsville collapse added momentum to discussions about updating OSHA regulations for emergency planning. While no new federal rules had been adopted as of 2025, workplace safety advocates have called for nationwide standards – such as requiring storm shelters in high-occupancy warehouses – to prevent similar tragedies in the future.
## Reconstruction and aftermath
After the disaster, Amazon moved quickly to clear the site and eventually rebuild the Edwardsville delivery center. The destroyed warehouse had essentially been split in half by the tornado, and the entire structure was demolished in the weeks following the incident. Reconstruction on the same site began in 2022. By September 2024 – roughly 1,000 days after the tornado – the warehouse was rebuilt and officially reopened for operations.[6] The new structure incorporated some improvements in line with updated local building codes: for example, the exterior precast concrete walls were built to a higher wind-resistance standard (able to withstand 114 mph gusts, up from 90 mph in the original design).[6] However, the rebuilt Amazon facility did not include a hardened storm shelter or safe room, a decision that drew disappointment from some community members and officials.[6] According to local authorities, none of the nearly 30 large warehouses in the Edwardsville area had dedicated tornado shelters as of 2024, reflecting a common practice in the industry.[6] Amazon noted that it leases the warehouse from a third-party owner and did not unilaterally add a shelter during reconstruction. Company representatives also acknowledged that across Amazon’s network of over 800 fulfillment and delivery sites in North America, the company had not retrofitted any facilities with storm shelters, opting instead to enhance its weather monitoring, on-site alert systems, and employee training after the Edwardsville event.[6] In the aftermath, Amazon hired a staff meteorologist to better monitor severe weather threats and updated its emergency response training programs for employees.[6]
The reopening of the warehouse was met with mixed feelings in the community. Many residents and officials welcomed the return of jobs and saw the new building as a sign of recovery – Edwardsville’s mayor called the rebuilt facility “a concrete reminder” of the community’s resilience.[6] At the same time, the anniversary of the tragedy prompted reflection on the lives lost. Families of the six workers who were killed have continued to press for improvements in workplace safety, even as they mourn their loved ones. The Edwardsville Amazon warehouse collapse stands as a somber reminder of the destructive power of tornadoes and has become a case study in how major companies and regulators address employee safety during natural disasters.
