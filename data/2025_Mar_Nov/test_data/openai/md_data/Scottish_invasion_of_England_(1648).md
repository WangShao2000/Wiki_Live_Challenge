# Scottish invasion of England (1648)
## Lead
The Scottish invasion of England in 1648 was a military campaign during the Second English Civil War, in which a Scottish army intervened in England on behalf of the imprisoned King Charles I. The Scottish force, known as the Engagers, was led by James, Duke of Hamilton and composed of Covenanter factions who had made a secret agreement with Charles I to restore him to power in exchange for establishing Presbyterianism in England for three years[1][2]. In July 1648, Hamilton’s army crossed the border into northern England, aiming to join Royalist uprisings against Parliament. Ill-equipped and poorly trained, the Scots advanced slowly and were confronted by Oliver Cromwell and the Parliamentarian New Model Army. The campaign culminated in the Battle of Preston (17–19 August 1648), where Cromwell’s forces defeated the Scots in a decisive victory. Hamilton’s army was routed, with thousands of Scots killed or captured, and Hamilton himself surrendered shortly afterward. The failure of the Scottish invasion effectively ended the Second Civil War in Parliament’s favor and paved the way for Charles I’s trial and execution in January 1649[3][4].
## Background
During the First English Civil War (1642–1646), Scotland’s Covenanter government had allied with the English Parliament under the Solemn League and Covenant, sending a Scottish army to fight King Charles I’s forces. In May 1646, after his defeat, Charles I surrendered to the Scottish army[5]. However, disagreements soon arose between the Scots and the English Parliament over religion and the political settlement. The Covenanters wanted Presbyterian church reforms across Britain, while Charles I resisted such terms[6][7]. The Scots eventually handed Charles over to the English Parliament in January 1647 in exchange for a financial settlement of £400,000[8][9].
By late 1647, Charles I sought to exploit divisions between English Parliamentarians and their former Scottish allies. In December 1647, he negotiated a secret treaty with a faction of Scottish nobles led by the Duke of Hamilton. This agreement became known as the Engagement, under which Charles promised to impose Presbyterianism in England for three years and suppress religious Independents, in return for Scottish military assistance to restore him to the throne[1][2]. The Engagers (as Hamilton’s pro-royalist faction was called) gained a majority in the Scottish Parliament and began raising an army in early 1648 to support the King[10][11]. This plan was controversial in Scotland: Archibald Campbell, 1st Marquess of Argyll, and other conservative Covenanters (the “Kirk Party”) denounced the Engagement as a betrayal of the Covenant. Despite protests from the Kirk and some local rebellions against conscription, Hamilton proceeded with mustering forces for an English invasion[12][13].
At the same time, in the spring of 1648, Royalist uprisings against Parliament broke out in England and Wales, marking the start of the Second English Civil War. Insurrections erupted in South Wales, Kent, Essex, and other areas, as discontented Parliamentarian troops and Royalist supporters rallied to restore the King[14][15]. The plan was for the Scottish Engager army to coordinate with these revolts. In preparation for the Scots’ intervention, English Royalist forces under Sir Marmaduke Langdale and Sir Philip Musgrave seized the border strongholds of Berwick-upon-Tweed (on 28 April 1648) and Carlisle (on 29 April 1648)[16]. Securing these northern towns opened the route for Hamilton’s army to enter England and link up with Royalist sympathizers. However, the overall Royalist strategy was poorly coordinated, and many of the planned risings were suppressed by Parliament before the Scots arrived[17].
## Invasion and Initial Moves
Hamilton’s Engager army was slower to mobilize than anticipated. Due to significant opposition and desertions at home, the Scots gathered far fewer troops than promised. By early July 1648, Hamilton had only assembled around 9,000 men (approximately 6,000 infantry and 3,000 cavalry), barely one-third of the intended strength[13]. These recruits were mostly inexperienced conscripts; contemporaries noted the army was badly equipped, ill-disciplined, and prone to plundering as it marched[18][19]. Many Scottish civilians were relieved to see the unruly soldiers depart when the force finally crossed into England on 8 July 1648[18].
Upon entering England, Hamilton’s army occupied parts of Cumberland and Westmorland in the far north. Rather than advancing swiftly south, the Scots waited for reinforcements throughout July. Additional levies and a contingent of veteran Scottish troops from Ireland (around 3,000 men under Major-General George Munro) eventually brought the Engager army’s size up to a reported 15,000–18,000 by early August[20][19]. However, Munro quarreled with Hamilton’s deputy, the Earl of Callendar, and as a result Munro’s experienced regiments were left behind in Scotland or in reserve near the border rather than joining the main advance[21]. This internal discord deprived Hamilton of some of his best troops.
Meanwhile, Parliamentarian forces in the north, though initially outnumbered, acted to contain the Scottish incursion. Major-General John Lambert commanded Parliament’s Northern Association Army of roughly 3,000–4,000 men in Yorkshire and the northeast[22]. Throughout June and July 1648, Lambert shadowed the Engagers’ movements and engaged in minor skirmishes to slow their progress[23]. He fell back ahead of the larger Scottish force, fortifying positions to block a potential Scottish advance into Yorkshire. By withdrawing across the Pennines and concentrating at Barnard Castle, Lambert also prevented Hamilton from linking up with Royalist holdouts at Pontefract Castle (in Yorkshire), which had declared for the King in early June[24].
As the stalemate continued, Hamilton debated his route into the heart of England. The most direct path was through Yorkshire toward London, but Lambert’s defenses made that risky. Instead, Hamilton resolved to take a more westerly route through Lancashire, where Royalist support might be stronger. He intended to march south-west towards north Wales, to join forces with Lord Byron and Welsh Royalists who were attempting to rise up in coordination[25]. This decision meant bypassing Lambert’s position by moving down the less protected western side of the Pennines.
In late July and early August, the Scottish army trudged slowly through heavy summer rains and difficult roads in northern England[26][27]. Morale and discipline in the Engager ranks deteriorated further during the march. The unusually cold, wet weather discouraged English recruits from turning out, while numerous Scottish soldiers deserted and returned home due to hardship and poor leadership[28][29]. By the first week of August, Hamilton’s forces reached southern Westmorland and Lancashire. On 9 August 1648, the Scots arrived at Hornby in Lancashire[30]. There, Hamilton held a council of war and finalized the plan to proceed via the Preston route (west of the Pennine hills) rather than attempting to enter Yorkshire[30]. The hope was that this western approach would allow the Engagers to link up with Royalist insurgents in Wales or the Midlands.
Unbeknownst to Hamilton, Parliament was mustering a powerful response. After suppressing the South Wales revolt in July, Lieutenant-General Oliver Cromwell marched north with veteran New Model Army units. Fresh from capturing Pembroke Castle in Wales (which fell on 11 July), Cromwell brought a force of around 8,000 seasoned troops (including cavalry and artillery crews, though he left his heavy guns behind to move faster)[31][32]. He arrived in the north of England just in time to confront the Scottish invasion. On 12 August, Cromwell rendezvoused with Lambert’s contingent at Wetherby in Yorkshire, uniting their forces. The combined Parliamentarian army in the north now numbered roughly 8,600–9,000 men, about half the size of Hamilton’s army on paper[33][32]. Despite the numerical disparity, the Parliamentarians were battle-hardened, well-disciplined, and led by experienced commanders, whereas the Engager army was strung out over a long marching column and unaware that Cromwell was so close[34][35].
## Battle of Preston (17–19 August 1648)
By mid-August, the two armies were converging in Lancashire. Hamilton’s troops were spread out along the road south from Lancaster toward Preston, with a portion of the Scottish cavalry far ahead on a foraging expedition. On 16 August, as the Engagers approached Preston, Hamilton still did not realize that Cromwell’s forces were rapidly advancing from the east[34][36]. He posted a Royalist detachment under Sir Marmaduke Langdale (about 1,500–2,000 English Royalist infantry and horse) to guard his northern flank and the road from Yorkshire, while the main Scottish army began crossing the River Ribble at Preston to continue south[37][38]. Many of Hamilton’s units (including most of his cavalry and all his artillery train) had already moved south of the Ribble or were stretched out along the route to Wigan, leaving only Langdale’s outposts covering the rear.
On the morning of 17 August 1648, Oliver Cromwell’s army struck. Moving swiftly along the north bank of the Ribble, Cromwell aimed to catch the Scots from the rear, cutting off their line of retreat to the north[27][39]. Langdale’s troops first clashed with Cromwell’s vanguard near Clitheroe, and Langdale hurriedly fell back toward Preston to make a stand[37]. Outside Preston, Langdale deployed his infantry and dismounted dragoons on Ribbleton Moor, using hedges and ditches for cover, while a small force of Scottish cavalry (lancers) held the road itself[40]. Despite driving rain and muddy ground that hampered cavalry, Cromwell pressed the attack with his forward regiments. Initial assaults by Parliamentarian cavalry down the narrow lane were repulsed by Langdale’s lancers[41]. Cromwell then reorganized and launched a coordinated assault later in the afternoon, sending infantry to flank the Royalist musketeers in the enclosures while his horse cleared the main road[42]. After several hours of fierce fighting, Langdale’s outnumbered contingent was overwhelmed and pushed back into the town of Preston[43].
As the battle raged on 17 August, the Duke of Hamilton belatedly realized his rear guard was in serious trouble. He attempted to send some Scottish infantry back across the Ribble to reinforce Langdale. However, Hamilton’s second-in-command, the Earl of Callendar, vehemently objected to reversing the march, arguing it was futile to send foot soldiers without cavalry support[44][45]. Hamilton yielded to Callendar’s counsel and continued withdrawing the majority of his troops south of the river, leaving Langdale to fend for himself. By the time Hamilton’s token reinforcements arrived, it was too late – Cromwell’s forces had taken Preston and captured most of Langdale’s infantry in close-quarters fighting within the town[46]. Langdale and a handful of survivors escaped Preston and rejoined Hamilton north of the Ribble in the evening, with Parliamentarian troops in hot pursuit up to the riverbank[47].
Cromwell now faced the task of forcing a crossing of the Ribble, as the Scots held the south bank. Major-General William Baillie, commanding the Scottish infantry, formed a defensive line on high ground between the Ribble and the smaller River Darwen, while about 600 musketeers were detailed to defend the stone bridge over the Ribble[48][49]. The Scots managed to hold the Ribble Bridge for nearly two hours against repeated assaults[49][50]. Eventually, Cromwell’s infantry carried the bridge “at push of pike,” driving off the Scottish musketeers[49]. The Parliamentarians then quickly seized the bridge over the Darwen, forcing Baillie’s main position to be abandoned as night fell on 17 August[49][50]. The Scottish baggage train and artillery were captured in Preston, depriving Hamilton’s army of supplies.
Under cover of darkness and torrential rain on the night of 17/18 August, Hamilton ordered a desperate retreat from Preston. The Scottish army, minus the troops already lost, pulled back along the road toward Wigan in an attempt to reunite with their detached cavalry. Due to miscommunication, Hamilton’s infantry and John Middleton’s cavalry column (returning from Wigan) ended up on parallel roads and failed to rendezvous as planned[51][52]. Early on 18 August, Middleton’s horse accidentally blundered into Cromwell’s advance guard in the darkness and turned back south, while Cromwell continued to harry the rear of the Scottish column with cavalry strikes[52]. The retreat became a running battle down the road through Chorley toward Wigan. The exhausted Scots fought several delaying skirmishes as rearguard actions; Colonel Thornhaugh, a Parliamentarian cavalry officer, was killed in one sharp clash, but the relentless pressure kept the Scots disorganized and on the move[52].
By 19 August 1648, Hamilton’s beaten forces made a final stand near Winwick Pass, just north of Warrington in Cheshire. About 4,000 Scottish infantry under Baillie, with a remnant of cavalry, entrenched themselves behind barricades at Winwick to block Cromwell’s pursuit[53]. With many of his men starving, low on ammunition, and near mutiny, Baillie was unable to hold for long. Cromwell’s troops assaulted the Scottish position and, after heavy fighting, succeeded in breaking through by the afternoon of 19 August[54]. Recognizing the futility of further resistance, Baillie sought terms. He met Cromwell at Warrington Bridge and agreed to surrender unconditionally[54][55]. The remaining Scottish infantry – roughly 3,000 survivors – laid down their arms at Warrington, yielding their flags and weapons to the Parliamentarians[56][55].
Duke Hamilton and the remnants of his cavalry (several thousand horsemen) had ridden ahead of the main force before the surrender. Accompanied by the Earl of Callendar and other officers, Hamilton fled southward, attempting to escape capture[57]. Parliamentarian detachments under Major-General Lambert gave chase across Cheshire and Staffordshire[57]. One by one, the fleeing Royalist leaders were caught: John Middleton was taken after being thrown from his horse, and Sir Marmaduke Langdale was captured hiding in an inn at Nottingham a few days later[58]. Finally, on 25 August 1648, with his remaining followers dwindling and surrounded, the Duke of Hamilton surrendered to Lambert at Uttoxeter in Staffordshire[59][60]. This marked the definitive end of the Scottish invasion.
## Aftermath
The collapse of Hamilton’s invasion force in August 1648 had far-reaching consequences. The Second Civil War was effectively decided in Parliament’s favor. With the Scottish Engager army destroyed, Royalist resistance in other regions crumbled swiftly. In the south of England, the last holdouts in the Siege of Colchester surrendered to Parliamentary commander Sir Thomas Fairfax on 28 August 1648, just days after the news of Preston[3][61]. In North Wales, Royalist leader Lord Byron abandoned his uprising and fled abroad, realizing no relief would come[62]. In the far north, Sir Philip Musgrave and the remaining northern Royalists held Carlisle until October 1648 before capitulating, and Pontefract Castle (Yorkshire) – the final fortress still flying the Royalist flag – was starved into submission by March 1649[63].
Parliamentarian victory in the civil war now appeared certain, and the political fallout was dramatic. Many in the New Model Army and Parliament held King Charles I directly responsible for the bloodshed of the second war, accusing him of treason for conspiring with the Scots against his own kingdom[64][4]. The defeat of the Scottish invasion emboldened the Army’s hardline faction (the Independents) to demand accountability from the King. In December 1648, the Army purged Parliament of moderates (an event known as Pride’s Purge), and the remaining “Rump Parliament” put Charles I on trial. The King was found guilty of treason and executed on 30 January 1649, an unprecedented regicide fueled in part by his alliance with the Scots and the recent devastation of war[3][65].
The Duke of Hamilton, as commander of the invading Scottish army, met a similar fate. After his surrender, Hamilton was imprisoned and later brought to trial by the English government. He was beheaded for treason in March 1649, just a few weeks after the King’s execution[65][66]. Other Scottish and English officers captured during the campaign were also punished: Sir Marmaduke Langdale and Sir Philip Musgrave, among others, were imprisoned (though many would be released in a subsequent political settlement). Major-General Baillie and the common Scottish soldiers taken at Preston and Warrington were treated as prisoners of war. Oliver Cromwell reported that around 9,000 Scots were captured in the Preston campaign[67]. Several thousand of these prisoners – considered dangerous or too numerous to keep – were transported to forced labor overseas. Contemporary records indicate that droves of Scots captives were shipped to the colonies in the West Indies (Barbados) and New England/Virginia to serve as indentured servants on plantations[68].
In Scotland, the failure of the Engagement provoked a power shift. The Kirk Party (radical Covenanters opposed to the King) seized the opportunity to topple the Engagers’ government in Edinburgh. In what became known as the Whiggamore Raid (September 1648), Covenanter forces led by Argyll and David Leslie marched on Edinburgh and ousted the Engager-dominated Committee of Estates[69]. They aligned with Cromwell, who brought an army into Scotland in late September to secure his northern flank[69]. All Scottish officials who had supported the Engagement were removed from power. This realignment meant that by the end of 1648, Scotland’s leadership officially disavowed Hamilton’s failed enterprise and once again took a hard Presbyterian stance.
Despite the Engagers’ defeat, the Scots did not accept the outcome of Charles I’s execution. The Scottish Parliament (now controlled by Argyll and the Covenanters) proclaimed Charles’s exiled son, Charles II, as king in February 1649 – on the condition of upholding the Covenant. This set the stage for renewed conflict between the Commonwealth of England and Scotland. In 1650, Charles II reached an agreement with the Covenanters, leading to a Scottish royalist attempt to invade England once more, which culminated in Cromwell’s invasion of Scotland and the final battles of the Wars of the Three Kingdoms (Dunbar 1650 and Worcester 1651). However, the Scottish invasion of England in 1648 remains notable as the critical turning point that ended the second civil war. The crushing of Hamilton’s Engager army at Preston demonstrated the strength of the New Model Army and eliminated the last major field force upholding Charles I’s cause, thereby clearing the way for the revolutionary establishment of the Commonwealth of England.
