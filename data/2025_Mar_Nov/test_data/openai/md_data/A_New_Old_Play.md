# A New Old Play (椒麻堂会)
## Lead
A New Old Play (Chinese: 椒麻堂会; Jiāomá tánghuì) is a 2021 Chinese epic historical drama film directed, written, and edited by Qiu Jiongjiong[1]. It is Qiu’s first narrative feature film[2]. The film follows a Sichuan opera troupe clown performer who, after dying in the 1980s, relives fifty years of vivid memories spanning the turbulent mid-20th-century history of China[3][4]. Set in a stylized “afterlife” framework, the story incorporates real historical events—such as the Second Sino-Japanese War and the Cultural Revolution—recounted through the protagonist’s theatrical recollections[5]. A New Old Play is distinguished by its inventive staging: Qiu presents the narrative as a series of theatrical tableaux with deliberately artificial sets and surreal fantasy sequences, blurring the line between stage and cinema[6]. The film premiered at the 74th Locarno Film Festival in 2021, where it won the Special Jury Prize[7]. It went on to receive multiple festival awards, including the Firebird Award (Best Film) at the Hong Kong International Film Festival and the Young Jury Award at the Festival des 3 Continents[7]. The film has been met with widespread critical acclaim for its ambitious blend of personal narrative and political history.
## Plot
In the 1980s, Qiu Fu, a renowned clown-role actor in a Sichuan opera troupe, dies and is escorted to the underworld’s Ghost City by two supernatural attendants[3]. Before his soul can pass on, he is granted one last look back at the memories of his life. In flashbacks, a young orphan Qiu Fu is adopted in the 1920s by a Sichuan opera New-New Theatre troupe during a time of civil war[8]. The troupe’s founder, a former soldier nicknamed Pocky, raises Qiu Fu as an apprentice clown performer[9]. As years pass, Qiu Fu and his fellow performers experience the upheavals of modern Chinese history. During the Second Sino-Japanese War in the 1940s, the troupe joins the resistance against Japanese occupation[10]. After the war, the adult Qiu Fu becomes a star in the opera, but the new Communist era brings political pressure on the troupe, forcing them to disband and wander through a changing world[11]. In the 1960s, the Cultural Revolution proves catastrophic for traditional artists: Qiu Fu is persecuted and at one point confined to a pigsty as revolutionary zealots denounce Sichuan opera as “feudal” art[12]. Despite these hardships—war, famine, and political turmoil—Qiu Fu’s passion for opera endures.
Back in the underworld realm, Qiu Fu encounters the ghosts of old friends and colleagues as he reminisces[13]. Finally, before he can enter the afterlife proper, he must drink the Mengpo Soup of forgetfulness. Surrounded by familiar faces at the outskirts of Ghost City, Qiu Fu reluctantly sips the bowl of Forgetfulness Soup, which causes all his lifelong memories of art, struggle, and sorrow to fade away[12]. Having completed this ritual, Qiu Fu’s spirit departs, and the curtain falls on his “new old” play—a life’s journey defined by both the grand sweep of history and the intimate dramas of the stage.

## Production
Qiu Jiongjiong conceived A New Old Play as a tribute to his family and the tradition of Sichuan opera. The film’s narrative is loosely based on the life of Qiu’s grandfather, Qiu Yu, who was a well-known chou (clown) performer in Sichuan opera[16]. Drawing from this personal history, Qiu crafted a script that intertwines the private lives of theatre artists with major events in China’s 20th-century political turmoil. Notably, Qiu cast his father Qiu Zhimin (a veteran stage actor) in the role of Pocky, essentially having him portray a character modeled on Qiu Zhimin’s own father (the director’s grandfather)[9]. This multi-generational involvement lent the project a deeply personal resonance.
A stylized group tableau from A New Old Play, highlighting the film’s blend of stage drama and cinema. Director Qiu designed elaborate sets on a soundstage to resemble a traditional Sichuan opera performance.
Filming took place entirely on constructed indoor sets to achieve a deliberate theatrical aesthetic. The production was staged in a large warehouse studio (approximately 400 square meters) that was transformed into a self-contained world of the living and the dead[17]. Qiu Jiongjiong, who is also a painter and visual artist by background, served as his own art director and implemented an imaginative, handcrafted design for the film[18]. The sets were painstakingly detailed, including elements like an exquisite miniature model village and a glowing sun shaped like a Buddha’s head, all created to evoke a dreamlike memory-scape of China[19]. The film’s scenes unfold in long, flowing takes that resemble stage tableaux vivants. Actors perform against obviously artificial backdrops, and transitions between scenes often use old-fashioned theatrical tricks. This approach – blending absurdist comedy, political melodrama, vaudeville, and ritualistic tableaux – reinforces the idea that the story is a “play” reflecting on history, rather than a straightforward realist depiction[6][18]. Supernatural and symbolic sequences (such as ghosts escorting Qiu Fu and other phantasmal imagery) are integrated into the drama, further emphasizing the film’s surreal, operatic tone.
The dialogue in A New Old Play is performed in the Sichuanese dialect of Chinese (with English subtitles for international releases) to maintain authenticity[20]. The cinematography, led by Feng Yuchao (credited as Robbin Feng), employs a static camera at a low “stage” angle for many scenes, reminiscent of watching an opera on stage. Qiu’s distinctive visual style and long takes required careful choreography of the actors and meticulous set design. The director has mentioned in interviews that working outside the Chinese state studio system gave him the freedom to experiment with such unorthodox storytelling methods, although it also meant the film was an independent production with international backing (Hong Kong’s Uluka Studio and France’s Hippocampe Productions, among others)[21].
## Release
A New Old Play had its world premiere at the 74th Locarno International Film Festival (Switzerland) in August 2021, where it was part of the international competition lineup[22]. At Locarno, the film’s nearly three-hour runtime and unconventional style made a strong impression; the festival jury awarded it the Special Jury Prize (the festival’s second-highest honor)[22]. The film also received a separate Junior Jury Award (Second Prize) from Locarno’s youth jury, reflecting its cross-generational appeal[22].
Following its debut, A New Old Play toured the international festival circuit in late 2021. It was screened at the 43rd Festival des 3 Continents in Nantes, France, where it won the Young Jury Award[23]. At the 18th Golden Apricot International Film Festival in Yerevan, Armenia, the film received a Special Jury Mention in the main competition[24]. The film was also selected for prestigious festivals such as the Busan International Film Festival (South Korea) and the Singapore International Film Festival in 2021, continuing to garner attention for its unique blend of art and history[25]. In November 2021, A New Old Play won the Young Cinema Competition – Firebird Award (Best Film) at the 46th Hong Kong International Film Festival, as well as the festival’s FIPRESCI Prize awarded by international critics[26]. Additionally, the film was nominated for Best Cinematography at the 14th Asia Pacific Screen Awards in 2021, recognizing Feng Yuchao’s work behind the camera[27].
In early 2022, the film made its North American debut. It was showcased at the International Film Festival Rotterdam (Netherlands) in February 2022, and in New York City it was featured as the closing film of the Museum of the Moving Image’s First Look 2022 festival (an event highlighting innovative new films)[28]. The U.S. theatrical release of A New Old Play began on May 20, 2022, in New York, distributed by Icarus Films[29]. The opening run at Manhattan’s Anthology Film Archives introduced the film to American audiences, followed by limited showings in select art-house theaters in cities like Los Angeles and San Francisco. Given the film’s length and foreign language nature, the U.S. release was modest, with a one-theater opening that earned a small box-office gross of about $2,500 in its first weekend[30]. The film later became available for streaming and educational viewing through the dGenerate Films collection (via platforms such as OVID.tv) in 2023, and a Blu-ray edition was released by Vinegar Syndrome’s Kani label, helping A New Old Play reach a wider audience of world cinema enthusiasts.
Notably, A New Old Play has not had a general commercial release in mainland China. Due to its sensitive historical subject matter and avant-garde style, the film has primarily circulated in international festivals and special screenings. Qiu Jiongjiong, as an independent filmmaker, worked largely outside the state studio system, and the film did not go through China’s official censorship process. Nevertheless, the acclaim it has received abroad has bolstered Qiu’s reputation within Chinese independent cinema circles.
## Reception
Upon its release on the festival circuit and limited theatrical runs, A New Old Play received critical acclaim. On review aggregator Rotten Tomatoes, the film holds a perfect 100% approval rating based on professional critic reviews (9 out of 9 reviews were positive)[31]. Critics have praised the film’s richly layered narrative, bold artistic vision, and the way it poignantly merges tragedy and farce to recount history. Austin Considine of The New York Times selected A New Old Play as a “Critic’s Pick” and described it as a “magnificently layered historical epic” that “breathes new life into a story as old as civilization.”[32] Considine noted how the film uses the lens of theatre to reflect on China’s past, making the old stories feel fresh and emotionally resonant. Writing in The New Yorker, critic Richard Brody praised Qiu Jiongjiong’s direction as “daring and imaginative, with affecting intimacy and candid observations.”[33] Brody highlighted the film’s archly stylized approach to depicting 20th-century Chinese upheavals, remarking on Qiu’s ability to be at once critical and compassionate in portraying historical events.
Other reviewers echoed these positive sentiments. Sean Gilman of The Chinese Cinema noted that although the film runs 179 minutes, it remains “a lively, lovely film, even in its saddest moments,” commending Qiu’s storytelling for sustaining viewer engagement through its mix of humor and sorrow[34]. Critics often compared A New Old Play to earlier classics that blend personal fate with national history: for instance, its expansive narrative of an opera family through war and revolution drew comparisons to Chen Kaige’s Farewell My Concubine (1993) and Hou Hsiao-hsien’s The Puppetmaster (1993)[35]. Reviewers also pointed out the film’s unique aesthetic; The Austin Chronicle called it “an epic and fantastical depiction of five decades of Chinese history”, and Shelly Kraicer, a prominent scholar of Chinese cinema, lauded A New Old Play as “the brightest light in the Chinese independent cinema world at this moment.”[36] Overall, the film’s reception was strongly favorable, with critics and festival juries appreciating its ambitious scope, visual creativity, and heartfelt homage to the enduring spirit of art and artists in turbulent times.

These screenings and accolades cemented the film’s status as a significant work in contemporary world cinema and marked Qiu Jiongjiong as an important voice in Chinese independent filmmaking.
