# 2025 Enderlin tornado
The 2025 Enderlin tornado was a large and violent tornado that struck rural areas near Enderlin, North Dakota, on June 20, 2025. Rated an EF5 on the Enhanced Fujita scale, it was the first tornado to receive the EF5 classification in the United States in over 12 years[1][2]. The tornado reached an estimated peak wind speed of 210 mph (338 km/h) and grew up to 1.05 miles (1.7 km) wide at its largest point[3][2]. Over a 19-minute lifespan, it carved a path of destruction approximately 12 miles (19 km) long across portions of Ransom and Cass counties in southeastern North Dakota[3][2]. The tornado destroyed multiple farmsteads, derailed a freight train, and caused three fatalities, making it one of the deadliest tornadoes in North Dakota in decades[4][5]. Its occurrence ended a long period with no EF5 tornadoes nationwide and marked only the third F5/EF5 tornado on record in North Dakota’s history[6][7].
## Meteorological background
In mid-June 2025, a widespread severe weather outbreak impacted the northern Great Plains. On June 20, an intense mesoscale convective system developed over the region, producing a long-lived derecho (a fast-moving line of severe thunderstorms) that swept eastward across North Dakota and beyond[8]. This system generated destructive straight-line winds of 70–106 mph and spawned numerous tornadoes along its path[8]. In eastern North Dakota alone, at least nine tornadoes were reported that evening amid the severe thunderstorms[8]. The atmosphere was characterized by very warm, moist air and strong wind shear, providing an environment conducive to tornadic supercells forming ahead of the main derecho squall line[9].
One particularly strong supercell developed over Ransom County in southeastern North Dakota on the night of June 20. Around 11:00 p.m. local time, this storm produced the tornado near the small city of Enderlin, about 60 mi southwest of Fargo. The tornado touched down south of Enderlin at approximately 11:02 p.m. CDT[10] and tracked toward the north-northeast. It moved through mainly rural farmland and open country, eventually crossing the Maple River and the Ransom–Cass county line. The tornado remained on the ground for roughly 19 minutes before dissipating around 11:21 p.m. CDT in southwestern Cass County[11]. Its formation occurred after dark, making it a rain-wrapped, nighttime tornado that was difficult to see, which added to the danger for anyone in its path.
## Tornado event and damage
Damage to a farm property east of Enderlin in the aftermath of the June 20, 2025 tornado. The violent tornado destroyed several farms, obliterated buildings, and tossed heavy machinery and vehicles, indicating extreme wind speeds.
As it intensified, the Enderlin tornado grew to a massive size—over one mile wide at peak—and inflicted devastating damage in a mainly rural area east of Enderlin[2][12]. Entire farmsteads were leveled: at least two farm homes were completely destroyed and several outbuildings and grain bins were obliterated[4][12]. In one case, a farmhouse was swept entirely off its foundation, leaving only the basement behind, a level of destruction consistent with the highest tornado intensity[13]. Sturdy trees in the tornado’s path were debarked and reduced to bare trunks, and crop fields were scoured by the intense winds[14][15]. A free-standing communications tower collapsed, and high-voltage transmission towers were also blown down by the tornado[13].
One of the most striking incidents occurred south of Enderlin, where the tornado struck a moving freight train. The powerful winds derailed 33 rail cars, including 19 fully loaded grain hopper cars and 14 tanker cars[16]. Several of these heavy rail cars were violently thrown from the tracks—one empty tanker car was lofted and flung about 500–600 feet from the rail line into a field[16]. This extraordinary feat of tossing large, weighty objects indicated the presence of winds well over 200 mph at that location. Engineers later estimated that roughly 230 mph winds would be required to hurl a 72-ton grain hopper car the way the Enderlin tornado did[17][18]. The mangled train cars, along with the wiped-out buildings and shredded trees, served as key evidence of the tornado’s extreme intensity.
Despite its ferocity, the tornado narrowly missed the town of Enderlin itself. The city of Enderlin experienced mainly peripheral effects, such as downed power lines and power outages, but the populated town center was largely spared from major damage[19]. However, rural residents in the direct path suffered catastrophic impacts. The tornado claimed three lives in two separate farm locations east of Enderlin[19][20]. At one farm, an elderly husband and wife were killed when their home was obliterated, and at another nearby farm a third person was killed as the tornado destroyed that residence as well[20]. All three victims were aged in their 70s or 80s. No other serious injuries were reported, in part because of the sparse population in the tornado’s immediate path and the fact that few vehicles were on rural roads at that late hour. The three fatalities made this the deadliest North Dakota tornado in nearly five decades – no tornado in the state had killed three or more people since an F4 tornado in 1978 that caused five deaths in Elgin, North Dakota[5].
## Aftermath and emergency response
In the hours and days following the disaster, local and state authorities mobilized to respond. First responders from Enderlin and surrounding communities, along with sheriff’s deputies, conducted search-and-rescue operations and welfare checks through the night of June 20–21[21][22]. They faced hazardous conditions immediately after the tornado, as lightning, heavy rain, and high winds from the broader storm system continued in the area, complicating the response efforts[23]. By daybreak on June 21, the scope of the damage was evident with debris from homes and farms strewn across fields and roads. Emergency crews and utility companies worked to clear roadways and restore electricity to Enderlin and nearby rural areas that had lost power.
North Dakota Governor Kelly Armstrong declared a statewide disaster emergency on June 21, 2025, in the wake of the tornado and the accompanying severe storms[24]. This declaration activated the state’s emergency operations plan and made state resources available to support the affected communities. “Our hearts and sympathies go out to the family and friends of the three North Dakotans who tragically lost their lives in Friday’s tornado, and to the close-knit community in Enderlin, which suffered widespread damage,” Governor Armstrong stated in announcing the disaster declaration[25]. He also commended the swift actions of first responders and local officials in the aftermath of the storm[25].
In the weeks that followed, community and volunteer efforts played a significant role in the recovery. Hundreds of volunteers from Enderlin and neighboring counties turned out to help farmers and residents clean up debris scattered across fields and properties[26]. Relief organizations and local groups organized cleanup crews, and even drones were used to help locate and collect pieces of wreckage and personal belongings thrown by the tornado[26]. Local banks and charities established relief funds to assist families who lost homes or farm buildings, and mental health services were offered to survivors coping with trauma[27]. One such effort, the Enderlin Area Tornado Relief Fund set up at a regional bank, provided financial assistance to those hardest hit[27]. Community leaders noted that some residents faced unexpected rebuilding costs due to insurance gaps, underscoring the need for continued financial support during recovery[28].
At the state level, damage assessments were conducted throughout the summer to quantify the destruction from the tornado and the wider storm outbreak. On July 21, Governor Armstrong formally requested a federal disaster declaration to unlock additional recovery funds for North Dakota[29]. The President of the United States approved the disaster declaration in early September 2025, making federal aid and FEMA assistance available to supplement state and local recovery efforts[30]. This assistance helped fund the rebuilding of infrastructure and provided grants to individuals and businesses who suffered uninsured losses. Governor Armstrong welcomed the federal support, calling the storm “one of the most powerful summer storms in recent history” for the state and emphasizing the importance of disaster aid in helping communities rebuild[31]. By late 2025, reconstruction of damaged farms was under way, and the Enderlin area continued its long-term recovery from the historic tornado.
## Analysis, rating, and historical significance
Because of the extraordinary damage caused by the Enderlin tornado, meteorologists took an unusually long time to analyze and rate its intensity. Initially, the National Weather Service (NWS) office in Grand Forks rated the tornado as an EF3 based on the damage observed in the immediate aftermath[32]. This preliminary rating (indicative of winds around 140 mph) was assigned partly because many of the structures hit were not engineered buildings, and standard damage indicators were limited in such rural areas[33][34]. However, the scope of destruction clearly suggested a very powerful tornado, so the NWS launched an in-depth investigation into the event. In the weeks after the tornado, experts from the NWS collaborated with wind engineers and researchers – including members of the Northern Tornadoes Project in Canada and renowned storm damage experts like Tim Marshall and Dr. Greg Kopp – to perform a forensic analysis of the damage[35][36]. They examined high-resolution aerial imagery, surveyed structural debris, and performed calculations on the derailed train cars and other unique damage indicators. The team also reviewed radar data, which showed extremely intense velocity signatures co-located with the time and location of the most severe damage[37][38].
This comprehensive re-analysis concluded that the tornado’s winds had exceeded 200 mph. On October 6, 2025, the National Weather Service announced that the Enderlin tornado’s rating was being upgraded to EF5, the highest category on the Enhanced Fujita scale[39][40]. The revised damage survey determined that wind speeds “greater than 210 mph” occurred at the site of the train derailment and other locations, warranting the EF5 classification[41][42]. In their report, meteorologists noted classic EF5 indicators such as a clean-swept foundation of a house and trees stripped completely of bark and branches[43][44]. The tossed railroad cars – an unconventional damage indicator – provided additional, crucial evidence. According to the analysis, the distances and weights involved (rail cars up to ~130 metric tons thrown hundreds of feet) implied wind forces in the EF5 range[17][18]. The upgrade made the Enderlin tornado the first EF5 tornado in the United States since May 2013, ending what had been the longest period on record without any tornado rated EF5 in the country[1][45]. The 12-year “EF5 drought” was notable, as several strong tornadoes in the intervening years had come close but did not meet the criteria due to lack of suitable damage evidence[46][47].
The 2025 Enderlin tornado holds significant places in weather record books. It became one of only three tornadoes ever documented at F5 or EF5 intensity in North Dakota[6]. The other two were historic events in the 1950s: an F5 tornado in Fort Rice, ND in 1953, and the devastating Fargo tornado of June 20, 1957, which was also rated F5 and killed 12 people in the Fargo area[7]. Thus, the Enderlin tornado was North Dakota’s first tornado rated in the top category in 68 years. With three fatalities, it was also the state’s deadliest tornado in nearly 47 years, the worst since the July 1978 F4 tornado in Elgin, ND that caused five deaths[5]. Nationally, the confirmation of an EF5 broke a long absence of such top-end events and reminded meteorologists and the public that even the northern Plains are capable of producing tornadoes of the maximum destructive intensity. The Enderlin tornado of June 20, 2025 will be remembered not only for its tragic impacts on a small community, but also for its historic intensity and the extensive scientific effort that went into understanding the full magnitude of its force.
