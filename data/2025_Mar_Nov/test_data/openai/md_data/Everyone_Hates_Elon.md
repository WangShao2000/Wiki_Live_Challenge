# Everyone Hates Elon
Everyone Hates Elon is a British political campaign and activist group formed in 2025 to oppose the influence of tech billionaire Elon Musk in politics[1]. The group emerged amid a public backlash to Musk’s involvement in far-right causes and his advisory role in U.S. President Donald Trump’s administration[2][3]. Everyone Hates Elon gained notoriety through provocative guerrilla marketing stunts and protests aimed at Musk and his companies, which have attracted widespread media attention and public debate[4][5].
## Background and Formation
Elon Musk’s political activities in 2024–2025 – including public interventions in British politics and support for controversial far-right figures – led to a sharp decline in his popularity in the UK[2]. A March 2025 YouGov poll indicated that 80% of Britons held an unfavorable view of Musk[6]. In this climate, a small collective of anonymous activists across Britain founded Everyone Hates Elon in early 2025, describing Musk as “a gruesome man” whose growing influence needed to be checked[7]. The group’s stated mission is to “stop [Musk] having this incredible influence over all of our lives” by rallying public opposition to his actions and ideology[7]. Everyone Hates Elon positions itself against extreme wealth and far-right politics, channeling public anger into satirical protests and fundraising for causes Musk is believed to oppose. The group has used its growing platform to raise thousands of pounds for charitable organizations such as Everyday Racism and Rainbow Migration – charities they say the billionaire would dislike – as part of their campaign[7].
## Campaigns and Activities
Everyone Hates Elon first drew attention in February 2025 with unauthorized anti-Musk posters on London’s public transport. One viral poster at a Bethnal Green bus stop depicted a Tesla car renamed the “Swasticar” – referencing Nazi imagery – and featured Musk in a Nazi-like salute pose, captioned, “Goes from 0 to 1939 in 3 seconds”[4][8]. The poster urged people “Don’t buy a Swasticar,” blatantly equating Tesla with far-right symbolism. The group took credit for these guerrilla ads, sharing photos of activists surreptitiously installing them in bus shelters and offering downloadable templates for supporters worldwide[9]. Transport for London officials confirmed the posters were not authorized and removed them when found, as they violated rules against political advertising[10]. Despite this, the satirical ads spread rapidly online, contributing to Everyone Hates Elon’s growing notoriety. Similar fake ads appeared on the Tube and at bus stops, mocking Musk’s relationship with Donald Trump (one parody ad read “Autopilot for your car. Autocrat for your country.”) and labeling Tesla as “the Swasticar” with slogans like “Now with White Power Steering”[11][12]. These stunts were designed to highlight Musk’s ties to far-right politics in a darkly humorous way and “say it how we see it,” according to the organizers[13].
In March 2025, the group escalated its activism with in-person pranks targeting Tesla showrooms. Everyone Hates Elon members entered a Tesla dealership in North London and attempted to install a life-size cardboard cutout of Musk giving the Nazi salute, pretending to be “revamping” the showroom’s décor[14]. Staff quickly realized the ruse and removed the cutout, but video of the prank – delivered in deadpan corporate style by an activist – went viral on social media[15][16]. In a follow-up stunt, a member brought satirical car air fresheners labeled “Musk-Be-Gone – Covers the stench of fascism” into another Tesla store, before being escorted out by security[17][18]. The group also distributed hundreds of stickers on Tesla vehicles in the UK bearing the slogan “Don’t Buy a Swasticar” to spread their message at ground level[19]. These guerrilla tactics combined humor and shock value to draw attention to Musk’s behavior; as one organizer explained, traditional activism “doesn’t reach enough people” whereas bold, funny messages like “Elon Musk is a bellend” (as one poster bluntly stated) are more memorable[20][21].
One of Everyone Hates Elon’s most publicized protests occurred in April 2025, when the group hosted a dramatic anti-Tesla “art project” titled London vs Musk. With the help of an anonymous donor, they acquired a used 2014 Tesla Model S that was destined for scrap and set up a public “rage room” event in South London where people could physically vent their frustration at Musk by smashing the car[5][19]. Dozens of participants lined up wearing protective helmets and took turns swinging sledgehammers and baseball bats at the electric vehicle, which had been stripped of its battery for safety[22][5]. A banner reading “Everyone Hates Elon” hung behind the wrecked Tesla, which was graffitied with phrases like “billionaires suck” and “pay your tax” during the destruction[23][24]. The group described the event as a form of catharsis and social commentary—“giving Londoners a chance to stand up to far-right hatred and billionaires and express how they feel about the current state of the world… Therapy is expensive, but this is free,” an Everyone Hates Elon spokesperson said of the smash-up protest[25]. Dubbed a “public art project” to spark debate about wealth inequality, the spectacle concluded with the Tesla’s remains set to be auctioned for charity (with proceeds donated to food banks)[5][26]. Notably, the campaign invited disillusioned Tesla owners to take the first swings, giving priority access to former Tesla customers who felt “betrayed” by Musk’s recent actions[27][28]. The London vs Musk stunt drew significant media coverage in the UK and abroad, underlining the depth of public anger toward Musk’s politics and the symbolic downfall of his brand.
Alongside these high-profile stunts, Everyone Hates Elon runs an ongoing crowdfunding initiative leveraging Musk’s prolific social media use. The group’s supporters can pledge a small donation (as little as £0.01) for every tweet Musk posts on X (Twitter), with the accumulated funds directed to “causes he hates” and to finance further protest actions[29][30]. By turning Musk’s own online activity into a fundraising trigger, the campaign aims to “turn the hate he spews… into something positive” and sustain their movement’s momentum[29]. Organizers reported that this scheme was on track to raise about £150,000 by the end of 2025, reflecting substantial grassroots support for their cause[31]. Money raised has gone toward producing posters, stickers, and organizing events, as well as donations to charities advocating for racial justice and migrant rights in direct opposition to Musk’s political stance[30]. The group often publicizes its actions on Instagram, TikTok, and Bluesky, where its satirical videos and updates have garnered millions of views[32].
In September 2025, Everyone Hates Elon broadened its protest scope to target Musk’s allies. Ahead of a state visit by President Donald Trump to the UK, the group crowdfunded and unveiled a giant banner displaying a photograph of Trump alongside convicted sex offender Jeffrey Epstein on the lawn near Windsor Castle[33]. This guerilla stunt – billed as “the world’s biggest photo” of Trump with Epstein – was intended to embarrass Trump and highlight the company he keeps, just as he arrived to meet King Charles III[33][34]. In a post on X (Twitter), Everyone Hates Elon quipped that Trump was “coming to the UK to avoid the Epstein story” and that “the British public just crowdfunded” an unwelcome reminder of it[35]. The enormous banner, unfurled on 15 September 2025, exemplified the group’s willingness to confront billionaire power beyond Musk himself, especially given Musk’s close alignment with Trump. The action received widespread attention online and underscored the group’s anti-establishment, anti-billionaire message on an international stage[34].
## Public Reaction and Impact
Everyone Hates Elon’s provocative campaign has fueled public discourse about Elon Musk’s role in politics and the responsibilities of billionaires. The group’s actions tapped into broader dissatisfaction with Musk: commentators noted that Musk’s alignment with far-right politics and his role in the Trump administration caused substantial damage to his personal brand and companies. In early 2025, Tesla’s reputation and sales were faltering in key markets, which observers partly attributed to backlash against Musk’s political behavior[36][37]. In Europe, Tesla vehicle sales dropped nearly 50% year-on-year in January 2025, at a time when the overall auto market was growing, reflecting a sharp decline in consumer interest[38][37]. Surveys showed that the percentage of car shoppers considering a Tesla fell from over 20% to single digits by mid-2025, coinciding with Musk’s controversial public statements[39]. The rise of Everyone Hates Elon and similar activist efforts formed part of this wave of resistance: protests calling for boycotts of Tesla took place on both sides of the Atlantic in 2025, including coordinated demonstrations at Tesla dealerships in London, Berlin, Paris, and multiple U.S. cities[40][41]. An American group known as “Tesla Takedown” organized an international day of action in April 2025, during which hundreds of Tesla showrooms were picketed in solidarity against Musk’s leadership[40][41]. Vandalism against Tesla vehicles was also reported (for example, a late-March incident in which over a dozen Teslas were burned in a suspected arson in Rome)[42]. In response, President Trump angrily condemned anti-Tesla protesters in the U.S., labeling those attacking the cars as “domestic terrorists,” and his administration directed federal law enforcement to investigate threats against Tesla[43]. This reaction underscored the political stakes of the conflict: Musk’s opponents framed their actions as resistance to authoritarianism and wealth inequality, while his allies portrayed the protesters as dangerous radicals.
Media coverage of Everyone Hates Elon has generally highlighted the creativity and humor of the group’s tactics, while also noting the legal and ethical gray areas of their methods. Outlets like The New Yorker and Byline Times have described the campaign as a form of guerrilla satire that cleverly channels public anger at the ultra-rich[11][44]. The group’s use of insults and Nazi analogies has been controversial; some observers question whether such provocative messaging crosses a line, even as others find it to be an effective critique of Musk’s flirtations with extremist rhetoric. The transport authorities in London made it clear that Everyone Hates Elon’s fake adverts were illicit (since political ads are not permitted on the network) and removed them promptly[10], but the speed with which new posters kept appearing showed the group’s persistence. Musk himself has not directly addressed the campaign group in public statements, and Tesla Inc. declined to comment on the stunts when asked by reporters[45]. However, Musk’s public actions suggest awareness of the mounting dissent: in April 2025, after Tesla’s steep profit drop and negative press, he announced he would be “spending less time” on his government role, a move interpreted by some as an attempt to stem further backlash[46].
By late 2025, Everyone Hates Elon had established itself as a noteworthy part of the political protest landscape in Britain. While its tongue-in-cheek name is deliberately provocative, the group’s activities have sparked serious conversations about the influence of billionaires on democracy and the rise of what they view as authoritarian politics. Their campaign combines street theatre, social media savvy, and crowdfunding to mobilize public sentiment. The efficacy of such tactics remains a point of debate, but Everyone Hates Elon has undoubtedly succeeded in keeping Elon Musk’s political conduct in the spotlight. As one co-founder put it, the group aims to be “funnier” than Musk in order to win public support in the attention economy[32]—turning outrage into action through irreverence. Whether or not their actions directly contributed to Tesla’s market troubles, Everyone Hates Elon exemplified a new wave of activist innovation in the mid-2020s, uniting disparate grievances under the banner of holding the world’s richest man to account.
