# National Geographic Society Headquarters
The National Geographic Society Headquarters is a historic complex of buildings in Washington, D.C., that serves as the headquarters for the National Geographic Society. Located on a city block between 16th and 17th Streets NW at M Street, the campus consists of four interconnected structures built over the 20th century[1][2]. The complex includes the original Renaissance Revival building from 1904, known as Hubbard Memorial Hall, and subsequent additions from 1912, 1963, and 1981–85 that reflect a range of architectural styles[1][2]. The headquarters houses the National Geographic Museum and the 400-seat Grosvenor Auditorium for public programs[3]. In 2023, the campus was added to the National Register of Historic Places in recognition of its architectural and historical significance[4].
## History
### Early Years and Expansion (1888–1940s)
The National Geographic Society was founded in Washington, D.C. in 1888, initially holding meetings at the Cosmos Club. It lacked a dedicated building until the Society opened its first headquarters in 1904[5]. This first building, Hubbard Memorial Hall, was named for the Society’s founding president Gardiner Greene Hubbard. Designed by the firm Hornblower & Marshall (also architects of the Smithsonian’s Natural History Museum), Hubbard Hall was a Renaissance Revival structure at the corner of 16th and M Streets NW[6]. As National Geographic Magazine’s popularity grew, membership surged from about 2,700 to 150,000 in just eight years, spurring the need for more space[7]. In 1912, the Society added a second building adjacent to Hubbard Hall to accommodate its expanding operations[8]. Washington architect Arthur B. Heaton designed the 1912 addition, complementing Hubbard Hall’s style with buff brick and limestone trim[1][9].
Over the following decades, National Geographic continued to expand its headquarters. In 1948, construction began on a new wing extending the 16th Street building[10]. Sometimes referred to as the “Editors’ Wing,” this expansion provided more office and editorial space as the magazine and Society staff grew. By mid-century, the Society’s campus had become a fixture on 16th Street, even playing an unusual role during the Cold War: from the 1960s to early 1970s, the FBI secretly operated a surveillance outpost in the National Geographic building to monitor the nearby Soviet embassy on 16th Street[11]. The headquarters also contained a fully equipped fallout shelter, complete with cots, showers, medical supplies and even a morgue, installed in the 1960s as a preparedness measure during the Cold War[12].
### Modern Expansion and Renovations (1960s–2000s)
In 1963, the Society undertook a major expansion with the construction of a new 11-story office and exhibition building on the west side of its campus (at 17th and M Streets). Designed by prominent modernist architect Edward Durell Stone, the 17th Street annex opened in 1964 and marked a dramatic departure in style from the Society’s older buildings[13][14]. Stone’s design – clad in white Vermont marble, black granite, and bronze – exemplified the New Formalism trend in modern architecture[15][16]. The structure features narrow vertical marble fins and recessed dark glass panels, creating a regular rhythm of bays, and a projecting flat roof over a colonnaded first-floor walkway[17][16]. The ground floor housed the Society’s Explorer’s Hall, an exhibit space open to the public as a small science museum and venue for Geographic’s famed photo displays and artifacts[2]. This mid-century building became the new centerpiece of National Geographic’s headquarters, reflecting the Society’s heightened stature; its opening was noted as a significant addition to Washington’s architecture and the Society’s mission[14].
By the late 1970s, National Geographic again needed more capacity. The Society demolished the aging 1940s editors’ wing and commissioned Skidmore, Owings & Merrill (SOM) to design a new office building and auditorium on the M Street side of the block[18][19]. Completed in 1981 (with final touches by 1984–85), the fourth major structure in the complex included a 400-seat theater for lectures and events (later named the Gilbert H. Grosvenor Auditorium) and a landscaped public plaza[19][20]. SOM’s design was notably different: a postmodern-style building with terraced, setback upper stories resembling a “concrete mountain,” and a broad outdoor plaza featuring plantings and large granite boulders as landscape elements[21][22]. In 1984, an installation artwork titled Marabar by sculptor Elyn Zimmerman – composed of carved granite boulders and a reflecting pool – was added to the plaza, enhancing the campus’s public space[22][23].
Throughout these expansions, the headquarters remained a hub of exploration and education. The Society’s explorers and researchers frequently gathered in Washington to share their findings; the auditorium and exhibition hall hosted countless lectures, film premieres, and events where returning explorers presented their stories. In some cases, the headquarters literally became a home away from home – for example, in the 2000s conservationist J. Michael Fay was known to sleep in a Society office while working on projects in D.C.[24].
### 21st Century Developments
In the 2010s, recognition of the headquarters’ historical and architectural importance grew. Hubbard Hall and the 1912 addition had long been contributing properties in the Sixteenth Street Historic District (a designation encompassing that corridor of notable buildings)[25]. In 2017 the D.C. Preservation League nominated the 1963 Edward Durell Stone building for historic landmark status, noting its exemplary New Formalist design and association with the National Geographic Society’s legacy[15][14]. Rather than list only that building, the Society pursued landmark designation for the entire campus, compiling a full history of the site[26]. By 2023, three of the central National Geographic buildings (the 1904, 1912, and 1963 structures) were officially listed on the National Register of Historic Places[4], recognizing the headquarters as a unique ensemble reflecting over a century of the Society’s presence in Washington.
Meanwhile, the Society embarked on a major overhaul of its campus to carry its mission into the future. In 2020, plans were unveiled to reimagine the headquarters – now nicknamed “Base Camp” – with expanded museum galleries and visitor experiences[27][28]. In October 2022, National Geographic broke ground on a new five-story pavilion along M Street, the first new building on the site in decades[29]. Dubbed the National Geographic Museum of Exploration, this modern wing at 1600 M Street NW will tie together all existing buildings and provide state-of-the-art exhibition space, an education center, a new entrance plaza, and other visitor amenities[29][30]. The design by Hickok Cole Architects aims to honor the historic 16th and 17th Street buildings while creating an immersive “wonder”-themed environment for the public[31]. As part of the renovation, the beloved Marabar stone installation was carefully removed in 2022 and relocated off-site (to American University’s campus) to make way for the redesigned courtyard[32]. The new museum pavilion and revamped campus are scheduled to open in mid-2026, coinciding with the Society’s 138th anniversary[29].
## Campus and Architecture
Hubbard Memorial Hall (1904): The oldest building of the headquarters is a three-story Renaissance Revival structure at 16th and M Streets, built in 1902–1904[1]. It was designed by Hornblower & Marshall and features a buff-colored brick façade with limestone trim and a red terracotta hipped roof, evocative of an Italian palazzo[33][34]. The interior originally housed the Society’s offices, map room, library, and meeting spaces. Hubbard Hall’s elegant classical style helped establish National Geographic’s image in the capital and has been preserved through subsequent renovations. It is a contributing property in the Sixteenth Street Historic District[25].
Administration Building (1912): Directly south of Hubbard Hall and connected to it, this extension provided much-needed office space as the Society grew. Designed by Arthur B. Heaton (with later enlargements in the 1930s and 1940s), the building maintained the Renaissance Revival aesthetic of Hubbard Hall[1]. Together, Hubbard Hall and the 16th Street additions present a unified historic streetscape of buff brick, large arched windows, and classical detailing facing 16th Street[33]. These structures housed editorial offices and the expanding cartographic department as National Geographic Magazine’s production ramped up in the early 20th century.
17th Street Building (1963): Formally known as the Explorers Hall building or M Street annex, the 10-story modernist structure fronting 17th Street NW is the most prominent element of the headquarters. Edward Durell Stone’s design is characterized by its white marble piers (fins) separating vertical strips of bronze-framed dark glass and black Swedish granite spandrels[17]. The building’s symmetry and colonnaded plaza give it a monumental, “temple-like” appearance, often cited as a noteworthy example of New Formalism in D.C. architecture[14][16]. An elevated marble-clad walkway surrounds the base, and the name "National Geographic Society" is engraved above the entrances[35][36]. Inside, the ground floor houses exhibition space (originally called Explorer’s Hall) and a lobby finished in the same Vermont marble. When it opened, this building allowed the Society to publicly showcase artifacts, photographs, and interactive displays from its expeditions, effectively creating a museum attraction for visitors. The 17th Street building continues to serve as the Society’s main administrative hub and features office floors above the public areas. In 2017, it was nominated as a D.C. historic landmark for its design and association with the Society[15][37], and in 2023 it was included in the National Register listing of the campus[4].
M Street Building and Plaza (1981–1985): The fourth major component of the complex is the building wrapping around the south and west sides of the campus, along M Street. Designed by Skidmore, Owings & Merrill and completed by 1985, it is a terraced mid-rise structure notable for its stepped-back form and integrated public plaza[21]. The M Street facade features pink-tinted concrete and glass, arranged in receding layers that create garden terraces on the upper levels[21]. At street level, SOM’s design prioritizes open space: a broad plaza and courtyard occupy the center of the block, furnished with geometric planters, trees, and seating which provide an oasis of greenery in downtown. A ring of large granite boulders (part of the Marabar art installation, 1984) had been a signature feature of the plaza until 2022[22][38]. Within the M Street building is the Gilbert H. Grosvenor Auditorium, a 400-seat theater named after the Society’s longtime president and editor. The auditorium has hosted National Geographic’s lecture series, film festivals, and special events for decades. It was outfitted with advanced audio-visual technology during recent renovations to continue serving as a premier venue for documentary screenings and talks[3]. The 1980s expansion not only gave the Society a state-of-the-art theater and new offices, but also created a unifying design element – the plaza – that linked all the campus buildings together with a pedestrian-friendly space.
Museum and Public Spaces: The National Geographic Museum, an evolution of the old Explorer’s Hall, is a public exhibition space that has been housed in the 17th Street building and adjacent areas. It features rotating exhibits on exploration, natural history, science, and photography, drawn from the Society’s vast archives and missions. As of the mid-2020s, this museum space is undergoing a major transformation and expansion. The new Museum of Exploration pavilion (opening 2026) will greatly enlarge the museum’s footprint and create a new entrance for visitors on M Street[29][30]. According to the Society, the expanded museum will offer interactive experiences, an immersive theater, educational facilities (the “Learning Launchpad”), and a showcase of the Society’s rich history of exploration[30]. The campus redesign will also introduce a refreshed outdoor courtyard featuring representations of global ecosystems and a giant version of National Geographic’s iconic yellow rectangular logo frame[30]. Despite these modern additions, the new construction is being built to integrate with and respect the historic architecture of the older buildings[31]. All exhibits and signage will be presented in multiple languages and with accessible design, aligning with National Geographic’s educational mission.
## Historic Designation and Legacy
The National Geographic Society Headquarters stands as an architectural chronicle of the Society’s evolution and its role in Washington’s cultural landscape. The complex’s blend of Beaux-Arts classicism, mid-century modernism, and late-20th-century design reflects successive eras of the Society’s growth. In April 2023, the headquarters (notably Hubbard Hall, the 1912 building, and the Edward Durell Stone building) was officially listed on the U.S. National Register of Historic Places[4]. The D.C. Historic Preservation Review Board recognized the site under multiple criteria: for its association with National Geographic’s influential history in science and education, and for the distinctive architectural qualities of its buildings (such as Stone’s New Formalist design)[14][39]. Prior to this, the 16th Street frontage was already protected within a historic district, and the Society had maintained the historic character of those structures for many years[25]. With the landmark designation, the entire campus’s preservation is ensured even as it undergoes modernization.
Over more than a century, the headquarters has not only been the administrative center of National Geographic, but also a symbolic “base camp” for exploration. It is the place where famous explorers, scientists, photographers, and educators have gathered to share knowledge – from early 20th-century expeditions to Machu Picchu, to 21st-century initiatives in conservation and technology. The Society’s leadership, including figures like Gilbert H. Grosvenor and his successors, operated from these halls while expanding the reach of National Geographic Magazine and related media to millions of people worldwide[40][24]. The buildings themselves have appeared in media and guidebooks as landmarks in downtown Washington. As the headquarters enters its next phase with the Museum of Exploration, it continues to bridge its storied legacy with forward-looking engagement, remaining a notable institution at the intersection of science, education, and public inspiration.
