# Closures of the University of Wisconsin branch campuses
## Background
The University of Wisconsin System (branded as the “Universities of Wisconsin”) historically included 13 two-year branch campuses spread across the state. These institutions – originally founded in the 1940s as extension centers or community colleges – were meant to broaden access to public higher education in line with the “Wisconsin Idea.” They served as affordable feeder schools for the UW’s four-year universities, allowing students in rural and smaller communities to begin college close to home. In 2018, amid ongoing enrollment declines, the UW System merged these 13 two-year colleges into branch campuses of seven four-year universities as a cost-saving and efficiency measure[1][2]. Despite this restructuring, enrollment at most branch campuses continued to fall sharply in the 2010s and early 2020s. Collectively, the two-year campuses’ student headcount dropped from over 13,000 in 2010 to around 5,000 by fall 2022[3]. Several branch campuses saw declines of 60–70% in enrollment over the decade, reflecting statewide demographic changes and student preference shifts toward larger universities or online programs[4][5]. At the same time, state budget support for the UW System stagnated. A tuition freeze combined with funding cuts (including a $66 million cut in 2013) constrained campus budgets, a policy that analysts say disproportionately harmed the small two-year campuses’ viability[6][1]. By the early 2020s, many branch campuses were operating with severely reduced resources, faculty, and course offerings, contributing to a feedback loop of further enrollment declines. University leaders and state officials began debating how to respond to these trends, with some proposing campus mergers or partnerships with local technical colleges to avoid outright closures[7]. Ultimately, however, a series of campus closure decisions were made starting in 2022 as enrollment and financial pressures became unsustainable.
## Initial Closure – UW–Platteville Richland
The first branch campus to shut down was UW–Platteville Richland, located in Richland Center, Wisconsin. In November 2022, UW–Platteville announced it would end in-person instruction at the Richland campus due to extremely low enrollment[8]. At the time, only about 60 students were enrolled at Richland (down from over 500 in the mid-2010s)[9]. The Richland branch officially closed in the summer of 2023, making it the first UW System campus to close in decades. The closure caught local officials by surprise – Richland County leaders said they were not informed until the decision went public and lamented the sudden loss of the community’s higher education center[10]. The shutdown of UW–Platteville Richland raised alarm that other two-year campuses might face a similar fate[11]. It also left the surrounding rural region without a nearby public college option for the first time since the 1960s. Richland’s closure became a precedent for the UW System’s approach to its struggling branch campuses going forward.
## Wave of Closures in 2023–2024
Following Richland’s demise, a broader wave of branch campus closures and cutbacks unfolded throughout 2023 and 2024. In October 2023, Universities of Wisconsin President Jay Rothman issued a directive addressing three additional sites: he permanently closed the Richland campus (affirming the earlier decision) and announced that two other branches – UW–Milwaukee at Washington County and UW–Oshkosh Fond du Lac – would end in-person classes by June 30, 2024[12][13]. After spring 2024, those two campuses were to cease face-to-face instruction and transition any remaining programs or students to other institutions (e.g. online or main campuses). This directive acknowledged that the affected centers had experienced steep enrollment losses (each declining to only a few hundred students) and persistent financial shortfalls[4][14]. Around the same time, UW–Green Bay decided that its Marinette branch campus would also suspend in-person instruction after the 2023–24 academic year, moving all courses online due to low enrollment at that location[15][16].
In early 2024, yet another branch closure was put on the horizon: UW–Milwaukee announced plans to shut down its Waukesha two-year campus. Citing ongoing declines in enrollment and limited funds, UWM leadership stated in February 2024 that the Waukesha campus would be fully closed by the end of June 2025[17]. A teach-out period allowed Waukesha’s remaining students to finish the 2024–25 year, after which the campus would no longer operate. Then in June 2024, UW–Oshkosh revealed it would terminate in-person teaching at its Fox Cities branch campus in Menasha. According to local officials, UWO’s Fox Cities campus was slated to halt all classes by June 30, 2025 and effectively close, making it the sixth UW two-year campus marked for closure in this period[18][19]. (UW–Oshkosh had already ended face-to-face instruction at its smaller Fond du Lac campus in May 2024, consolidating those resources to the main Oshkosh campus[20].)
By mid-2024, the toll among the UW System’s branch campuses was unprecedented. In addition to Richland, the following two-year campuses had closed, announced impending closure, or stopped on-site classes: Washington County, Waukesha, Fond du Lac, Fox Cities (Menasha), and Marinette[21]. All were suffering from dwindling student numbers (for example, UW–Washington County’s enrollment fell from about 1,000 students in 2012 to just 332 by 2022[22]). Universities of Wisconsin officials explained that these difficult steps were necessary to “realign [the branch campuses] to current market realities” – noting that most new students now either enroll at a four-year university or choose online programs, leaving the two-year centers underutilized[23][24]. By August 2025, a WPR report summarized that six of the 13 branch campuses had either closed or were in the process of closing due to “declining enrollment and budget shortfalls” during this two-year span[25].
## Developments in 2025: Baraboo Closure and Campus Relocations
The trend of branch campus contractions continued into late 2025. In October of that year, UW–Platteville announced it would close the Baraboo–Sauk County branch campus at the end of the 2025–26 academic year[26]. The Baraboo campus (located in West Baraboo, near Wisconsin Dells) had seen its enrollment plunge to only 116 students in fall 2025 – a stark drop from a peak of around 758 students in 2000[27]. University officials stated that such low enrollment could no longer justify keeping the campus open, despite the site’s long history. Baraboo became the second branch under UW–Platteville to be shuttered (following Richland Center), and by one count it was the eighth UW branch campus to be closed or substantially downsized since 2023[28]. Operations at Baraboo–Sauk County were scheduled to cease in May 2026, with plans to assist remaining students in transferring to UW–Platteville’s main campus or other institutions[29][30]. Local county leaders, who had recently invested funds to cover the campus’s budget deficit, were disappointed by the closure but began discussing future uses for the facility[31].
Not every branch campus in 2025 was closed outright; in at least one case the solution was relocation and partnership. In September 2025, UW–Stevens Point announced that its Wausau branch campus would move to the nearby Northcentral Technical College (NTC) campus in Wausau in fall 2026[32]. Instead of shutting down, UWSP at Wausau will co-locate with NTC and continue offering UW courses there, allowing students to seamlessly transition from a two-year technical associate’s program to four-year bachelor’s coursework at the same site[33][34]. University officials cast this as a collaborative model to preserve local access in Marathon County despite the Wausau campus’s enrollment having fallen by over 70% in the past decade[5]. (By contrast, UW–Stevens Point’s smaller Marshfield branch was still operating independently as of 2025, with no merger plans announced[35].) Another example of this adaptive approach was seen in Waukesha: after closing the UW–Milwaukee at Waukesha campus, UWM forged a partnership with Waukesha County Technical College (WCTC) to open a University Center on the WCTC campus. Debuting in late 2025, this center allows UWM to continue offering a selection of bachelor’s degree programs to students in the Waukesha area, using hybrid online and on-site classes at the technical college[36][37]. These initiatives represent an effort to maintain a presence in communities that lost UW branch campuses, albeit in a different format than the traditional two-year college model.
By the end of 2025, the University of Wisconsin System’s footprint of two-year campuses had shrunk dramatically. Only seven of the original 13 branch campuses remained fully operational with in-person instruction[38]. The surviving campuses (such as UW–Eau Claire – Barron County, UW–Green Bay – Manitowoc and Sheboygan, UW–Whitewater – Rock County, UW–Stevens Point – Marshfield, etc.) were generally those with somewhat higher enrollment or community support, but they too faced uncertainty. University administrators indicated that the future of the remaining branches would depend on “community needs and the ability of the Universities of Wisconsin to meet those needs,” leaving open the possibility of further consolidations or program changes to come[39][40].
## Impact and Reactions
The closure of multiple UW branch campuses in quick succession has generated significant concern about the impact on students, communities, and Wisconsin’s higher education system. Educational access emerged as a primary worry. Many of these two-year campuses were the only public college option in their respective counties or regions, serving place-bound students who could not easily commute to a main campus. Observers noted that when a local branch campus closes, it “completely eliminates an entire option” for high school graduates and working adults in that area to pursue a college degree[41][42]. The branch campuses often provided a low-cost, open-admissions entry point to higher education; their loss may especially affect nontraditional students and those from rural or lower-income backgrounds. Early evidence suggested that some displaced students transferred successfully to other UW campuses or online programs, but others simply dropped out or never enrolled due to the added distance and inconvenience.
Local economic and social impacts have also been significant. Aside from educating students, the small UW campuses employed faculty and staff and contributed to the local economy. Each closure meant job losses in the dozens (or more) for the host community, from instructors to support personnel. For example, hundreds of employees across the affected campuses have been left without jobs as facilities shut down[43]. Small businesses in some towns feared the loss of student and faculty spending, and community groups lamented losing the cultural and educational events that the campuses hosted. Richland Center’s mayor described the closure of UW–Richland as “heartbreaking” for the community, and similar sentiments were echoed in Waukesha, Fond du Lac, and other affected areas. County governments, which typically own the branch campus buildings, suddenly found themselves responsible for vacant facilities. In Washington County, officials reported spending nearly \$750,000 per year on maintenance and utilities for the empty West Bend campus after its closure, pending a decision on how to repurpose the property[44][45]. This has put pressure on local governments to find new tenants or buyers for former UW sites. (In one case, Washington County moved to sell the ex-UW campus to a private Christian school and a conservation group, to recoup costs[46][47].)
Reactions from state and local leaders have been mixed. Some Republican legislators and UW System officials have defended the closures as unfortunate but necessary adjustments. They argue that it is fiscally responsible to consolidate resources given that branch campus enrollments have become unsustainably low. State Senator Duey Stroebel, for instance, said it was “not surprising” that UW–Washington County had to close its doors and urged stakeholders to transition to a “more sustainable model” for delivering higher education locally[48]. Many of these leaders encourage partnerships with technical colleges or expanding online education as pragmatic solutions. On the other hand, Democratic lawmakers and some community advocates have criticized the closures, attributing them to policy choices. State Senator Kelda Roys contended that years of state disinvestment in public universities – driven by Republicans’ budget cuts – “manufactured” the financial crises that led to the branch campuses shutting down[49][50]. She and others argue that the closures could have been averted with better funding and a stronger commitment to the Wisconsin Idea of educational access. Local county executives have also voiced frustration. Outagamie County Executive Tom Nelson, reacting to the Fox Cities campus announcement, warned that losing that campus would put the “future of the UW System in northeast Wisconsin in jeopardy,” and he criticized UW–Oshkosh for poor communication during the decision process[51][52]. In neighboring Winnebago County, however, Executive Jon Doemel acknowledged the closure as inevitable, given the changing higher ed landscape, even if it was a painful outcome[53]. These divergent viewpoints highlight a broader debate over whether the UW System’s contraction is a result of unavoidable demographic and market forces or the product of political choices and mismanagement.
The UW System and state government have taken some steps to mitigate the damage of the branch campus closures. To address the issue of empty campus facilities, Wisconsin lawmakers in 2023–24 approved financial support for affected communities. In 2024, the state legislature passed a bill providing grants of $2 million to each county that lost a UW two-year campus, aimed at helping local officials redevelop or find new uses for the vacated properties[54][45]. This funding was initially inspired by the situation in Richland County and later expanded to include other closures (Fond du Lac, Washington County, etc.), with additional funds set aside anticipating future campus losses. Meanwhile, the Universities of Wisconsin have emphasized efforts to maintain student opportunities despite campus shutdowns. They have increased promotion of transfer pathways so that branch campus students can move to a four-year university or the online UW College virtually without losing credits[55][56]. New “University Center” hubs at technical colleges (like those in Waukesha and planned in other regions) are intended to keep a UW presence in communities by offering junior/senior-level courses in partnership with local institutions[36][57]. Additionally, some branches that remain open have expanded dual-enrollment programs with high schools or added targeted bachelor’s programs, in hopes of drawing more students. Despite these adaptive measures, the overarching outcome is that the University of Wisconsin System’s physical footprint in smaller communities has significantly contracted. As a Wisconsin Public Radio analysis noted in late 2025, only seven of the 13 original two-year campuses were still operating normally – marking a substantial retreat from the UW System’s once-statewide reach[58]. Alumni, educators, and residents in affected areas continue to watch these developments closely, concerned about the long-term consequences for Wisconsin’s commitment to accessible public higher education.
