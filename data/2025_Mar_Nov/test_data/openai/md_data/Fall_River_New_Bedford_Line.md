# Fall River/New Bedford Line
The Fall River/New Bedford Line (formerly the Middleborough/Lakeville Line) is a commuter rail line of the Massachusetts Bay Transportation Authority (MBTA) serving southeastern Massachusetts in the United States. It runs from Boston’s South Station through the South Shore region to a junction at East Taunton, then splits into two branches terminating in the cities of Fall River and New Bedford[1]. The line was extended and renamed in March 2025 as part of the South Coast Rail project, restoring passenger train service to Fall River and New Bedford for the first time since 1958[2]. The 2025 extension added six new stations and about 37 miles of track to the MBTA commuter rail network[3][1]. The project cost approximately $1.1 billion and had been in planning for decades, with initial projections in the 1990s targeting a 1997 opening[4]. Trains on the line take roughly 90 to 95 minutes to travel between South Station and either Fall River or New Bedford[5]. The Fall River/New Bedford Line operates in MBTA fare Zone 8 (the outermost zone), with a one-way full fare of $12.25 (or $6 for reduced-fare passengers) as of 2025[5].
## Route and Service
Map of the Fall River/New Bedford Line route. The line shares its initial route with other Old Colony Lines, beginning at South Station in Boston and running south through Quincy and Braintree. It then continues along the former Old Colony Railroad mainline through Brockton and Bridgewater to Middleborough. A new Middleborough station was constructed slightly north of the old Middleborough/Lakeville stop, to enable the connection toward Fall River and New Bedford[1]. At East Taunton, the route branches: one Fall River branch heads southwest through Freetown to the Fall River Depot terminal, and the New Bedford branch runs south through a Church Street stop into downtown New Bedford, where it terminates at New Bedford station[1]. These were all newly built stations opened with the 2025 project, in addition to East Taunton and the relocated Middleborough station, totaling six new stations[1]. The existing stations between Boston and Middleborough (such as Braintree, Brockton, and Bridgewater) remained part of the line. Fall River, New Bedford, and Taunton had been the largest Massachusetts cities within 50 miles of Boston without commuter rail service until this extension was completed[1].
MBTA commuter trains on the Fall River/New Bedford Line provide a “one-seat” ride from the South Coast region to Boston. On weekdays, a full schedule of direct trains serves both branches: typically 15 inbound/outbound trips per day on the Fall River side and 17 on the New Bedford side[6]. In total, 32 daily trains run between South Station and the East Taunton junction on weekdays, with trains generally operating about every 70 minutes during commuter hours[6]. On weekends, service is less frequent (about every two hours), with a combined 26 trips per day between Boston and East Taunton and alternating service to each branch[6]. The final departures each night leave South Station around midnight to reach New Bedford and Fall River in the late night hours[7][8]. In addition to the direct Boston trains, some shuttle train trips are scheduled between East Taunton and the branch endpoints to augment service frequency; East Taunton station functions as a transfer point in certain off-peak times[9][10]. All trains on the line are diesel-operated, as the route is not electrified (consistent with the rest of the MBTA’s Old Colony commuter lines). The line’s new stations offer parking facilities, and initial promotions included free parking and free fares at the new South Coast stations for a limited time after opening[11].
Early ridership figures have shown a significant increase in usage after the line’s launch. By mid-2025, the MBTA was recording roughly 2,000 more daily passenger trips on the Fall River/New Bedford corridor compared to the previous year, when the service only ran to Middleborough/Lakeville[12]. This represents about a 33% rise in ridership along the line, at a time when overall MBTA commuter rail ridership was slightly declining[13]. Weekday commuter trips accounted for most of the surge (over one-third higher than before), indicating strong demand from South Coast residents traveling to jobs in the Boston area[14][15]. Local officials have welcomed the new service as a means to improve regional connectivity and economic opportunity, though transportation analysts note that a shift from highways to rail will take time; highway traffic data in late 2025 did not yet show a noticeable decrease in car volumes on routes from the South Coast to Boston[16][17].
## History and Development
Regular passenger rail service to New Bedford, Fall River, and Taunton was discontinued in the late 1950s, when the New York, New Haven and Hartford Railroad ended its Old Colony Division commuter operations due to financial losses[18]. The last train from New Bedford ran in September 1958[18], marking the end of an era in which those cities had been connected to Boston by rail for about a century. In the decades that followed, southeastern Massachusetts became the only major region around Greater Boston without commuter rail access. State transportation officials periodically studied restoring service, and by the late 1980s the MBTA had initial plans to extend the Stoughton Line through Taunton as a direct route to the South Coast[19]. In 1991, Governor William Weld publicly committed to have commuter rail running to Fall River and New Bedford by 1997[20], and some state bond funding was authorized for the project in the mid-1990s[21]. Preliminary work even began in 1998, when a groundbreaking was held and bridge upgrades started, despite the route not being finalized at the time[22]. However, the ambitious schedule proved untenable. In 2003, the plan was halted by Governor Mitt Romney, citing budget constraints and the MBTA’s inability to finance new expansions at that time[23]. This pause left the South Coast Rail initiative stalled for several years.
The project regained momentum in the late 2000s. In 2007, Governor Deval Patrick’s administration re-launched the South Coast Rail planning process[24], affirming it as a priority for the state and estimating the cost at $1.4 billion[25]. Over the next several years, detailed environmental reviews and engineering plans were developed. By 2014, the Massachusetts legislature had authorized $2.3 billion in capital funding for the project as cost projections grew[26][27]. A major decision came in 2017, when the MBTA and MassDOT elected to pursue the South Coast Rail in two phases[28]. Phase I would use the existing Middleborough/Lakeville Line right-of-way to provide service to New Bedford and Fall River more quickly, while Phase II would later build a more direct route (via the previously planned Stoughton Line extension) in the future[29]. The two-phase approach was somewhat controversial locally—some officials in New Bedford were skeptical of the indirect Phase I route—but it allowed construction to begin sooner on the available rights-of-way[30]. Under the Phase I plan, trains from Boston would travel the longer Old Colony route via Middleborough, and a new connection from Middleborough to East Taunton would link to the South Coast branches. Phase II (the “Full Build”) was envisioned to follow in the 2030s, providing a straighter path from Boston through Stoughton and Taunton with electrified tracks and additional stations, reducing travel times by 15–25 minutes[31][32].
Construction of Phase I officially broke ground in mid-2019 after the necessary permits and funding were in place[33]. Early action work included upgrading existing freight lines, replacing track, and building new bridges to handle passenger service. By late 2022, as the project neared completion, voters in New Bedford and Fall River overwhelmingly approved joining the MBTA service district in their communities[34]. This local referendum was required to integrate the cities into the MBTA network and commit to the associated local funding formula. The physical construction of tracks, signals, and stations for Phase I was largely finished by the end of 2023. Testing and crew training took several additional months, and the start of service, originally hoped for late 2023, was postponed slightly into the next year[35]. The Fall River/New Bedford Line officially opened for passenger service on March 24, 2025[36][35]. Opening ceremonies were held at the new stations, and the inaugural trains from New Bedford (departing at 4:27 a.m.) and Fall River (5:05 a.m.) carried enthusiastic first-day riders including local officials[3][37]. The MBTA offered free rides during the first week of operations and on all weekends in April 2025 to encourage the public to try the new service[36][11]. It had been roughly 65 years since residents of the South Coast cities last had passenger rail, a milestone noted by officials at the launch[2].
In the months following the launch, some operational challenges emerged. The MBTA had to adjust schedules by mid-2025 to improve reliability and address community feedback. In July 2025, the agency modified the timetable by shifting some departure times and eliminating two late-night shuttle trains that had been running from East Taunton to the branch termini after midnight[38][39]. These changes were made to reduce noise disturbances in residential areas near the tracks and to improve on-time performance[40][41]. Despite early hiccups such as a period of crew shortages causing a few train cancellations in April 2025[42],[43] the line’s performance stabilized, and by mid-year the MBTA reported more consistent operations on the new service.
## Future Plans
The Fall River/New Bedford Line as opened in 2025 represents the first phase of South Coast Rail, and planning continues for further improvements. Phase II of the project (the “Full Build”) will create a more direct rail corridor from Boston to the South Coast by extending the MBTA Stoughton Branch south through Easton and Taunton to connect with the Phase I route at East Taunton. This full build would include several additional stations (for example, a station in downtown Taunton is anticipated, among others along the new route) and would shorten the travel distance to Fall River and New Bedford. It is also mandated to be an electrified line, as required by a state law for the Stoughton extension[44]. Phase II is not yet funded and, as of 2025, has no firm timeline for construction; MBTA officials have indicated it will not open until the 2030s at the earliest[31][32]. The realization of Phase II will depend on future capital funding and the resolution of environmental permitting challenges (the Stoughton route passes through sensitive wetlands that were a source of delay in the past)[31].
There are also proposals to extend commuter rail service beyond the current termini. One active idea is to further extend the Middleborough branch southward to reach Buzzards Bay on Cape Cod. The CapeFlyer seasonal train already uses the Cape Cod rail line in summer, but local lawmakers have suggested making year-round service to the Buzzards Bay area a part of the MBTA system[45]. In 2023 a bill was introduced in the state legislature to require the MBTA to run commuter trains from Middleborough (Lakeville) to Buzzards Bay with several daily trips, as a first step toward eventually serving other Cape Cod towns[45]. This proposal remains under study and would require additional agreements (such as track upgrades and coordination with the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers for the Cape Cod Canal railroad bridge). If pursued, a Buzzards Bay extension could potentially be operated as an offshoot of the Fall River/New Bedford Line, since it would branch from the same vicinity near Middleborough. As of late 2025, no funding has been allocated for this expansion, but local interest in better rail connections to Cape Cod continues to be voiced.
