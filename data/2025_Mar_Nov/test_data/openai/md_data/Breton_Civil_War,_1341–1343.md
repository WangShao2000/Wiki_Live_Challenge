# Breton Civil War, 1341–1343
The Breton Civil War (1341–1343) was a dynastic conflict in the Duchy of Brittany, arising from a disputed succession to the ducal title. It broke out in 1341 after Duke John III of Brittany died on April 30 without direct heirs and with two rival claimants to succeed him[1]. One claimant was John of Montfort, the duke’s half-brother, and the other was Charles of Blois, who claimed the duchy through his wife, Jeanne de Penthièvre (John III’s niece)[1]. The dispute over Brittany became entangled in the broader context of the Hundred Years’ War, as King Edward III of England backed Montfort’s cause while King Philip VI of France supported Charles[2]. The initial phase of the Breton succession war lasted until a papally mediated truce in 1343, after which open hostilities in Brittany paused. This first phase, often termed the War of the Two Jeannes (referring to Jeanne de Penthièvre and Jeanne of Flanders, Montfort’s wife), ended inconclusively with neither claimant decisively victorious[3]. The wider war over the Breton succession resumed in subsequent years and ultimately concluded in 1364 with a Montfort victory, but during 1341–1343 the conflict reached a temporary stalemate under the Truce of Malestroit[4][5].
## Succession Dispute and Outbreak of War (1341)
The succession crisis was triggered by the death of Duke John III, who had no children. Complicating matters, he had at different times suggested different successors: his half-brother John of Montfort and his niece Jeanne de Penthièvre (whose husband was Charles of Blois)[6][7]. Upon the duke’s death in April 1341, both sides pressed their claims, plunging Brittany into a civil war of succession. Initially, John of Montfort moved swiftly to seize control of the duchy. Accompanied by his wife Jeanne of Flanders, he secured the ducal treasury at Nantes and occupied numerous key towns and castles across Brittany, including Rennes, Vannes, Hennebont, Brest, and Dol-de-Bretagne[7]. In May 1341 an assembly of local burgesses and minor nobles in Nantes acknowledged Montfort as the new Duke of Brittany[7]. However, most of the higher nobility of Brittany hesitated or sided with the Penthièvre-Blois faction; indeed, Montfort’s support among the great lords was only marginal, while many nobles rallied to Charles of Blois’s claim backed by royal French approval[8].
Alarmed by Montfort’s actions and his appeal to England for support, King Philip VI of France decided to intervene. He summoned both claimants to Paris in an attempt to adjudicate the dispute peacefully[9]. When Montfort refused to abandon his claim, Philip VI charged him with treason for allying with England, and in a decisive move the French king formally recognized Charles of Blois as Duke of Brittany while ordering Montfort’s arrest[10]. In mid-1341, Montfort was thus imprisoned in Paris, and Philip invested his nephew Charles with the duchy’s title[10]. For a brief moment, it appeared the French crown had settled the succession by diktat, before any major battles occurred.
John of Montfort, however, managed to escape captivity in Paris and returned to Brittany later in 1341[11]. His escape reignited the war, prompting King Philip to send a French royal army to enforce the decision. Charles of Blois, now accompanied by French forces, entered Brittany to subdue Montfort’s supporters. By the autumn of 1341, a French-led host of reportedly over 7,000 men laid siege to Nantes, where Montfort had taken refuge[12]. The French made an example of captured Montfort partisans – executing prisoners in view of the city walls – to intimidate the defenders into surrender[13]. Facing overwhelming force and internal pressure, Montfort capitulated at Nantes in November 1341. He was taken prisoner yet again and sent to confinement in the Louvre in Paris[13]. With Montfort removed from the field, King Philip VI declared Charles of Blois the rightful duke. By the end of 1341, the Montfortist cause appeared crippled: their leader was in captivity, and much of Brittany had been overrun by forces loyal to Charles and the French crown.
## War of the Two Jeannes and English Intervention (1342)
John of Montfort’s imprisonment shifted the focus of the war to two remarkable women – Jeanne of Flanders and Jeanne de Penthièvre – whose efforts in 1342 lent the conflict its nickname “War of the Two Jeannes”[3]. Montfort’s wife, Jeanne of Flanders (also known as “Jeanne la Flamme” for her fiery spirit), refused to concede defeat after her husband’s capture[14]. She assumed leadership of the Montfortist faction, rallying the remaining supporters of her family’s claim. Despite the loss of many castles, the Montfortists held out in a few strongholds, notably the fortified coastal town of Hennebont in southern Brittany and the port of Brest in the west[3]. Jeanne of Flanders made Hennebont her base and prepared to withstand a coming assault by Charles of Blois’s forces[15].
In spring 1342, Charles of Blois, supported by the French, besieged Hennebont in an effort to crush the last Montfort resistance[16]. Jeanne of Flanders oversaw Hennebont’s defense with notable resolve. Donning armor and directing both soldiers and townspeople, she bolstered morale and put everyone to work strengthening the fortifications[16]. During the siege, Jeanne led a bold sortie: with a small cavalry force of around 300, she sortied out of Hennebont, surprised the besiegers’ lightly guarded camp, and set fire to the enemy’s supplies and tents[17]. This daring attack earned her the nickname “La Flamme” (“the Flame”) and disrupted the besieging army[17]. Although Charles’s troops regrouped and Jeanne had to retreat, she managed to slip out and ride to the Montfortist-held port of Brest, where she gathered reinforcements. She soon returned with fresh troops and supplies, further prolonging the defense of Hennebont[18]. Jeanne’s courageous actions bought critical time, but by early summer 1342 the situation in Hennebont was growing dire as French forces continued the siege.
The intervention of England at this juncture proved decisive in preventing a total Montfortist collapse. In late June 1342, an English fleet – sent by Edward III, who saw Brittany as a new front in his war with France – arrived off the Breton coast. The English ships appeared at Hennebont, commanded by an English admiral (often identified as Sir Walter Manny)[19]. The arrival of English reinforcements by sea lifted the spirits of the defenders. Confronted by the sudden presence of English troops in Brittany, the French besiegers abandoned the siege of Hennebont and withdrew[20][21]. Jeanne of Flanders and her allies thus survived the crisis, and the momentum of the war shifted once again. By mid-1342, House Montfort – with English backing – had regained a foothold in the conflict even as two-thirds of Brittany remained loyal to the Blois-Penthièvre side[21].
Over the next months, England escalated its military involvement. In August 1342, a larger English expeditionary force under William de Bohun, Earl of Northampton, landed at the Breton port of Brest to reinforce the Montfortists[22]. Northampton brought roughly 1,300 English troops, soon joined by additional men under the exiled Breton noble Sir Robert of Artois, bringing the English force to around 2,000 soldiers[22]. Their landing forced Charles of Blois – who had been besieging Brest – to lift that siege and fall back[23]. The newly arrived English and Breton Montfortist forces then took the offensive. In September 1342, Northampton led a campaign inland and laid siege to the pro-Blois stronghold of Morlaix in northern Brittany[24]. This provoked Charles of Blois to respond with a substantial French army (including French, Breton, and Genoese troops) that greatly outnumbered the English contingent[24]. The two sides clashed at the Battle of Morlaix on 30 September 1342. The engagement was hard-fought and ended inconclusively – the English used defensive tactics (dismounted men-at-arms and longbowmen in wooded ground) to blunt the French assaults, and both armies eventually disengaged without a decisive victory[25]. The French suffered heavy losses (reportedly around 50 knights killed), but the English were unable to force a complete rout[25]. Strategically, the Battle of Morlaix demonstrated English military effectiveness, yet it failed to settle the Breton succession issue, leaving the combatants in a continued stalemate.
By late 1342, the war in Brittany had drawn in ever larger forces and was effectively a proxy theater of the Hundred Years’ War[2][21]. In November 1342, King Edward III himself arrived in Brittany with additional English troops, raising the English presence to about 5,000 men[26]. Edward’s army seized control of much of western Brittany and laid siege to Vannes, an important town held by Charles of Blois[26]. In response, King Philip VI dispatched his son, John, Duke of Normandy (the French heir), with a large royal French army to Brittany. By the end of 1342, the French and English kings — through their lieutenants — faced each other in a tense stand-off (Edward III holding Vannes, and the French concentrating near Nantes)[27]. Both sides were wary of escalating to a full-scale pitched battle in the winter. The intervention of the Papacy helped pave the way for a pause in the fighting: Pope Clement VI offered to mediate, anxious to check the bloodshed between Christian kingdoms.
## Truce of Malestroit (1343)
On 19 January 1343, representatives of England and France agreed to the Truce of Malestroit, which suspended hostilities in Brittany and elsewhere. The truce was brokered by envoys of Pope Clement VI and accepted by both King Edward III and King Philip VI[4]. Under the terms of this ceasefire, each side would retain control of the territories and strongholds they currently occupied in Brittany as of the truce’s signing[28]. This effectively froze the military situation: the Montfortists (with their English allies) held much of Brittany’s western half (including Brest and other coastal towns, and Vannes which was entrusted to papal custody during the truce), while Charles of Blois and the French maintained control of eastern and central Brittany[27][4]. The Truce of Malestroit was intended to last until mid-1346, providing a breather in the wider Hundred Years’ War[4].
The truce brought temporary relief to war-torn Brittany but failed to resolve the underlying succession dispute. Notably, the question of who was the legitimate Duke of Brittany remained unsettled – the treaty did not award the duchy to either side definitively[29]. For the Montfort faction, however, the ceasefire had one positive outcome: Duke John of Montfort, who had been languishing as a prisoner in Paris, was released from captivity in late 1343 as part of the truce arrangements[29]. Montfort was reunited with his wife Jeanne of Flanders after two years apart, and he traveled to England, where he continued to seek King Edward’s support for his restoration[30].
Despite the formal truce, sporadic clashes and local unrest in Brittany did not entirely cease. Tensions remained high, and each faction mistrusted the other’s intentions. The lull in major operations lasted only a short while. Within a couple of years, the conflict would reignite in Brittany as the broader war between England and France resumed.
## Aftermath and Continuation of the War
The 1341–1343 Breton civil war ended in an uneasy stalemate, setting the stage for further conflict. When the Truce of Malestroit expired, hostilities resumed in 1345[31]. John of Montfort returned to Brittany with a contingent of English support and attempted to rally his partisans again. However, Montfort did not live to see the final victory of his cause – he died in September 1345, reportedly of illness, at Hennebont during the renewed fighting[32]. Leadership of the Montfortist claim passed to his young son, John (Jean) de Montfort, while Jeanne of Flanders, suffering from mental health issues possibly brought on by the stresses of war, was sidelined from active command (King Edward III had her confined in England for the remainder of the war)[31][33].
The conflict for the Breton succession continued intermittently for over two decades beyond 1343. It became fully integrated into the Hundred Years’ War, with England and France backing their respective Breton proxies. Notable later episodes included the Battle of La Roche-Derrien (1347), where Charles of Blois was captured by the English, and the legendary chivalric combat known as the Combat of the Thirty (1351) between partisans of each side. Ultimately, the civil war reached its climax at the Battle of Auray on 29 September 1364, where the Montfortist forces (led by John of Montfort’s now-adult son, John IV, allied with England) decisively defeated the Blois faction. Charles of Blois was slain in the battle, and his widow Jeanne de Penthièvre was forced to surrender her claims shortly thereafter[5]. The Treaty of Guérande in April 1365 formalized the end of the war: John IV de Montfort was universally recognized as Duke of Brittany, securing the duchy for the Montfort family and conclusively ending the Breton War of Succession[5].
Though the first phase of the Breton civil war (1341–1343) did not produce a clear winner, it was a crucial episode in Brittany’s history and in the early Hundred Years’ War. The civil war devastated parts of Brittany and demonstrated the significant influence of external powers in regional disputes. The intermittent conflict showcased the tenacity of Jeanne of Flanders and the volatility of noble loyalties. In the end, the Montfort claim prevailed, but only after a prolonged struggle that outlasted both original claimants. Brittany’s ducal succession was thus settled in favor of the Montfort line, largely thanks to English intervention, while France’s preferred candidate fell in battle – an outcome that underlined the intertwined fate of the Breton succession crisis with the larger Anglo-French war of the 14th century.
