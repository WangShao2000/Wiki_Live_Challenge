# Central Africa Regiment
## History
Formation and Early Campaigns: The Central Africa Regiment was the regular military unit of the British Central Africa Protectorate (modern Malawi) in the late 19th century[1]. The protectorate’s administration began raising a local force in 1891, which by 1896 was formally organized as the Central Africa Rifles[2][3]. Captain (later Sir) William H. Manning of the British Army was instrumental in raising and training the regiment[1]. The force was composed of African askari (soldiers) led by British officers, with around 100 Sikh instructors and non-commissioned officers brought from India as support[4]. Recruitment initially favored groups such as the Atonga and Yao, while the militarily powerful Angoni (Ngoni) people were avoided as recruits in the early years[4].
In its first decade, the Central Africa Rifles fought to establish colonial control and suppress the slave trade. The unit took part in the so-called “Slavers’ War” (1888–1895) against Arab slave traders around Lake Nyasa[5][4]. It also engaged in several expeditions against the Angoni. After minor clashes in 1896–1897, a larger Angoni uprising in the north (the Angoniland Rebellion of 1898) prompted a major military response[6]. In January 1898, six companies of the Central Africa Rifles (accompanied by Sikh artillerymen and Maxim gun teams) marched to Fort Jameson in neighboring North-Eastern Rhodesia, where they decisively defeated the Angoni forces and quelled the rebellion by February 1898[7][8]. The campaign’s end marked the pacification of the last major resistance in the area, and afterwards the British began to recruit some Angoni into the regiment as well[9]. For these early campaigns, many of the regiment’s soldiers received the Central Africa Medal with clasp “Central Africa 1894–98” in recognition of their service[10].
Expansion and Overseas Service: In late 1898, the government decided to expand the protectorate’s military capacity. The original Central Africa Rifles was re-designated as the 1st Battalion, Central Africa Regiment (CAR) in December 1898[11]. A 2nd Battalion was then raised on 1 January 1899, with the specific aim of allowing service outside the protectorate[11]. This expansion was partly to enable response to conflicts elsewhere in Africa and to relieve other British units overseas[12]. The new 2nd Battalion was soon tested: it was sent to Mauritius for training and staging, and in early 1900 it deployed to British Somaliland to join the campaign against the forces of Mohammed Abdullah Hassan (the “Mad Mullah”)[13]. Meanwhile, companies from the 1st Battalion were dispatched to West Africa. In mid-1900, Central Africa Regiment contingents took part in the Ashanti War in the Gold Coast, where they were noted for their performance in the field[14]. Some CAR companies also served in The Gambia during this period, assisting local colonial forces[14]. In recognition of the regiment’s contributions, King Edward VII received a detachment of Central Africa Regiment soldiers in London and awarded campaign medals to them for the Ashanti and Gambia expeditions[14].
By 1900, with two battalions in service, the unit was officially unified under the title “Central Africa Regiment.” The 1st and 2nd Battalions continued to serve in separate theaters (the 1st primarily in Central and West Africa, and the 2nd in East Africa). Half of the 2nd Battalion’s strength remained engaged in the Somaliland campaign into 1901, while other companies from both battalions were rotated through various colonial expeditions in Africa[15][16]. The regiment’s operational role during these years was wide-ranging: its troops battled indigenous resistance in its home region and supported British military efforts across the continent.
Incorporation into the King’s African Rifles: As part of a broader reorganization of British colonial forces, the Central Africa Regiment was merged into the newly formed King’s African Rifles (KAR) on 1 January 1902[17]. Under this reorganization, the two battalions of the Central Africa Regiment became the 1st and 2nd (Nyasaland) Battalions of the King’s African Rifles[17]. The change was mainly administrative; the Nyasaland battalions continued to operate with the same personnel but as part of the larger KAR multi-battalion regiment that included units from East Africa and Uganda[3]. The former Central Africa Regiment units went on to serve with distinction as part of the KAR in the World Wars and other campaigns throughout the 20th century. The Central Africa Regiment’s legacy persisted long after its 1902 amalgamation: the 1st (Nyasaland) Battalion, KAR was eventually retained as the core of Malawi’s post-independence army. (The 2nd Nyasaland Battalion was disbanded in 1962, but the 1st Battalion was renamed the Malawi Rifles upon Nyasaland’s independence in 1964, carrying on the lineage of the old Central Africa Regiment)[18].
