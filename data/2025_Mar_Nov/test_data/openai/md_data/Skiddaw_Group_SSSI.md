## Geography and extent
The Skiddaw Group SSSI spans much of the Northern Fells region of the Lake District. It is centered around the Skiddaw massif and surrounding uplands, including areas such as Skiddaw Forest, Caldbeck Common, and the Uldale Fells. The designation extends across a broad area of open fells, roughly forming an irregular circle around the high ground north of Keswick. Prominent peaks within the SSSI include Skiddaw (931 m, the highest summit in the area) and Blencathra (868 m), along with other fells like High Pike and Great Calva. The site also encompasses remote upland valleys and tarns – for example, Bowscale Tarn and Scales Tarn are included within the SSSI boundary[1]. These mountain tarns are noted for their clear, oligotrophic waters and intact peat-moss surrounds. The Skiddaw Group SSSI serves as the headwaters for several Cumbrian rivers (such as the River Caldew), reflecting its extensive coverage of high-altitude catchment areas[6].
## Habitats and ecology
Habitat diversity is a key feature of the Skiddaw Group SSSI. The site contains large expanses of moorland heath (dominated by heather and other dwarf shrubs) and extensive blanket bog, with smaller areas of grassland on the lower slopes[1]. Scattered stands of juniper scrub occur on the fellsides – the Skiddaw area is one of the few parts of north-west England where upland juniper still persists naturally[1]. The blanket peat bogs are particularly significant for conservation: they act as a massive carbon store and support specialized flora. However, some peatlands in the SSSI were historically degraded by drainage and peat cutting, and are now the focus of restoration efforts to re-wet the bog and encourage Sphagnum moss recovery[2]. Overall, while the habitats are extensive, assessments have noted that in certain areas the typical upland plants and animals are present only in reduced numbers due to past land use (such as overgrazing)[2]. Recent management initiatives – for example, temporary fencing of small plots on Caldbeck Common – have been introduced to help heathland vegetation regenerate by reducing grazing pressure from sheep[1].
The Skiddaw Group SSSI supports a diverse community of montane breeding birds typical of the high fells[1]. Birds of the open uplands found here include species of conservation concern such as the golden plover and merlin, which breed on the high moors, as well as peregrine falcons that nest on crags. Large populations of more common upland birds (like wheatears, meadow pipits, and ravens) are also present. The importance of the area for birdlife is reflected in regular surveys of its breeding bird populations[1]. In addition to birds, the SSSI provides habitat for upland invertebrates – a number of rare or localized species of insects and other invertebrates have been recorded. For example, Natural England commissioned a detailed survey of the site’s invertebrate assemblage in 2017 as part of its monitoring program[2]. The combination of expansive heath, bog, and flush habitats supports dragonflies, upland beetles, and other specialist fauna. Larger mammals are scarce on these open fells, but small groups of red deer and feral goats roam parts of the area, and the peatlands are important for amphibians (such as frogs and newts) which breed in upland pools.
## Geological features
The “Skiddaw Group” SSSI takes its name from the Skiddaw Group of geological formations, and the site is of significant geological interest. The bedrock underlying the northern Lake District fells consists largely of the Skiddaw Group rocks – a sequence of early Ordovician mudstones and siltstones (often referred to as Skiddaw Slates). This area is effectively the type locality for the Skiddaw Group; the rock unit itself was named after Skiddaw mountain, and the SSSI encompasses classic exposures of these sedimentary strata[6]. In the higher fells, glacial and periglacial landforms are evident in the landscape – including frost-shattered scree slopes, mountain corries, and blockfields – which provide important records of Pleistocene climate and geomorphology[6].
Another major reason for the site’s designation is the presence of mineral veins and historical mining sites in the Skiddaw massif and Caldbeck Fells. The northern fells have a long history of mining for metals such as lead, copper, tungsten and arsenic, and several old mines within the SSSI are nationally significant for their mineralogy. Notably, Carrock Mine (on the slopes of Carrock Fell) is included in the SSSI for its exceptionally rich mineral assemblage – Natural England describes this site’s mineral deposits as “nationally important”[4]. Carrock Mine was worked for tungsten until the early 1980s and is also protected as a Scheduled Monument for its mining heritage[4]. Other disused mining localities, such as Drygill (Dry Gill), Roughton Gill, Red Gill, and Burdell Gill in the Caldbeck area, have yielded a remarkable diversity of rare minerals. Collectors in the 19th and 20th centuries discovered many unusual mineral specimens here (for example, green crystals of the arsenate mineral pharmacosiderite were famously found at Burdell Gill)[7]. In fact, some of these sites are regarded among the most important mineral localities in the UK for certain lead, copper and arsenic minerals[8].
Because of the scientific value of these geological features, measures have been taken to prevent their damage. By the late 20th century, unregulated mineral collecting had caused noticeable depletion of exposed mineral veins and mine dumps on the Caldbeck Fells. This high collecting pressure was recognized as a serious threat to the finite mineral resource of the area[5]. In response, the landowners (the Lake District National Park Authority and others) in cooperation with Natural England implemented a permit system to control mineral collecting in the SSSI. Specific “open” and “closed” zones were established around the most sensitive sites – for example, casual collecting is prohibited in highly sensitive spots such as Red Gill, and only allowed by permit in less sensitive zones[8]. Signs posted at access points warn that it is an offence to disturb or remove any of the SSSI’s protected rocks, minerals, flora or fauna without prior consent[8]. These conservation measures have been largely effective: a 2022 assessment by Natural England found that while some mineral vein exposures showed past damage, there was no recent evidence of illegal collecting activity at those sites.
## Conservation and management
Skiddaw Group SSSI is actively managed to maintain its special scientific interest. Natural England is the regulatory authority for the site, working in partnership with landowners and commoners. Much of the area (for instance, Caldbeck and Uldale Commons) is common land used for sheep grazing, so balancing traditional upland farming with habitat conservation is an ongoing challenge. The blanket bogs and heaths are sensitive to grazing pressure and burning; as noted above, experimental habitat restoration projects are in progress to improve their condition[1]. As of the mid-2020s, official condition assessments show that large portions of the Skiddaw Group SSSI remain in “unfavourable” condition, although many units are classified as “unfavourable – recovering” due to recent management interventions (such as re-wetting peat and reducing grazing).
In 2022, a significant portion of Skiddaw Forest (about 1,200 ha of the SSSI around Skiddaw itself) was purchased by the Cumbria Wildlife Trust to establish a new nature reserve[2]. This initiative – creating “England’s highest nature reserve” – aims to enhance the biodiversity of the core of the SSSI through re-establishing degraded habitats and reintroducing lost species. The Wildlife Trust has a 100-year vision to restore a richer mosaic of upland habitats on Skiddaw, including native woodland (temperate “Atlantic” rainforest on the lower slopes), healthy blanket bog, heather-rich heathland, flower-rich grassland, and pockets of montane scrub on the high fells[2]. The project will involve tree planting (with locally sourced upland tree stock), blocking old drainage channels to re-wet peat bogs, and potentially reintroducing fauna like the black grouse and water vole, which have disappeared from these fells in recent decades[2][9].
The Skiddaw Group SSSI is an integral part of the Lake District’s natural heritage. It was listed in the classic work A Nature Conservation Review (1977) as an outstanding upland site, and it forms one of the core protected areas underpinning the UNESCO World Heritage status of the Lake District’s cultural landscape. Ongoing conservation efforts aim to ensure that the diverse habitats and geological features of this upland landscape continue to be safeguarded for the future.
