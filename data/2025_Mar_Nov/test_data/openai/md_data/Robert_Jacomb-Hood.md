# Robert Jacomb-Hood
Robert Jacomb-Hood MICE (25 January 1822 – 10 May 1900) was a British civil engineer who served as the first Resident Engineer of the London, Brighton and South Coast Railway (LB&SCR) upon its formation in 1846[1]. During his tenure in the mid-19th century he oversaw significant expansion of the railway’s network, including the design and construction of the LB&SCR’s portion of London Victoria station, which opened in 1860[2]. Jacomb-Hood later continued to advise the company as a consulting engineer and joined its board of directors in 1883[3]. He was a Member of the Institution of Civil Engineers and was regarded for his contributions to Victorian railway development.
## Early life and career
Jacomb-Hood was born on 25 January 1822 in Kimbolton (near Riseley), Bedfordshire[4]. He began his engineering career in the early 1840s, working as an assistant to William Baker (who later became chief engineer of the London and North Western Railway) on a railway project in Cheshire[5]. His early experience under Baker’s mentorship prepared him for rapid advancement in the booming railway industry. In July 1846, at the age of 24, Jacomb-Hood was appointed Resident Engineer to take charge of the entire newly formed LB&SCR network[6]. This role effectively made him the chief engineer responsible for all the civil engineering works of the company’s lines.
## London, Brighton and South Coast Railway
As the LB&SCR’s first resident engineer, Jacomb-Hood oversaw an era of rapid expansion and infrastructure building for the railway in the 1840s and 1850s. Under his supervision, the company undertook the construction of numerous lines, branch routes, and stations to extend its reach across southeast England. His office planned and executed new railway links in South London and across Surrey and Sussex, keeping pace with the growing demand for rail transport[7]. Among the notable projects during his tenure was the extension of the railway into London’s West End. Jacomb-Hood designed the LB&SCR’s portion of Victoria Station in central London, which was completed ahead of schedule and opened on 1 October 1860[2]. This terminus, built in partnership with the London Chatham and Dover Railway, gave the LB&SCR a valuable London endpoint and showcased Jacomb-Hood’s capability in managing a major urban railway project.
Jacomb-Hood’s leadership contributed to the successful completion of other expansions. During the 1850s, the LB&SCR opened new routes along the Sussex coast and inland. By the early 1860s, lines such as the suburban loop through Peckham Rye and Denmark Hill, the extension from Leatherhead to Dorking, and the branch from Three Bridges through East Grinstead to Tunbridge Wells were either planned or underway, projects in which his engineering staff (including architects like C. H. Driver) played key roles[7]. Jacomb-Hood’s tenure coincided with the railway’s transition from its initial main lines to a broader network serving numerous towns and seaside resorts.
In late 1860, Jacomb-Hood stepped down from the day-to-day post of chief engineer of the LB&SCR (the position was then filled by Frederick Banister) after nearly 14 years at the helm. He remained highly respected within the company and in the wider engineering community. In May 1869, the LB&SCR appointed Jacomb-Hood as a Consulting Engineer to assist his successor Banister with ongoing works, reflecting the value of his expertise and experience[3]. He continued to provide guidance on engineering matters as the railway undertook further improvements and new construction in the 1870s.
## Later years and legacy
Beyond his consulting role, Jacomb-Hood stayed involved with the LB&SCR at the executive level. In 1883, he was appointed to the board of directors of the railway[3]. Serving as a director, he contributed to the company’s strategic decisions during the late Victorian period. His long association with the LB&SCR, from its inception through its heyday, gave him a unique perspective on railway management and engineering. Jacomb-Hood was a Member of the Institution of Civil Engineers (M.I.C.E.), reflecting his professional standing. Colleagues and later historians have noted his role in shaping the early success of the LB&SCR’s infrastructure. His work on London Victoria station, in particular, remained a prominent part of the station’s history as one of London’s major rail terminals.
Jacomb-Hood died on 10 May 1900 at his home “Bargrove” on Frant Road in Royal Tunbridge Wells, Kent[4]. He was 78 years old. At the time of his death, the railway world was transitioning into the 20th century, building upon foundations laid by Victorian engineers like him. The LB&SCR itself would later be absorbed into the Southern Railway in 1923, but many of the lines and structures developed under Jacomb-Hood’s oversight continued in operation, a testament to his sound engineering.
## Personal life
Robert Jacomb-Hood married Jane Stothard Littlewood (1827–1869) in the mid-19th century[8]. Jane was the daughter of George Littlewood, a London printer[9]. The couple made their home in London and had several children. Their eldest son, George Percy Jacomb-Hood, born in 1857, became a distinguished painter and illustrator[10]. Another son, John Wykeham Jacomb-Hood (born 1859), followed in his father’s footsteps as an engineer; he later served as Chief Engineer of the London & South Western Railway and was President of the Institution of Mechanical Engineers in 1911–12[11]. Robert Jacomb-Hood’s family connections extended his legacy into both the arts and engineering fields.
