# Assassination of George Lincoln Rockwell
George Lincoln Rockwell (March 9, 1918 – August 25, 1967) was an American far-right political figure and the founder of the American Nazi Party (renamed the National Socialist White People’s Party in 1967). On August 25, 1967, Rockwell was assassinated in Arlington, Virginia, when he was shot dead outside a laundromat by John Patler, a disgruntled former member of his own neo-Nazi organization[1][2]. Rockwell died at the scene from a gunshot wound to the chest[3]. Patler was later convicted of first-degree murder in Rockwell’s killing and sentenced to 20 years in prison[4]. The assassination attracted national attention and dealt a crippling blow to the American Nazi movement, which soon fell into obscurity without Rockwell’s leadership[5].
## Background
George Lincoln Rockwell was a U.S. Navy veteran of World War II and Korea who became notorious as the leader of the American Nazi Party. He established the party in 1959 and headquartered it in Arlington, Virginia, where his inflammatory racist and antisemitic activities drew widespread outrage in the 1960s[6]. Rockwell relished media attention and staged provocative rallies, speeches, and publicity stunts in pursuit of his white supremacist ideology. By the mid-1960s, he had earned a reputation as one of the country’s most prominent neo-Nazi figures. In January 1967, Rockwell sought to soften the Nazi image of his group by officially renaming the American Nazi Party to the National Socialist White People’s Party (NSWPP), part of a strategy to broaden its appeal beyond avowed Hitler admirers[7].
Within Rockwell’s organization, however, internal tensions were growing. One of Rockwell’s closest associates was John Patler (born John Patsalos in 1938), a former U.S. Marine of Greek-American background who had risen to a leadership role in the group[8]. Patler was a devoted follower who even altered his surname to emulate “Hitler,” and he had encouraged Rockwell to rebrand the party and deemphasize overt Nazi imagery[9][7]. Despite their former mentor-protégé relationship, Rockwell and Patler developed a bitter rift over the party’s direction. In April 1967, Rockwell expelled Patler from the NSWPP after a series of personal and ideological disagreements[10]. Rockwell reportedly began to worry about traitors in his ranks; according to his father, Rockwell “had always expected” a violent end and was “more afraid of his own men than people were of him”[11][12]. Just weeks after Patler’s expulsion, an unknown assailant fired shots at Rockwell outside the party’s Arlington headquarters (in an incident one witness blamed on Patler), foreshadowing the deadly attack to come[10].
## Assassination
On the late morning of August 25, 1967, Rockwell drove to a coin-operated laundromat (the Econ-o-Wash) in Arlington’s Dominion Hills Shopping Center, located just across the street from his party’s “Hatemonger Hill” headquarters[3][8]. He was dressed inconspicuously and had come to do his laundry. At approximately 11:45 a.m., Rockwell left the laundromat and returned to his car, reportedly to retrieve bleach from home for his wash. As the 49-year-old climbed into the driver’s seat of his blue-and-white 1958 Chevrolet, two gunshots rang out from the roof of the shopping center[13][14]. One bullet ripped through Rockwell’s windshield and struck him in the chest, causing fatal injuries, while a second bullet missed. Rockwell’s car lurched into another vehicle before coming to a stop, and he collapsed onto the parking lot pavement beside his laundry and newspaper. Bystanders initially thought a car had backfired, but they soon found Rockwell lying wounded; within minutes, he had bled to death from a heart wound[15].
Pandemonium followed the shooting. A witness in a nearby barbershop, hearing the shots, saw a man sprinting along the shopping center roof and then fleeing the scene on foot[16][17]. Several onlookers pursued the suspect and alerted the authorities. Arlington County police arrived quickly, and within about half an hour they apprehended a man fitting the shooter’s description roughly half a mile away. The suspect was caught running towards a bus stop near the intersection of North Harrison Street and Washington Boulevard[18]. He was identified as John Patler, Rockwell’s recently ousted follower. Patler’s pants were soaking wet up to the knees, suggesting he had waded through a creek behind the shopping center during his escape[19][20]. In a backyard along the escape route, officers discovered a discarded brown raincoat and a gray cap believed to belong to the gunman[21]. They also recovered the murder weapon – a vintage 7.63 mm German Mauser semi-automatic pistol – lying in the shallow water of Four Mile Run creek under a footbridge near the scene[21]. The 29-year-old Patler offered no resistance as he was taken into custody; notably, one of the arresting officers recognized him as a familiar face from Rockwell’s band of uniformed neo-Nazis[19].
## Investigation and Trial
John Patler was charged with the murder of George Lincoln Rockwell. Authorities soon pieced together evidence tying him to the assassination. Forensic analysis found footprints on the roof of the Dominion Hills Shopping Center that matched Patler’s shoes[22]. Ballistic tests indicated that the recovered Mauser pistol had been test-fired on rural property belonging to Patler’s father-in-law in Highland County, Virginia, shortly before the crime[22]. Several witnesses reported seeing a man of Patler’s appearance running in the area right after the shooting[23][17]. All of the evidence was circumstantial – no one saw Patler pull the trigger – but it formed a persuasive narrative when presented in court.
Patler’s trial for first-degree murder took place in December 1967 at the Arlington County Circuit Court. He pleaded not guilty. During the trial, Patler adamantly denied killing his former leader, and his defense attorneys attempted to cast doubt on his involvement[4][24]. The defense argued that Patler had no opportunity to commit the crime, asserting that he did not even know Rockwell had gone to the laundromat that morning[25]. Patler – who did not drive a car – claimed that on the morning of August 25 he was at home with his wife and young child, ran a few errands on foot, and then went for a walk after an argument with his wife[26]. His lawyers even reenacted Patler’s alleged walking route with a track athlete to argue he could not have covered the distance from his house to the murder scene in the short time frame. They also floated an alternative theory: that another disgruntled Nazi might have been the real shooter, upset with Rockwell’s recent “denazification” of the party’s image[4].
Prosecutors, led by Commonwealth’s Attorney William Hassan, presented a strong circumstantial case that Patler had both motive and means to kill Rockwell. They noted the deep personal grudge Patler harbored after being expelled from the group a few months prior. They emphasized the evidence of Patler’s presence at the scene (the footprints and eyewitness accounts) and the fact that Patler’s coat, cap, and gun were found discarded along the escape path[22][21]. The prosecution contended that Patler had staked out the laundromat roof awaiting Rockwell’s arrival and then ambushed him from above. They also highlighted Patler’s marksmanship training as a former Marine, implying he had the skills to carry out the shooting.
On December 15, 1967, after a relatively brief deliberation of just four hours, the jury returned a verdict of guilty in the first-degree murder of Rockwell[27]. Patler showed little emotion, but his wife wept loudly as the verdict was read[28]. The judge imposed the jury’s recommended sentence of 20 years’ imprisonment for Patler’s crime[4]. Prosecutors had originally sought the death penalty, but in the end Patler was spared execution. In 1969, Patler lost an appeal of his conviction in the Virginia Supreme Court, and the U.S. Supreme Court declined to review the case, leaving the verdict and sentence intact[29].
## Aftermath
Rockwell’s assassination had immediate repercussions for his followers and the disposition of his remains. As a U.S. military veteran, Rockwell was technically eligible for burial in a national cemetery, and his family initially planned to inter him in his home state of Maine[30]. However, Rockwell’s neo-Nazi followers in Arlington insisted on handling his funeral themselves and turned it into a macabre spectacle. On August 29, 1967 – four days after the murder – a group of uniformed American Nazi Party members attempted to conduct a Nazi-style funeral for Rockwell at Culpeper National Cemetery in Virginia[31]. They arrived with swastika flags, arm bands, and plans to perform fascist salutes and rituals at the gravesite. U.S. Army officials, however, refused to allow any Nazi regalia or ceremonies on the federal cemetery grounds[32]. This led to a tense standoff between dozens of Rockwell’s armed stormtroopers and military police guarding the cemetery[33]. The confrontation lasted for six hours, drawing media attention and onlookers, and was described by one reporter as a “classic comic fiasco” given its strange mix of solemnity and absurdity[33]. In the end, the authorities did not permit the burial to proceed. Rockwell’s followers were forced to abandon their Nazi funeral plan when cemetery after cemetery refused to accept the body for interment. His coffin was loaded back into a hearse and returned to an Arlington funeral home[33][34]. Shortly thereafter, Rockwell’s remains were cremated in secrecy. His ashes were turned over to his second-in-command, 33-year-old Matt Koehl, who had been named Rockwell’s successor as leader of the NSWPP[35]. (According to later accounts, Rockwell’s ashes were preserved by the neo-Nazi group and kept in an urn for decades, awaiting a hoped-for future entombment in a memorial site[34].)
With Rockwell dead, the leadership of his organization passed to Matthias “Matt” Koehl, a longtime party officer. Koehl vowed to continue Rockwell’s mission, but the American Nazi movement never recovered its momentum after the loss of its founder[5]. Almost immediately, the NSWPP began to splinter and decline. Within months of Rockwell’s demise, the group’s notorious Arlington “Nazi barracks” was shut down – the landlord evicted the uniformed tenants, and vandals soon defaced the property with painted swastikas and Stars of David in a final act of retribution[36][37]. Koehl moved the headquarters to a series of temporary locations in Arlington over the next few years, attempting to keep the operation alive[38]. Although the party continued to attract a fringe following and occasional publicity (including sporadic street marches and scuffles with protesters in the 1970s), it struggled without Rockwell’s showmanship and charisma[39]. In 1982, the dwindling organization — still under Koehl’s influence — formally renamed itself the “New Order” and relocated to the Midwestern United States, seeking a lower profile outside Arlington[40]. The New Order (as well as various small neo-Nazi splinter groups) traced its lineage to Rockwell’s movement, but none achieved the prominence or notoriety that Rockwell had in the 1960s. Today, historians note that Rockwell’s death marked the effective end of the first era of organized neo-Nazism in America, even though his ideological legacy of white supremacism lived on in other extremist groups in the ensuing decades[41][1].
John Patler, the assassin, served only a portion of his 20-year sentence behind bars. After exhausting his appeals, Patler was incarcerated at a Virginia state prison work camp. He ended up serving about eight years in prison. In August 1975, Patler was released on parole for good behavior[42]. However, he subsequently violated the terms of his parole and was re-arrested, resulting in additional time in custody through the late 1970s[42]. While in prison, Patler had reverted to using his birth name, John Patsalos, and in 1977 he legally changed his name back to that Greek original spelling[43]. After finally regaining his freedom in the early 1980s, Patsalos largely dropped out of public view. According to later reports, he attempted to start a small Spanish-language newspaper at one point, and he found work as a commercial artist and illustrator, a vocation he had pursued even during his neo-Nazi days[42]. Patsalos generally refused to speak to the press about Rockwell or the assassination; he remained openly bitter about his former mentor. As of the 2010s, the one-time killer was living a quiet life in New York City, while the man he murdered — George Lincoln Rockwell — endures as a symbolic figure in the annals of American extremism.
