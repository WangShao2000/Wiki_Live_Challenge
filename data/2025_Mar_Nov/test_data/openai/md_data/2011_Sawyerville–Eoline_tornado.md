# 2011 Sawyerville–Eoline tornado
## Background
On April 27, 2011, a massive tornado outbreak ravaged the Southeastern United States as part of the multi-day April 25–28, 2011 Super Outbreak, the largest tornado outbreak ever recorded. Atmospheric conditions produced an unprecedented 362 confirmed tornadoes over four days, causing approximately 321 deaths and around $12 billion in damage across several states[1][2]. Alabama was the hardest-hit state, with dozens of intense tornadoes and over 240 fatalities on April 27 alone. The Sawyerville–Eoline tornado was one of 62 tornadoes that struck Alabama that day, occurring during the outbreak’s second and most violent wave of afternoon supercell storms. These storms generated multiple long-track, strong to violent tornadoes in a short span, including several EF4–EF5 tornadoes in northern and central Alabama. The Sawyerville–Eoline tornado, while not the highest-rated of these, proved to be especially destructive in its own right.
## Tornado path and damage
The Sawyerville–Eoline tornado first touched down around 5:30 p.m. CDT on April 27, 2011, in far southwestern Greene County, Alabama[3]. It began as a relatively weak tornado (rated EF1) near the small community of Tishabee by the Tombigbee River, but it quickly intensified to EF2 strength as it moved northeast through rural areas north of Forkland[4]. In Greene County, the tornado damaged or destroyed at least four mobile homes, three farm outbuildings, and two churches, and it uprooted countless trees; two people sustained minor injuries in this area[4].
Continuing on its northeast track, the tornado crossed the Black Warrior River into neighboring Hale County, west of the town of Sawyerville. As it approached Sawyerville, it intensified to a strong EF3 with estimated winds of about 145 mph[3]. The tornado crossed Alabama State Route 14 just north of Sawyerville and tore through the rural community, inflicting severe damage. Numerous structures were obliterated or severely damaged in this area – including many mobile homes, several single-family houses, and at least one church – and thousands of trees were snapped or flattened[3]. The destruction in the Sawyerville vicinity was extensive, and this segment of the tornado’s path resulted in at least 40 injuries and 6 fatalities in Hale County[5]. Most of those who lost their lives were in or near mobile homes that were destroyed by the tornado’s intense winds.
The tornado maintained its strength as it pushed further northeast into the Talladega National Forest and crossed into Bibb County. It reached the Eoline community (south of Centreville) around 6:30 p.m., still at EF3 intensity with a damage path nearly one mile wide[6]. In Eoline, the twister caused devastating structural damage: roughly a dozen homes (including both frame houses and mobile homes) were completely leveled, and many other residences were damaged to varying degrees[6]. The Eoline Volunteer Fire Department building and another local business were destroyed by the direct hit[7]. One person was killed when their vehicle was thrown near the fire station, and about 10 other people in the Eoline area were injured[8]. After passing through Eoline, the tornado began to weaken somewhat. It crossed Alabama Highway 5 south of West Blocton, causing additional tree and structural damage with winds around 125 mph (low-end EF2 strength)[9]. Several homes and mobile homes near County Road 26 in Bibb County sustained major damage or were destroyed in this phase. The tornado continued northeastward and finally dissipated just after 6:50 p.m. near the community of Marvel, in the far northeastern corner of Bibb County[10]. In total, the tornado remained on the ground for approximately 85 minutes, cutting a continuous path of destruction roughly 72 miles (116 km) long across three counties[11].
## Impact and casualties
The Sawyerville–Eoline tornado caused severe damage and loss of life in multiple rural communities. In all, seven people were killed and fifty people were injured by the tornado’s rampage through Greene, Hale, and Bibb counties[12]. Six of the fatalities occurred in Hale County, Alabama, where the tornado’s most destructive EF3 damage happened in the Sawyerville area[5]. A seventh fatality took place in Bibb County (near Eoline) when the tornado struck that community[8]. Dozens of homes were destroyed along the tornado’s path, the majority of them being mobile homes which are particularly vulnerable to strong tornado winds. Several small churches in the rural countryside were also demolished, and one fire station (in Eoline) was leveled[6]. Thousands of trees were uprooted or snapped, especially within the Talladega National Forest, leaving swathes of forest flattened[3][6]. According to later damage assessments, the tornado caused an estimated $36 million in property damage along its track[13], a significant impact for the sparsely populated areas affected.
Local and state authorities issued timely warnings for the storm – tornado warnings were in effect for all the affected counties from 4:50 p.m. until 7:45 p.m. as the supercell tracked across the region[14]. This advance warning likely prevented even higher casualties, as residents in the tornado’s path had some time to seek shelter. Emergency responders from surrounding communities assisted in the aftermath, despite many roads being blocked by debris and fallen trees. The hardest-hit areas of Hale and Bibb counties saw extensive cleanup and recovery efforts in the weeks following the disaster, although the event received less national attention than the catastrophic tornado that struck Tuscaloosa and Birmingham on the same day (which caused dozens of deaths and far greater urban damage).
## Analysis and records
Meteorologically, the Sawyerville–Eoline tornado was notable for its combination of length, width, and intensity. The National Weather Service rated it EF3 on the Enhanced Fujita scale based on the damage observed, with an estimated peak wind speed of 145 mph[12]. Its path length of about 72 miles made it one of the longest-track tornadoes of the April 27 outbreak, rivaling or exceeding the track lengths of some stronger tornadoes that day[11]. In terms of duration and continuity, it remained on the ground longer than most tornadoes in Alabama’s history.
In a 2014 study of tornado energy, researchers found that the Sawyerville–Eoline tornado was exceptionally powerful in total energy release despite its EF3 rating. Using damage area data, they estimated the tornado’s total kinetic energy output at approximately 123 terajoules (TJ)[15]. For comparison, that is roughly twice the energy released by the atomic bomb dropped on Hiroshima in 1945 (around 67 TJ)[15]. This measurement made the Sawyerville–Eoline event the most energetic tornado of the entire 2011 Super Outbreak in that analysis, surpassing even the outbreak’s EF5 tornadoes in total energy. The tornado’s unusually large damage footprint (up to a mile wide) and very long path contributed to this high energy value[15]. This finding illustrates that a tornado’s destructive potential is not determined by wind speed alone; path length and width (the area affected) also play a significant role in the overall impact. The Sawyerville–Eoline tornado stands out as an example of a long-track tornado that, while “only” rated EF3, was capable of devastation on a scale comparable to the most violent tornadoes, due to its sustained intensity over a vast area.
