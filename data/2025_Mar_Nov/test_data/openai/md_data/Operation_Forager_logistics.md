# Operation Forager Logistics
Operation Forager was the codename for the Allied invasion of the Mariana Islands (Saipan, Tinian, and Guam) in mid-1944 during World War II. The campaign’s logistical planning and execution were critical to its success. As the largest amphibious operation of the Pacific War up to that time[1], the Mariana invasion required unprecedented coordination of transport, supply, and support over vast ocean distances. More than 600 ships, including dozens of attack transports, landing ships, and warships, and roughly 300,000 U.S. personnel were involved in Operation Forager’s assault forces and support elements[2][3]. Supplying this force meant overcoming extreme range – about 3,500 miles (5,600 km) from the main U.S. base at Pearl Harbor – and managing complex delivery of troops and materiel onto heavily defended islands.
## Planning and Preparations
Logistical preparations for Operation Forager began months in advance. The U.S. Navy’s Service Force, Pacific Fleet (ServPac), under Admiral Chester Nimitz’s command, established advanced bases in the central Pacific to stage fuel, munitions, and provisions for the invasion. By June 1944, fleet anchorages with repair ships, tenders, and supply depots were operating at Eniwetok, Kwajalein, and Majuro atolls in the Marshall Islands[4]. Commodore Worrall R. Carter’s Service Squadron Ten managed forward logistics from Eniwetok, moving stocks of ammunition, fuel, food, and spare parts to support the combat fleet. In accordance with Nimitz’s plan, all combat ships loaded extra supplies before departure – up to 120 days’ worth of provisions and stores – to minimize immediate re-supply needs during the operation[5].
Because the invasion force would operate far from permanent bases, the U.S. Navy organized an extensive underway replenishment system. A series of eleven ocean fueling areas, each a 75×25-mile rectangle code-named after major oil companies, were designated across the Pacific for refueling the task forces at sea[6]. Fleet oilers, escorted by destroyers, shuttled between these rendezvous areas and forward bases to keep Admiral Marc Mitscher’s fast carrier Task Force 58 and the amphibious fleets fueled during the campaign[7][6]. Similarly, supply ships and floating depots were pre-positioned so that replacement aircraft, ordnance, spare parts, and other necessities could be shuttled forward as the fighting moved west.
Staging in Hawaii: The assault troops and landing craft assembled in Hawaii in spring 1944. In late May, a serious accident at Pearl Harbor’s West Loch nearly disrupted the operation. On May 21, 1944, as LSTs (Landing Ship, Tank) were loading ammunition and fuel for the invasion, an explosion aboard LST-353 triggered a chain reaction known as the West Loch disaster. The blasts and fires destroyed six LSTs outright and killed at least 163 Navy and Marine personnel (with hundreds more injured)[8][9]. Despite this loss of valuable landing ships just three weeks before D-Day, Operation Forager’s timetable remained on track. Replacements and reallocation of craft allowed the immense armada to depart Pearl Harbor as scheduled in early June 1944.
## Saipan Invasion Logistics
The first target of Operation Forager was Saipan, with landings commencing on 15 June 1944. Two Marine divisions (2nd and 4th Marine Divisions) led the assault, followed by the Army’s 27th Infantry Division in reserve. The logistical demands for the Saipan landing were enormous: 59 attack transports and troop ships carried the assault forces, along with 64 LSTs that ferried tanks, vehicles, and supplies directly onto the beaches[10]. This invasion force had to be landed across Saipan’s western coast, which was fringed by a coral reef and defended by well-fortified Japanese positions.
Ship-to-Shore Delivery: To get men and materiel ashore efficiently, the Americans made full use of amphibious vehicles and pre-fabricated landing equipment. At dawn on 15 June, a wave of 34 LSTs approached to launch nearly 720 amphibious tractors (LVTs) loaded with assault troops[11]. These LVTs (amtracs) could crawl over the reef and deliver Marines onto the beaches, mitigating the reef obstacle that might have stranded conventional landing craft. In addition, many LSTs were outfitted with pontoon sections strapped to their sides or decks[12][13]. After unloading troops and vehicles, Seabee engineers quickly bolted these steel pontoon units together to form floating piers and causeways extending from the beach. This improvisation created makeshift docks over the coral reefs, allowing landing craft and small boats to offload heavy equipment and supplies directly, rather than grounding on the reef. The use of these portable piers and causeways greatly sped up the flow of cargo onto Saipan and was an innovation refined from earlier Pacific landings.
Beachhead Supply Operations: During the first days of fighting, all supplies – ammunition, rations, fuel, and water – had to be landed across the assault beaches under combat conditions. DUKW amphibious trucks and LVTs shuttled payloads from ships offshore to supply dumps on the beaches. Despite Japanese artillery fire and congestion onshore, U.S. Navy beach parties and Marine shore engineers established organized supply points for each landing sector. Priority was given to ammunition and water for the frontline troops. By D+1, temporary pontoon barges were also being used as lighters to ferry bulk cargo. As pockets of coastline were secured, the U.S. forces were able to bring in larger vessels – for example, LSTs could beach at high tide to unload bulldozers, trucks, and artillery. Within a week, as the battle moved inland, the logistical situation improved: the Americans captured Saipan’s small port facilities at Charan Kanoa and Tanapag Harbor. Seabees cleared wreckage and expanded these ports to accommodate supply ships. Notably, a 12-by-72-foot pontoon pier was installed at Tanapag Harbor, providing berths for Liberty ships to unload cargo[14]. This allowed much larger quantities of materiel to be delivered directly pier-side by the end of the Saipan operation, reducing reliance on over-the-beach unloading.
Another logistical challenge was the massive expenditure of ammunition by naval gunfire support and ground forces. The pre-invasion naval bombardment alone had fired thousands of heavy shells, and sustaining the offensive required continuous resupply of ordnance. Initially, plans called for emptying the assault transports’ own ammo stocks and then sending warships back to rear bases to rearm[15][16]. However, the intensity of combat made this rotation impractical. Instead, the Navy dispatched ammunition ships forward to Saipan. For example, the ammo ship USS Mazama was sent to the Saipan anchorage shortly after D-Day when certain naval shells (particularly 5-inch and 6-inch types) began running low[17]. At Saipan’s Garapan anchorage, the Mazama and other supply vessels offloaded ammunition to cruisers, battleships, and destroyers by using LCT barges and landing craft as tenders[18][19]. Heavy swells and enemy fire made this a delicate operation – on one occasion Mazama’s attempt to resupply a destroyer was aborted due to dangerous rolling – but it successfully kept the fire-support ships in action. Such adaptive logistics, bringing supply ships into the combat zone, were crucial to sustaining the momentum of the Saipan assault.
## Guam Invasion Logistics
The invasion of Guam (codenamed W-Day in Forager plans) was originally scheduled to occur shortly after Saipan, but it had to be postponed. The ferocity of Japanese resistance on Saipan and the unexpected duration of that battle meant the Guam assault was delayed for over a month, finally taking place on 21 July 1944. This delay had important logistical implications. Many of the same amphibious vessels used at Saipan were needed for Guam, and in the interim they had to be refueled, reloaded, and in some cases repaired. The U.S. also decided to land additional forces on Guam – notably the Army 77th Infantry Division – which were not present in the initial June planning. To accommodate this, a portion of the amphibious fleet (including large attack transports and cargo ships) was sent back to Pearl Harbor and Hawaii in early July to embark the 77th Division and additional supplies for Guam[20]. These ships then rushed to the Mariana area in time for the new invasion date. The coordination of this troop movement and re-supply across thousands of miles was a significant logistic feat in itself, effectively mounting a second large amphibious invasion on the heels of Saipan.
The assault on Guam unfolded on 21 July 1944, with two Marine divisions landing on the western side of the island (at beaches near Asan and Agat) and the Army’s 77th Division joining a few days later. Guam’s beaches, like Saipan’s, were backed by reefs and surf, so the initial ship-to-shore delivery again relied on LVTs, LCM landing craft, and DUKWs. Over the first several days, U.S. forces put ashore a tremendous volume of matériel to sustain the expanding beachheads. It is estimated that about 10,000 tons of supplies were landed per day in the first four days of the Guam operation[21]. This included everything from fuel and ammunition to construction equipment for engineers. All this had to be accomplished without established port facilities: all landing craft had to unload at the outer edge of the reef, transferring cargo to smaller amphibious vehicles or dragging it across causeways[22][23]. The Seabees had pre-assembled pontoon causeways which were quickly deployed on Guam’s beaches to facilitate unloading, just as on Saipan.
A primary objective for logistics on Guam was the capture of Apra Harbor, the island’s major port on the west coast. U.S. commanders understood that securing Apra Harbor quickly would greatly ease the supply situation. However, Apra Harbor’s entrance was initially blocked by the Japanese-held Orote Peninsula, which had to be cleared by ground combat. Until the peninsula fell, supply ships could not safely enter the harbor. During the first week of the battle, therefore, all supplies continued to be landed over the beaches. Despite difficult surf conditions and some Japanese artillery harassment, this over-the-shore supply method met the immediate needs of the invasion force. By 30 July, U.S. Marines and Army troops had captured Orote Peninsula and its airfield, opening Apra Harbor. The very next day (31 July 1944), the Navy drove several cargo ships and transports into Apra Harbor to begin unloading in a more conventional fashion[24][25]. Even before permanent piers were available, Seabees used pontoon barges and self-propelled lighters to offload ships in the harbor’s calm waters[25][26]. They rapidly erected pontoon piers at sites like the former fuel depot on Cabras Island, to prevent any supply bottleneck in the harbor[26]. Within about three weeks, multiple temporary piers were in place to accommodate Liberty ships and LSTs, sharply increasing the throughput of cargo. (This effort was not without setbacks – in October, a typhoon wrecked many of the pontoon piers, forcing them to be rebuilt quickly[27] – but by then the immediate operational supply needs had been met.)
Once Guam was secured (organized Japanese resistance ended on 10 August 1944), the island was developed into a massive logistics base. During and after the campaign, Guam earned the nickname “the nerve center of the Pacific drive,” as it hosted supply depots, repair facilities, airfields, and hospitals to support subsequent operations. From a logistics standpoint, capturing Guam and its port allowed the U.S. Navy to establish a forward hub in the Marianas. By late 1944, Guam was hosting fleet anchorages, extensive fuel tank farms, ammo dumps, and large airbases – all crucial for the final series of assaults (the Philippines, Iwo Jima, Okinawa) and the strategic bombing campaign against Japan.
## Tinian Invasion Logistics
The island of Tinian, just south of Saipan, was the third major objective of Operation Forager. The invasion of Tinian (24 July – 1 August 1944) presented a different logistical scenario, essentially a short-distance amphibious hop from Saipan. Because Saipan had been secured by mid-July, the U.S. was able to launch the Tinian assault directly from Saipan’s shores. This shore-to-shore operation saw two Marine divisions (the 2nd and 4th, redeployed from the Saipan battle) loaded into landing craft, LVTs, and DUKWs on Saipan, then ferried a few miles across the Saipan Channel to hit Tinian’s northern beaches[28]. The proximity greatly simplified logistics: supply lines ran back to Saipan’s established depots, and casualties or damaged equipment could be quickly sent to Saipan for treatment or repair.
Still, Tinian’s landing beaches were very limited in size (two small strips code-named “White Beach 1” and “White Beach 2”). To overcome this, the Marines again brought pontoon causeways and used LVTs for nearly all personnel and supply transfers in the first days. A continuous ferry shuttle ran between Saipan and Tinian, with LSTs and smaller landing craft moving back and forth carrying ammunition, water, and reinforcements as needed. Because the naval threat had been eliminated by this point (the Japanese fleet having been decisively beaten in the Philippine Sea), U.S. logisticians could operate with a high degree of flexibility around Tinian. Within 9 days, the relatively small island was secured, and Seabees began expanding Tinian’s port at Tinian Town and constructing airfields. Logistically, the Tinian operation benefited from the lessons of Saipan: there were ample stocks nearby, redundant supply methods, and close coordination between the services. Naval construction battalions on Tinian quickly built unloading ramps and rebuilt the damaged pier in Tinian Town to streamline the delivery of heavy construction equipment for the new airbases. By early August 1944, Tinian was being transformed into a major air logistics center – notably, it would soon host the biggest B-29 bomber base in the Pacific.
## Aftermath and Significance
Logistically, Operation Forager was a remarkable success. The ability to project and sustain such a large invasion force across the vast Pacific showcased the U.S. military’s growing logistical prowess in 1944. Despite the initial setback of the West Loch disaster and the complexities of coordinating multiple landings, the supply chain proved resilient and responsive. The forward-deployed Service Squadron Ten and other support units kept fuel and ammunition flowing to the fleet and troops, while the Seabees’ ingenuity in constructing ports, airfields, and infrastructure under combat conditions ensured that the captured islands became effective forward bases in short order. During Forager, no major Allied operation was halted due to lack of supply – a testament to thorough planning and improvisation.
The logistics of Operation Forager had lasting strategic payoff. Seventeen days after Saipan’s capture, the first B-29 bombers landed there; by November 1944, long-range B-29 bombing raids were being launched from the Marianas against Tokyo[29]. This was made possible by the rapid build-up of fuel, bombs, and aviation supplies delivered through the newly established supply bases on Saipan and Tinian. Furthermore, the Marianas became the staging and logistics hub for the climactic battles of 1945. Massive stockpiles on Guam supported the invasion of the Philippines and later Iwo Jima and Okinawa. In short, Operation Forager’s logistics not only enabled the immediate victory in the Marianas but also laid the groundwork for the final Allied offensives toward Japan. Military historians often cite Forager as a case study in successful large-scale amphibious logistics – demonstrating how careful preparation, adaptability, and seaborne supply capabilities can overcome the tyranny of distance in warfare[1][30].
