# 2009 NFC Wild Card playoff game (Green Bay–Arizona)
## Background
The 2009 NFC Wild Card playoff game between the Green Bay Packers and Arizona Cardinals was a first-round postseason matchup in the NFL’s 2009 season. It was played on January 10, 2010, at University of Phoenix Stadium in Glendale, Arizona, in front of 61,926 fans[1]. The Cardinals entered as the NFC West champions (10–6) and were the defending NFC champions from the previous season[2][3]. The Packers had an 11–5 record, finishing second in the NFC North behind the Minnesota Vikings, and earned a wild card berth[4]. Notably, the two teams had met in Arizona just one week earlier in the final game of the regular season, a 33–7 Green Bay win. In that Week 17 contest the Cardinals rested many starters and did not reveal much of their game plan, having already clinched their playoff position[5]. This set the stage for a much more competitive rematch in the playoffs.
Both teams featured high-powered offenses led by veteran and emerging star quarterbacks. Arizona’s 38-year-old Kurt Warner was in his final NFL season and had recently led the franchise to its first Super Bowl appearance; he was backed by Pro Bowl receivers Larry Fitzgerald and Anquan Boldin (though Boldin was sidelined for the playoff game with injuries)[6]. Green Bay was led by 26-year-old Aaron Rodgers, making his first career playoff start[7] after a strong 2009 season. The Packers offense had set a franchise record with 461 points scored during the season, and Rodgers threw for over 4,400 yards and 30 touchdowns[8]. Green Bay’s defense, coordinated by Dom Capers, was ranked #2 in the NFL and featured stars like Charles Woodson (the NFL Defensive Player of the Year)[9][10]. The matchup was highly anticipated as a duel between Warner, a two-time MVP and Super Bowl champion, and Rodgers, a rising star quarterback.
## Game summary
### First half
The game kicked off on a Sunday afternoon in Glendale under the roof at University of Phoenix Stadium. The early moments were dominated by Arizona’s defense, as Green Bay committed turnovers on each of its first two possessions. On the first play from scrimmage, Aaron Rodgers was intercepted by Dominique Rodgers-Cromartie on a deflected pass that had been broken up by linebacker Karlos Dansby[11]. Shortly after, Rodgers completed a short pass to Donald Driver, but Driver was stripped by Dansby and the fumble was recovered by Cardinals defensive lineman Alan Branch[11]. Arizona capitalized on the takeaways with quick scores. A one-yard touchdown run by Tim Hightower and a 15-yard touchdown pass from Kurt Warner to wide receiver Early Doucet put the Cardinals up 14–0 early[12][13]. Before the first quarter ended, Arizona added a 23-yard field goal by Neil Rackers, extending the lead to 17–0[14].
Green Bay’s offense finally settled in during the second quarter. The Packers got on the board with a 1-yard touchdown run by Aaron Rodgers on a quarterback sneak, set up by a long drive and an Arizona pass interference penalty at the goal line[15][16]. That cut the deficit to 17–7. The Cardinals answered with another efficient drive, as Warner found Doucet for a second touchdown, this time from 15 yards out, making the score 24–7[17]. Green Bay managed a Mason Crosby 20-yard field goal in the waning seconds of the first half after a 74-yard drive, bringing the halftime score to 24–10 in favor of Arizona[18][19].
### Second half
Arizona received the second-half kickoff and quickly struck again. Kurt Warner led an 80-yard drive and hit Larry Fitzgerald for a 33-yard touchdown pass, pushing the Cardinals’ advantage to 31–10 early in the third quarter[20]. This 21-point margin would be the largest lead of the game – and it would not last long. Facing a steep deficit, the Packers’ offense caught fire behind Rodgers’ passing. Green Bay drove 80 yards in 10 plays on the ensuing series, and Rodgers connected with Greg Jennings for a 6-yard touchdown, cutting the score to 31–17[21]. In a bold move, the Packers then surprised Arizona with an onside kick, recovering the ball to retain possession[21]. Rodgers took advantage by marching the team for another quick touchdown: an 11-yard pass to Jordy Nelson that brought Green Bay within 31–24 late in the third quarter[22].
The Cardinals responded before the quarter was out. On the next drive, running back Beanie Wells broke a 42-yard run to help Arizona down the field[23], and Warner found Fitzgerald again on a diving 11-yard touchdown catch to restore a two-score lead at 38–24 as the fourth quarter began[24]. The offensive onslaught continued unabated. Just three plays into the fourth quarter, Rodgers hit wide receiver James Jones for a 30-yard touchdown strike, once again pulling the Packers within seven points at 38–31[25]. After the Green Bay defense forced a punt — a rare stop in this shootout — the Packers offense struck yet again. Rodgers launched a 38-yard completion to tight end Jermichael Finley and a 28-yard pass to Donald Driver, advancing the ball to Arizona’s 1-yard line[25]. Fullback John Kuhn punched in a 1-yard touchdown run, and suddenly the game was tied 38–38 with about 11 minutes remaining[26].
Warner, maintaining his near-flawless play, coolly led the Cardinals on another scoring drive. He completed six consecutive passes at one point[27], including gains of 26 yards and 17 yards to Steve Breaston, the latter a touchdown that put Arizona back on top 45–38 with just under 5 minutes left in regulation[27][28]. Green Bay answered once more. Rodgers scrambled for 13 yards and hit Jennings for 22 yards to move into Cardinals territory[29]. With 1:52 to go, Rodgers found tight end Spencer Havner over the middle for an 11-yard touchdown, and Crosby’s extra point tied the game 45–45[30]. In a game full of offense, neither defense could hold the lead for long.
With the score knotted and time winding down, Arizona had a last chance in regulation. Warner drove the Cardinals into field goal range in the final minute, completing passes to Breaston and Fitzgerald to reach the Green Bay 16-yard line[31]. However, Arizona’s normally reliable kicker Neil Rackers missed a 34-yard field goal wide left with 14 seconds remaining, a shocking miscue that sent the game into overtime tied at 45–45[32][33].
### Overtime
Green Bay won the coin toss to start overtime, putting Rodgers and the offense on the field first[34][35]. On the very first play of the extra period, Rodgers nearly ended the game: he had wide receiver Greg Jennings streaking open behind the defense but narrowly overthrew what could have been a game-winning 80-yard touchdown pass[36][37]. The Packers soon faced a third-and-6 from their own 24-yard line. That’s when the Cardinals’ defense made the decisive play. Arizona cornerback Michael Adams came on a blitz and hit Rodgers, jarring the ball loose. The ball deflected off Rodgers’ foot and popped into the hands of linebacker Karlos Dansby, who returned the fumble 17 yards for a touchdown, sealing a 51–45 victory for Arizona in sudden-death overtime[38][39]. As Dansby crossed the goal line, the Cardinals erupted in celebration while a frustrated Rodgers slammed his helmet to the turf in disbelief[40][41]. It was an abrupt and stunning end to an extraordinary shootout.
The final play was not without controversy. Replays showed that Michael Adams grabbed Rodgers’ facemask as he sacked him, which ordinarily could have drawn a 15-yard penalty[42][43]. However, no penalty was called by the officiating crew, meaning the fumble return stood as the winning score. After the game, Rodgers refrained from blaming the officials, saying he was more concerned about his own mistakes than the missed facemask call[44][45]. (NFL rules at the time only penalized twisting or pulling the facemask, not incidental contact[46].)
## Aftermath and legacy
The 51–45 overtime win sent the Arizona Cardinals to the NFC Divisional Round, where they faced the New Orleans Saints. The high of the wild card triumph was short-lived – Arizona was beaten 45–14 by the Saints the following week, ending the Cardinals’ postseason run[47]. During that game, Kurt Warner suffered a heavy hit, and less than two weeks later the 38-year-old quarterback announced his retirement from the NFL on January 29, 2010[48][49]. Warner exited after one of the finest playoff performances of his career: against Green Bay he completed 29 of 33 passes for 379 yards, threw 5 touchdown passes with no interceptions, and posted a near-perfect 154.1 passer rating[50][51]. Remarkably, he had more touchdown passes (five) than incompletions (four) in the game[52].
For Green Bay, the heartbreaking loss marked the end of their 2009 season, but it also served as a springboard for future success. Rodgers finished his first playoff game with 423 passing yards and four touchdown throws (both franchise postseason records)[53], demonstrating that he could go toe-to-toe with a Hall of Fame quarterback. The Packers would regroup the next season and, with Rodgers now fully emerging as an elite player, eventually won Super Bowl XLV a year later, in February 2011[54].
The wild card shootout in Arizona immediately entered NFL lore as one of the most memorable playoff games ever. The teams combined for 96 points, making it the highest-scoring postseason game in NFL history[55]. They also scored an NFL-playoff record 13 total touchdowns in the game[56][10], and jointly amassed 1,024 yards of offense (the third-highest yardage total in playoff history up to that time)[57][58]. The two clubs achieved 62 first downs between them, another NFL postseason record at the time[58]. Each team punted only once in the entire game[59], underscoring the relentless pace of the offensive shootout. It was also the first NFL playoff game in six years to be decided by a defensive touchdown in overtime[60].
Players and coaches on both sides acknowledged the game’s extraordinary nature. “Whew, anybody else tired?” Kurt Warner quipped in the postgame press conference, reflecting on the furious back-and-forth scoring[61]. Cardinals head coach Ken Whisenhunt praised the contest as “one of the best games ever played in the playoffs”[3]. Even in defeat, Green Bay’s Mike McCarthy called it “clearly one of the toughest losses” of his career, while still expressing pride in how his team battled back multiple times[62]. The 2009 Packers-Cardinals wild card game has since been frequently cited among the greatest NFL playoff games. Years later, Warner himself described it as “the greatest offensive playoff game in the history of the NFL”[63], which is hard to dispute given the record-shattering numbers and dramatic ending. It remains a defining moment for both franchises – a thrilling shootout that showcased offensive excellence, resilience, and the unpredictability of playoff football.
