# The Story & the Engine
"The Story & the Engine" is the fifth episode of the fifteenth series of the British science-fiction television series Doctor Who. It was first broadcast on BBC One on 10 May 2025[1]. The episode was written by Nigerian-British playwright Inua Ellams and directed by Makalla McPherson[2]. It stars Ncuti Gatwa as the Fifteenth Doctor, with Varada Sethu as his companion, Belinda Chandra. Guest performers include Ariyon Bakare as a villainous figure called the Barber and Sule Rimi as the Doctor’s old friend Omo[3]. The episode also features a cameo by Jo Martin, who briefly reprises her role as the Fugitive Doctor (a past incarnation of the Doctor)[4]. Notably, The Story & the Engine made history behind the scenes: Ellams became the first Black man to write an episode of Doctor Who, and McPherson was only the second Black woman to direct the series (after Annetta Laufer in 2022)[2]. The on-screen cast is also almost entirely Black, as aside from one minor character’s appearance, no white actors feature in the story[5].
In the episode, set in 2019, the Doctor brings Belinda to Lagos, Nigeria – the series’ first adventure to take place in Africa[6]. Intending to visit Omo’s popular barbershop, the Doctor discovers it has been taken over by a mysterious new barber. This sinister Barber has trapped Omo and several customers inside the shop and forces them to tell stories while he cuts their hair. The barbershop is revealed to be more than it seems: it exists simultaneously on a normal Lagos street and in a strange realm of outer space, where it sits atop a giant spider’s web-like “engine” that feeds on the stories[7][8]. The Barber’s goal is to harness the narrative energy of his captives’ life stories to power this ship and reach the center of a cosmic story Nexus for a nefarious plan[9][10]. Over the course of the episode, the Doctor becomes ensnared in the Barber’s storytelling trap and must confront the engine’s demands. With help from Belinda and the Barber’s own assistant, Abena (Michelle Asante), the Doctor finds a way to overload the Story Engine and thwart the Barber’s scheme[11][12]. In the end, the Barber – who is revealed to be a mortal man long ago tasked with amplifying the myths of various trickster gods – stands down from his revenge plot and releases everyone[13][14]. The prisoners are freed back to Lagos, the gigantic spider-ship is destroyed, and the Barber repents after the Doctor persuades him to abandon his vengeful quest[15][16]. Omo and the Doctor reconcile, and the Doctor departs with Belinda, having saved the day and given the Barber a chance at redemption[17][18].
## Production
Development and writing: The Story & the Engine was conceived as a character-driven, “high concept” episode set largely in a single location. Inua Ellams, a poet and award-winning playwright known for exploring African themes, was brought on to write the script at the suggestion of star Ncuti Gatwa[19]. This marked Ellams’ debut in television writing. Showrunner Russell T Davies, who sought new voices for the series, asked Ellams to develop a story set in a barbershop, inspired by Ellams’s own acclaimed stage play Barber Shop Chronicles[20]. Ellams drew on West African folklore and ideas about the power of storytelling when crafting the episode’s central premise[21]. He has cited the West African trickster spider-god Anansi as a key inspiration – originally imagining Anansi as a “hive of all stories” that never gets credit – which evolved into the character of the Barber, a mortal man who carried the stories of the gods without recognition[21][22]. The script uses the barbershop setting as a metaphorical “safe space” for storytelling and community, a concept reflecting real-world barbershop culture in Black communities[23]. The episode’s dialogue and themes emphasize the importance of stories in shaping identity and legacy, aligning with the season’s broader motif of narratives coming to life.
Casting and characters: The principal guest role of the Barber was played by British actor Ariyon Bakare, whom the production approached directly for the part[24]. Bakare had previously appeared in the Doctor Who franchise (as the alien Leandro in 2015) and welcomed the chance to return in a more prominent role[25]. Sule Rimi was cast as Omo, the Doctor’s old friend who runs the Lagos barbershop, and the ensemble of trapped patrons included Stefan Adegbola (as Rashid), Jordan Adene (Tunde), and Michael Balogun (Obioma)[26][27]. Michelle Asante portrayed Abena (nicknamed “Abby” in the story), who is eventually revealed as the daughter of Anansi and the Barber’s accomplice[10][28]. In a surprise twist, actress Jo Martin appeared in a brief cameo as the Fugitive Doctor – a past incarnation of the Doctor – during a flashback sequence that shows the Doctor’s encounters with Anansi and other mythological figures[4]. This cameo, suggested by Davies, offers a “brief glimpse” of the Fugitive Doctor’s unseen adventures and ties into the episode’s celebration of the Doctor’s expansive history[4][29]. Notably, Inua Ellams himself makes a small cameo in the Lagos marketplace scene: he appears as a street trader who attempts to sell food to Belinda as she searches for the Doctor[30].
Filming and design: Although set in the bustling city of Lagos, the episode was filmed in Wales, with the production team recreating a Nigerian market street and barbershop within Bad Wolf Studios (Cardiff) and on backlot sets. Director Makalla McPherson described The Story & the Engine as one of the most visually ambitious episodes of the season, due to the effort of authentically depicting Lagos’s environment on screen[31]. The art department paid great attention to detail in constructing the set: actor Ariyon Bakare noted that the designers “recreated parts of Nigeria – the colours of the walls, the red earth, and the props – with such precision” to make the setting feel real[32]. To achieve the episode’s blend of the ordinary and the fantastical, McPherson used extensive pre-visualization and creative camera work to keep the single-location story visually dynamic[33][34]. Scenes inside the barbershop were interspersed with stylized visual effects: for example, as characters tell their stories, animated illustrations and holographic images appear on the shop’s windows to depict the narratives[35][36]. The production also created a CGI model of the giant Spider creature and its cosmic web. The sequence in which the Doctor opens the shop door to find a massive spider carrying the building through space was accomplished with a mix of practical set effects (wind and rigging to simulate the vacuum of space) and computer-generated imagery[37][7]. McPherson said audiences can expect the episode to be “bold, poetic, and deeply rooted in culture,” blending Lagos’s vibrant real-world atmosphere with supernatural elements of fantasy and mythology[38][39].
Related media: As a tie-in to the episode, the BBC released a short prose prequel written by Inua Ellams on the official Doctor Who website[40]. Titled “What I Did On My Holidays by Omo Esosa,” this short story (accompanied by artwork by Bunmi Agusto) was published on 8 May 2025 and recounts how a young Omo first met the Doctor during a forest fire in Nigeria decades earlier[40][41]. The prequel provides backstory for the friendship between Omo and the Doctor, which is referenced in the episode’s opening scene. A behind-the-scenes featurette for The Story & the Engine was also included in the Doctor Who: Unleashed making-of series, showcasing how the Lagos market set was constructed in Cardiff and how cultural consultants contributed to the episode’s Nigerian setting.
## Broadcast and reception
“The Story & the Engine” premiered in the UK on 10 May 2025, airing in the series’ regular Saturday evening slot on BBC One[1]. The debut broadcast had an overnight audience of 1.59 million viewers in the UK[1]. Official BARB 7-day figures later reported a consolidated viewership of 2.70 million (including catch-up and streaming), which placed the episode at around 35th in rank for the week’s UK TV broadcasts[42][43]. These ratings reflected a slight increase from the previous week’s episode, though they remained low in comparison to historical Doctor Who figures, partly due to strong competition from ITV’s reality programming that night[44]. Internationally, the episode was released via the Disney+ streaming platform (as part of the new co-production deal), making it available to global audiences on the same day as the UK broadcast[41].
Critical reception to The Story & the Engine was largely positive, with many reviewers praising its creativity, thematic depth, and representation, while some criticized its pacing and execution. Den of Geek described the episode as a “poetic, daring, and visually sumptuous” installment that succeeded in keeping Doctor Who “fresh and vital” by exploring new cultural territory[45][46]. The site’s review highlighted how the Lagos setting and Ellams’s perspective brought an “invigorating” energy to the series, and it welcomed the story’s celebration of the Doctor’s identity and community in a new context[47][48]. SciFi Vision similarly commended the episode’s unique concept – the idea of stories literally having power – calling it “a novel idea” and applauding how it incorporated African folklore and “universal themes of belonging and community” into the narrative[49]. The review noted that the visuals (such as the animated storytelling windows and the regrowing hair gimmick) effectively brought the imaginative premise to life on screen[49]. However, even favorable reviews had some reservations. Both SciFi Vision and other outlets observed that the character of Belinda, the Doctor’s companion, was underutilized in this episode – spending much of the runtime off-screen – which drew some disappointment[50][51]. Despite this, Critical Popcorn named The Story & the Engine one of the standout episodes of the 2025 season, attributing its strength to “the mind of a writer with a fresh perspective” and appreciating the “exciting drama” generated by its ensemble cast and folkloric storyline[52][53].
On the other hand, a few critics delivered more mixed or negative assessments. A second-opinion review on DoctorWhoTV.co.uk argued that the episode’s execution did not match its ambition. The reviewer described The Story & the Engine as “a slow-moving parade of monologues about ‘storytelling’, while forgetting to tell a compelling story of its own,” criticizing the script for heavy exposition and a lack of suspenseful momentum[54]. This critique found the narrative too static (owing to the single-location setting and abundance of dialogue) and felt the emotional stakes were undercut by insufficient character development – for instance, the Doctor’s deep bond with Omo is stated but not shown beyond exposition, weakening the impact of Omo’s betrayal[55]. The same review also took issue with the story’s resolution, arguing that the villains face de facto no consequences: after terrorizing and imprisoning people and “draining their personal stories for fuel,” the Barber and Abena are swiftly forgiven and given a fresh start, which the reviewer called “bizarre” in contrast to the harsher fate of the antagonist in the previous episode[56][57]. Despite these criticisms, even detractors acknowledged the episode’s imaginative ideas and thematic intentions. Fans and scholars have noted that The Story & the Engine is an unusually lore-rich chapter of the series, tying together threads of mythology and past Doctor Who “story gods” (from Anansi to the franchise’s own Land of Fiction lore) into a singular narrative[58][59]. While some found this dense and confusing at times, others have praised the episode for its ambition in expanding the Doctor Who universe’s treatment of myths and storytelling tropes.
In summary, The Story & the Engine was recognized for its cultural significance and inventive storytelling. It brought the Doctor to a new setting with authenticity and heart, and marked a milestone for representation both on-screen and behind the scenes. The episode’s reception underscored the balance of risks in Doctor Who’s format – some viewers were captivated by its lyrical, metaphor-heavy approach, while others yearned for a tighter plot. Radio Times, in its season roundup, listed The Story & the Engine as a “bold experiment” for the show, noting that its legacy may lie in how it inspired more diverse storytelling in subsequent series. The episode stands as a distinctive entry in the 15th series, remembered for the way it intertwined folklore with science fiction and for the conversations it sparked about whose stories get told and who gets credit in the tales that define us.
