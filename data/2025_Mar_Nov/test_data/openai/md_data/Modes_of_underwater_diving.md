# Modes of underwater diving
Underwater diving refers to human activity underwater with or without special breathing equipment. The modes of underwater diving are commonly classified by the method the diver uses to remain underwater and whether the diver is exposed to the ambient water pressure. The two broad categories are ambient pressure diving, where the diver’s body is at the same pressure as the surrounding water, and atmospheric pressure diving, where the diver is inside a pressure-resistant suit or vehicle and remains at normal surface pressure. Within ambient pressure diving, the main modes include breath-hold diving (freediving) without any breathing apparatus[1], SCUBA diving using self-contained breathing gas equipment[2], and surface-supplied diving where the diver is connected to a surface air supply via an umbilical hose[3]. In contrast, atmospheric pressure diving employs rigid armored suits that keep the diver at one atmosphere of internal pressure, enabling very deep dives without the physiological hazards of high ambient pressure[4]. Each mode of diving has distinct techniques, applications, advantages, and limitations, which are described below.
## Breath-hold diving (Freediving)
Freediving is underwater diving on a single breath of air, without using any breathing apparatus[1]. The diver inhales at the surface and then holds their breath for the duration of the dive, resurfacing before breathing again. Because there is no external air supply, freediving dives are typically short in duration and relatively shallow, but skilled freedivers can achieve remarkable depths and times. Modern competitive freedivers, using specialized training and techniques, have reached depths well over 100 meters on one breath. (In 2007, for example, a record “no-limits” dive reached 214 m in depth[5].) In practice, recreational and competitive freediving disciplines often involve dives of tens of meters in depth, and static breath-holds lasting several minutes.
Freediving is the oldest form of diving and has been practiced by humans for thousands of years for food gathering, pearl diving, and sponge harvesting. For instance, in Japan the traditional Ama divers (often women) have been freediving for over 2,000 years to collect shellfish and pearls, commonly diving to depths of 5–10 meters and sometimes up to 20 meters on a single breath[6]. Around the world, many cultures developed breath-hold diving techniques for fishing and harvesting resources long before modern diving technology. Today, freediving is not only a method for spearfishing and underwater recreation but also a competitive sport in its own right, with disciplines such as constant weight, free immersion, and dynamic apnea. Because freedivers are under ambient pressure and breathe no compressed gas, they avoid certain risks like decompression sickness, but they face others such as hypoxic blackout (loss of consciousness from lack of oxygen). Careful training, gradual depth progression, and the use of a buddy system are critical safety practices in freediving.
## Scuba diving
Scuba diving is a mode of underwater diving in which the diver uses a self-contained underwater breathing apparatus (scuba) to breathe from a compressed gas supply carried on their person[2]. In scuba, the diver typically wears one or more high-pressure cylinders filled with breathing gas (usually compressed air or nitrox) and breathes through a demand regulator mouthpiece. This apparatus allows the diver to swim freely without any tether to the surface, making scuba diving popular for recreation, scientific exploration, and underwater work in moderate depths. The word SCUBA is an acronym for “Self-Contained Underwater Breathing Apparatus”[2], reflecting that all necessary gas supply and breathing equipment travels with the diver.
A standard scuba setup is an open-circuit system: each breath the diver takes from the tank is exhaled into the water as bubbles. Open-circuit scuba is mechanically simple and very common, but it wastes the portion of oxygen that the diver does not metabolize[7]. An alternative is the closed-circuit or semi-closed rebreather, which retains the diver’s exhaled gas, removes the carbon dioxide, and replenishes oxygen, allowing the gas to be recycled for further breathing[7][8]. Rebreather systems greatly extend the underwater duration for a given gas supply and produce few or no bubbles (useful for stealth or wildlife observation), but they are complex and require careful monitoring of the gas mixture to avoid hypoxia or oxygen toxicity[8]. Scuba equipment can use a variety of breathing gases depending on the dive requirements: air (21% oxygen, 79% nitrogen) is standard for recreational dives, while enriched air nitrox (with higher oxygen fraction) is used to extend no-decompression limits at moderate depths. For deep or technical dives beyond about 40 meters, mixtures like trimix (oxygen, nitrogen, and helium) or heliox (helium and oxygen) are used to reduce nitrogen narcosis and manage oxygen exposure. These mixed-gas techniques fall under technical diving and require special training. Overall, scuba diving enables a high degree of mobility and autonomy underwater, but dive depth and duration are limited by the amount of gas the diver can carry and by physiological constraints such as decompression requirements. Scuba divers must ascent at a controlled rate and often perform safety stops (or staged decompression stops on deeper dives) to let dissolved inert gas escape safely and avoid decompression sickness.
## Surface-supplied diving
Surface-supplied diving is a mode where the diver’s breathing gas is provided from the surface, through a hose (or bundle of hoses) known as an umbilical. Unlike scuba divers who carry their own cylinders, a surface-supplied diver is tethered to a support station (such as a dive boat or shore facility) that pumps air or mixed gas down to the diver continuously[3]. This arrangement typically involves the diver wearing a helmet or full-face mask connected to the umbilical, and a communications line to talk to the surface team. Surface-supplied diving is the standard method for most commercial, military, and other professional diving work, especially for long-duration tasks or deeper dives. It originated with the classic “hard hat” divers of the 19th century and has evolved with modern helmets and deck decompression chambers.
Surface-supplied systems have several advantages over scuba for industrial use. First, the gas supply is essentially unlimited – the diver can remain underwater for extended periods without worrying about running out of air[9]. This makes it practical to undertake lengthy underwater construction projects, inspections, welding, salvage operations, and other tasks that would be impossible on a single tank of air[9]. Second, the tether provides a lifeline and communication link that enhances safety. The surface support team can monitor the diver’s status and speak to them, and they can assist or immediately pull up the diver in an emergency. Constant communication and topside monitoring significantly reduce the risk of the diver becoming lost or incapacitated without help[10]. Because the diver’s return to the surface can be controlled by the surface crew (e.g. via a winch on a stage or the umbilical), there is an added layer of safety and coordination. Surface-supplied divers also typically carry a backup emergency gas supply (a “bailout bottle”) on their harness in case the main hose supply is interrupted.
This mode of diving does come with trade-offs. The diver’s range of movement is limited by the length and management of the umbilical hose, which can be cumbersome and requires diligence to avoid entanglement[10]. The equipment (helmets, umbilicals, compressors or gas cylinders, etc.) is heavy and expensive, and thus surface-supplied diving is generally not used for casual or recreational purposes. It is primarily employed in professional contexts where the infrastructure and support personnel are available. Surface-supplied divers must follow careful procedures for decompression when surfacing from deeper or long-duration dives, often using on-site decompression chambers to safely eliminate inert gases from their body after a dive. Standard surface-supplied “air diving” is usually limited to relatively shallow depths (often up to about 50–60 m) due to the increasing risk of nitrogen narcosis and oxygen toxicity; for greater depths, surface-supplied systems can be used with helium-based mixed gases (surface-supplied mixed-gas diving) to extend depth range.
Saturation diving. In commercial projects that require very deep dives or many hours of work at depth, surface-supplied diving is often combined with saturation diving techniques. Saturation diving is a special mode in which divers live under high pressure in a sealed chamber for days or weeks, so that their bodies become fully saturated with inert gas at the working depth[11]. Once saturation is achieved, the time required for decompression does not increase with additional time spent underwater – the divers will need the same lengthy decompression regardless of whether they spend one day or several days working at depth[11]. The major benefit is that the divers can perform long shifts of work each day (transported to and from the underwater work site via a pressurized diving bell) and then only undergo a single, carefully managed decompression procedure at the end of their tour (instead of decompressing after every dive)[11]. Saturation divers live in a pressurized habitat (either an underwater habitat or more commonly a deck-mounted chamber on a ship) at the pressure equivalent of the work depth, and are supplied with breathing gas and essentials through that habitat. They typically breathe a heliox mixture (helium–oxygen) when at great depth, since using air would cause severe narcosis and other issues – depths below about 50–60 m generally require replacing nitrogen with helium. For example, saturation divers working around 200 m depth breathe heliox from the surface supply; at such extreme pressures a small percentage of nitrogen might be added to heliox to mitigate the effects of High-Pressure Nervous Syndrome (HPNS), a trembling condition caused by high-pressure helium[12]. Saturation diving is logistically complex and very costly, but it enables humans to operate at depths of several hundred meters. The deepest experimental saturation dive conducted reached a pressure equivalent to 701 m of seawater (a 1992 Comex experiment) – an extreme record. In practice, commercial saturation dives are commonly done in the range of 100–300 m for offshore oil, gas, and salvage work. This mode carries significant risks, and operations are conducted with a large support crew and strict procedures to ensure the health and safety of the divers.
## Atmospheric pressure diving
Beyond the limits of ambient pressure techniques, humans can also dive using systems that keep the diver at one atmosphere of internal pressure regardless of external depth. In this mode, often called atmospheric diving, the person is enclosed in a stiff, pressure-resistant suit or vehicle that prevents the ambient water pressure from affecting their body. The most prominent example is the Atmospheric Diving Suit (ADS) – a specialized armored suit that a diver wears to achieve great depths. An ADS is essentially a one-person articulated submersible shaped like a suit of armor, with movable joints that maintain a sealed internal pressure of 1 atm[4]. The suit contains an oxygen supply and CO₂ scrubbers to sustain the occupant, and often battery-powered thrusters or it may be connected to the surface by an umbilical for power and communication. Because the diver inside remains at normal pressure, they do not need to decompress when the ADS is brought back to the surface, and they are not subject to depth-related problems like nitrogen narcosis or decompression sickness[13]. This allows atmospheric diving suits to be used for very deep dives far beyond the depths reachable by unprotected divers. Modern ADS designs (such as the JIM suit, Newtsuit, or the Exosuit) have operational depths on the order of 600–700 m (around 2,000+ feet)[13], and they enable dive durations of many hours since life support can be maintained without the limitations of a human body under pressure.
Atmospheric diving suits are employed for specific tasks like deep ocean oil rig maintenance, submarine rescue, scientific sampling, or recovery operations at extreme depths. They combine some of the access and dexterity of a human diver (with manipulators that can use tools) with the pressure immunity of a submersible. However, ADS units are highly specialized and expensive pieces of equipment, and they are bulkier and less maneuverable compared to a free-swimming diver. The suit’s joints and life-support systems also impose practical limits on mobility and endurance. For even larger-scale or longer-duration deep missions, crewed submersibles (mini-submarines) or remotely operated vehicles are used rather than atmospheric dive suits. Those craft, like an ADS, maintain an internal one-atmosphere environment, but they are usually not considered “modes of diving” in the same sense, since the occupants do not directly contact the water. In summary, atmospheric pressure diving via suits allows humans to extend their underwater reach to depths otherwise inaccessible, by eliminating the physiological stresses of high-pressure exposure[13]. This mode complements the ambient-pressure diving modes, each being suitable for different depth ranges and purposes.
