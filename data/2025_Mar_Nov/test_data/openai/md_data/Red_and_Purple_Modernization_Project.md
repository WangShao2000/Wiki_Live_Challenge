# Red and Purple Modernization Project
The Red and Purple Modernization Project (RPM) is a multi-phase infrastructure improvement program by the Chicago Transit Authority (CTA) to rebuild and modernize the nearly century-old North Side Main Line of Chicago’s “L” rail system. The project focuses on the Red Line (Belmont station to Howard station) and the parallel Purple Line (Belmont to Linden in Wilmette), a corridor spanning about 9.6 miles[1]. The RPM program aims to replace aging tracks, structures, and stations, add passenger capacity, and improve service reliability and accessibility along this busy transit corridor[1]. Phase One of RPM, carried out from 2019 to 2025, had an estimated cost of $2.1 billion[2] and is one of the largest capital projects in CTA history. It included construction of a new bypass to eliminate a rail bottleneck, reconstruction of four Red Line stations, and installation of modern signal systems, among other upgrades[3]. Future phases of RPM are in planning and will continue the systematic overhaul of the remaining sections of the Red and Purple Lines on Chicago’s North Side.
## Background
The North Side Red and Purple lines were originally built in the early 1900s and, by the 21st century, much of the infrastructure and stations were long past their design lifespan[4][5]. Aging tracks and structures required frequent maintenance and caused slow zones, while many stations lacked accessibility features like elevators. The Red Line is the busiest route in the CTA rail network (providing nearly 40 million rides in 2024) and is often crowded at peak times[6]. However, the capacity to run additional trains on the North Side was constrained by an old track junction north of Belmont station where Brown Line trains intersected the Red/Purple tracks at grade, forcing trains to wait and creating a bottleneck[7]. By the 2010s, CTA planners determined that a major modernization was needed to increase train frequencies, relieve overcrowding, and bring the system into a state of good repair for decades to come[8][9].
In 2014, the transit agency proposed the RPM project as part of its Red Ahead program of Red Line improvements. To manage the scale and secure funding, the project was divided into phases. Phase One was developed as a core capacity project eligible for federal grants, focusing on the most urgently needed improvements. Environmental assessments and community input for Phase One were conducted in 2015, particularly for the new “Belmont Flyover” bypass plan which provoked some local controversy due to required property demolitions[10]. The City of Chicago and State of Illinois created a special Transit Tax Increment Financing (TIF) district along the corridor to help provide local funding for the project, capturing rising property tax revenues to reinvest in the rail modernization[11][12]. By early 2017, the U.S. Department of Transportation approved a federal grant package for Phase One, including a $956.6 million Core Capacity grant and additional funds through the Congestion Mitigation and Air Quality program, totaling about $1.07 billion in federal support[13]. This funding commitment – the largest transit infrastructure grant in CTA’s history at the time – enabled the RPM Phase One to move forward at a total cost of approximately $2.067 billion (about $2.1 billion) with the remaining funding coming from the local TIF and other sources[14][12].
## Phase One (2019–2025)
Phase One of the Red and Purple Modernization Project broke ground in late 2019 after preliminary site work and utility relocations earlier that year[15]. The CTA awarded a design–build contract to the Walsh-Fluor joint venture in 2018 to execute the major construction work[16][12]. The Phase One scope comprised three primary components[17]:
Red-Purple Bypass (Belmont Flyover): A new grade-separated bypass was built immediately north of Belmont station to carry northbound Brown Line trains up and over the Red and Purple line tracks. This eliminated the previous at-grade crossing at Clark Junction, a 1907-era junction that forced trains to cross paths and often caused delays[7]. Construction of the steel flyover structure began in October 2019, requiring the demolition of 21 older buildings on 16 parcels in the Lakeview neighborhood to clear the right-of-way[10]. The bypass opened for service in November 2021, removing the junction bottleneck. With this improvement, the CTA gained the ability to run up to eight additional Red Line trains per hour during rush periods, representing roughly a 30% increase in peak capacity through the corridor[9]. By eliminating train conflicts, the new flyover also allows faster train speeds (the curve north of Belmont was realigned) and has reduced delays and overcrowding on Red and Purple Line trains[9][18]. The flyover project cost was about $570 million of the overall Phase One budget[19].
Lawrence to Bryn Mawr Station Reconstruction: Four consecutive Red Line stations – Lawrence, Argyle, Berwyn, and Bryn Mawr (all in the Uptown and Edgewater districts) – were completely rebuilt. These stations dated back to 1914 and had narrow platforms and no elevators. Starting in 2020–2021, CTA closed each of the old stations and erected temporary stations at Argyle and Bryn Mawr to serve riders during construction[20][21]. The project involved demolishing the original track embankment and replacing it with a modern elevated viaduct and new bridges across the streets. Approximately 1.3 miles of track structure between Lawrence and Bryn Mawr were replaced with new steel and concrete structures[22]. The new stations were built with wider platforms, elevators and escalators, larger canopies, improved lighting, and other amenities, making them fully accessible and able to accommodate more passengers[23]. All four rebuilt stations opened together on July 20, 2025[24] – marking the first simultaneous opening of that many CTA stations since the launch of the Orange Line in 1993. The new elevated structure in this segment was designed to eliminate support columns in the middle of roadways below, improving sightlines and safety at street crossings[22]. By raising the track height to modern clearances and installing sound-dampening features, the reconstruction also reduced noise at street level[25]. This segment’s overhaul has enabled continuous CTA service improvements: as of 2025, all Red Line stations from Belmont through 95th Street are step-free accessible, a significant milestone toward system-wide accessibility.
Track, Signal, and Power Upgrades: In addition to the major civil works, Phase One included upgrades to tracks, signals, and power infrastructure to improve reliability and allow increased train frequency. A new automated signal system was installed on 23 track-miles between Howard and Belmont, replacing an antiquated 60-year-old signaling system[26][22]. The modern signals function akin to traffic lights for trains, enabling trains to run closer together safely and thereby boosting line capacity. Along with signals, the project upgraded traction power systems (including substations) to handle the demands of more frequent service[3]. The federal funding agreement also provided for the purchase of 32 new railcars to expand the CTA fleet for increased service[3]. During construction, the CTA maintained train service by using strategic staging: for example, a temporary bypass track was built parallel to the existing structure between Belmont and Newport Avenue so that Red and Purple trains could keep running while old tracks were demolished and replaced in sections[27][28]. Major construction for Phase One was substantially completed by the end of 2024, and final testing and ancillary work (punch-list items) continued into 2025[29][30]. The completion of Phase One delivered smoother rides and more reliable service for passengers, with the CTA reporting a significant reduction in slow zones and delays on the North Side as a result of the new infrastructure[25].
## Future phases
RPM Phase One addressed the most critical bottleneck and the replacement of four stations, but it covered only a portion of the overall 9.6-mile corridor. The CTA is planning additional phases to modernize the remaining sections of the North Side Red and Purple lines. In 2023, the agency launched the RPM Next Phases Planning Study to evaluate needs and options for three key segments: (1) Addison to Sheridan (the stretch just north of Belmont, including Sheridan station, which was not rebuilt in Phase One), (2) Thorndale to Howard (the upper end of the Red Line in Chicago, including several stations in Rogers Park), and (3) the entire Purple Line Evanston Branch from Howard to Linden (serving Evanston and Wilmette)[31][32]. These sections also contain century-old elevated structures and stations that require renewal, as well as some locations that still lack ADA accessibility. The planning study – expected to conclude in 2025 – will identify project alternatives and phasing for these future RPM phases, as well as initiate the environmental review and public engagement process[33][34].
Once fully realized, the multi-phase RPM program will reconstruct all stations and track between Belmont and Linden, effectively creating a modern transit corridor on the North Side. The CTA anticipates that the full project will allow further increases in train service to meet growing ridership demand, in addition to improving travel times and reliability for commuters[35][36]. However, implementation of Phase Two and beyond will depend on securing funding. The CTA will likely seek additional federal grants and local financing to cover the considerable cost of these expansions, similar to the public funding strategy used in Phase One. Meanwhile, the successful completion of RPM Phase One in 2025 has already delivered tangible benefits to riders and set the stage for the next century of rapid transit in Chicago’s northern communities.
