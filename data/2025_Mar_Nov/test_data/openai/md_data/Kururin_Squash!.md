# Kururin Squash!
Japanese cover art of Kururin Squash!
Kururin Squash! (Japanese: くるりんスカッシュ！, Kururin Sukasshu!) is a 2004 puzzle video game developed by Eighting and published by Nintendo for the GameCube. It was released exclusively in Japan on October 14, 2004[1]. The game is the third and final installment in the Kururin series, as well as the first entry in the series on a home console[2][3]. In Kururin Squash!, players control a constantly spinning helicopter-like craft (called the Helirin) and guide it through winding maze-like stages while avoiding walls and obstacles[4]. The gameplay builds on the concept introduced in the earlier Kururin titles – the Helirin rotates continuously, making navigation challenging, and the player must reach each goal without taking too much damage (three collisions will shatter the craft and end the level)[4][5]. Kururin Squash! introduced new features to the series, including 3D graphics, power-ups that transform the Helirin with special abilities, and multiplayer modes, expanding the formula of its Game Boy Advance predecessors[6][7]. Although the title never saw an official release outside of Japan, it received generally positive reviews from critics, who praised its inventive puzzle mechanics and colorful presentation[6][8].
## Gameplay
Kururin Squash! retains the core gameplay of guiding a spinning propeller craft through narrow courses, while adding several enhancements and new modes. The player pilots the Helirin – a small helicopter-like vehicle with a long rotating blade – and must maneuver it through each stage’s twisting corridors without hitting the walls or obstacles[9]. The constantly spinning blade means the player has to time and position movements carefully. Touching a wall or enemy causes the Helirin to take damage, and after three hits the vehicle breaks (resulting in a loss and requiring the stage to be restarted)[5].
To add variety, Kururin Squash! features stages with special Helirin transformations and abilities not seen in previous games. For example, certain levels take place underwater, equipping the Helirin as a submarine; in other stages the Helirin can attach a boxing glove to punch barriers and enemies, or use a flamethrower to attack obstacles[7]. A tornado ability lets the craft spin out a destructive twister attack[7]. These new powers not only change how players approach the maze navigation but also enable the inclusion of boss battles at the end of some worlds – the first time bosses have appeared in the Kururin series[7][10]. Boss encounters typically require use of the special abilities (for instance, using the punch or fire attacks) to defeat larger enemy characters.
The game introduces a currency and upgrade system, a new feature for the series. Gold coins are scattered throughout stages (and dropped by defeated enemies), encouraging players to explore and take risks to collect them[11]. If the player finishes a stage without taking any damage and collects all coins, a “Perfect” clear is achieved[12]. Coins can be spent in an in-game shop accessible from the world map[13]. The shop offers various bonus content and upgrades: for example, players can unlock additional Helirin models (some with novelty designs like a chocolate-bar-shaped Helirin or one with Nintendo logo blades)[14]. Other purchasable items include cosmetic changes to the interface (custom HUD styles), “Teacher Hare” replay videos that demonstrate perfect solutions to stages, world map illustrations, and even a extra mini-game that can be played by connecting a Game Boy Advance to the GameCube[13][14]. These extras are optional, but give completionists more to strive for and extend the game’s replay value[15].
Kururin Squash! offers multiple game modes. The primary mode is a stage-based single-player Adventure (or Puzzle Mode), in which the player progresses through a series of worlds (themed areas) rescuing Kururin’s siblings and advancing the simple story (see Plot below)[16]. Each world consists of several maze levels and usually culminates in a boss battle. There is also a Time Attack (or Race) mode, where players can replay levels to compete for the fastest completion times[17]. In Time Attack, the emphasis is on speedrunning through the courses, and the game records best times for each stage.
Additionally, Kururin Squash! includes local multiplayer modes for up to four players, taking advantage of the GameCube’s split-screen capabilities[7]. In Multiplayer Race mode, two to four players race simultaneously through a course, each controlling a Helirin. The game provides handicaps (for example, the leading player’s Helirin may become larger and thus harder to maneuver) to keep races competitive[18]. The second multiplayer option is a Battle mode, in which players compete in an arena-like stage to collect the most coins under a time limit[7]. In battle mode, power-ups can be used to attack or impede rivals – for instance, players can use the Helirin’s boxing glove or other abilities against each other, introducing a combative element in contrast to the purely time-based races[19]. The multiplayer modes were often compared to party games like Super Monkey Ball, as they provide a fast-paced, accessible competition suitable for group play[19]. While not as elaborately developed as dedicated party games, the multiplayer in Kururin Squash! was noted to be an enjoyable bonus, allowing both casual and experienced players to join in easily[19].
## Plot
The Kururin series is light on narrative, but Kururin Squash! provides a simple, whimsical story to give context to its stages. The protagonist is Kururin, a young bird-like character who pilots the Helirin. At the start of the game, Kururin and his large family (his siblings and their friends) embark on a happy family trip across the fictional world of Kururin[20]. However, during their travels, Kururin’s numerous brothers and sisters mysteriously go missing one by one in various countries that they visit. Kururin’s mission is to find and rescue all of his missing siblings and bring his family back together[20].
The game is divided into four main regions or countries, each with its own theme (such as grassy plains, ocean, sky, etc.), corresponding to the places where Kururin’s siblings have gone missing during the trip[20]. In Adventure mode, brief cutscenes between worlds depict Kururin’s journey and his encounters in these lands. The storytelling is presented in a cheerful, child-friendly manner – for example, characters speak in animal-like sounds and the cutscenes resemble puppet shows in style[21]. As he completes each world, Kururin manages to find one of his siblings (often after defeating a boss or completing a particularly challenging maze), who then returns home safely. By the end of the game, Kururin rescues all of his siblings across the four countries, reuniting the family. The plot is kept deliberately simple and cute, serving mostly as a backdrop for the puzzle gameplay rather than a deep narrative. This light story approach is consistent with the previous titles in the Kururin series (which also involved Kururin rescuing family members). Despite its simplicity, the inclusion of a story with characters and a goal provides a charming context for younger players, and motivates progression through the puzzle stages[16].
## Development and Release
Kururin Squash! was developed by the Japanese studio Eighting (also known as 8ing) and published by Nintendo. Eighting had developed the two prior games in the Kururin series on the Game Boy Advance, and Kururin Squash! continued this partnership on the GameCube platform[2]. The game was released in Japan on October 14, 2004[1]. As a GameCube title released late in 2004, it came out during the console’s final years on the market. Notably, Kururin Squash! was not released outside of Japan – neither North America nor Europe received an official localization or distribution of the game. This made Kururin Squash! one of the few Nintendo-published GameCube games to remain a Japan-exclusive.
The lack of international release was not entirely unexpected, as the Kururin series had a limited presence outside Japan. The original Kuru Kuru Kururin (2001) was released in Japan and also in Europe and Australia (as a relatively low-profile title), but never in North America[22]. Its sequel Kururin Paradise (2002) was Japan-only despite being shown at E3 2002 during its development[23]. By the time Kururin Squash! was developed, Nintendo may have determined that there was not enough worldwide demand to justify localization, especially given the mixed success of the earlier games abroad[24]. As one commentary noted, since Kururin Paradise never saw an overseas release, it “makes sense that Kururin Squash! didn’t either, so the series still hasn’t shown up in America aside from in the Super Smash Bros. games”[24]. Indeed, Kururin Squash! was never officially announced for Western markets, and no English-language version exists. Import enthusiasts could play the game on a GameCube via modding or using a disc swapping tool (as it has no region-free release).
Because it remained Japan-exclusive, Kururin Squash! did not receive extensive marketing in the West. However, it garnered attention among GameCube import gamers and was covered by some Western gaming media as an import review or feature. Its colorful graphics and Nintendo pedigree made it of interest despite the niche status. Over time, the game became something of a cult classic for puzzle game fans, known for its polished design and the curiosity of being a first-party Nintendo title Western players never officially got. The release remains the final game in the Kururin series – after 2004, no further sequels were developed. Eighting, the developer, shifted focus back to other projects (they are known for fighting games and other genres) after completing the Kururin series[25]. This GameCube installment thus represents the culmination of the series.
## Reception
Kururin Squash! was met with generally positive reviews from those who played it. In Japan, the popular magazine Weekly Famitsu scored the game 29 out of 40 (the four reviewers gave individual scores of 8, 6, 7, and 8)[26]. This indicated a fairly good reception, though slightly lower than some higher-profile Nintendo titles of the time. The Famitsu reviewers praised the game’s charming presentation and clever gameplay mechanics, but one of the lower scores suggested that certain aspects (possibly the game’s difficulty or depth) were seen as less impressive.
In the West, Kururin Squash! was not widely reviewed due to its lack of official release, but several gaming outlets and import reviewers gave it high marks. Nintendo World Report (then known as Planet GameCube) reviewed the import and awarded the game a 9.5 out of 10[27]. The review lauded Kururin Squash! for its “simple, yet fun concept” and described the title as a natural evolution of the series’ addictive puzzle formula[28]. The critic highlighted the game’s graphics and art style as a standout feature: the transition from 2D sprites on GBA to fully polygonal 3D on GameCube was handled excellently, with bright, crisp visuals and whimsical character animations[21][6]. Particle effects like dust clouds, lighting, and explosions were noted to be unusually high-quality for a puzzle game, adding to the visual appeal[29]. Nintendo World Report also praised the level design and new gameplay additions (like the special Helirin abilities and shop system) for expanding the game’s depth while maintaining the series’ accessible gameplay[30][11]. The review’s only minor criticisms were aimed at the audio (the music tracks, while catchy, are short loops, and the character sound effects can be a bit repetitive)[31][32]. Overall, the site strongly recommended Kururin Squash! to puzzle game fans, lamenting that it “never saw the light of day outside of Japan” and calling all three Kururin games “stellar” in quality[28].
Another import review by Kikizo (a UK-based gaming outlet) gave Kururin Squash! a positive score of 8.5 out of 10[33]. The reviewer described the game as “immediately accessible” and “addictive”, praising its elegant simplicity and the way it builds challenge as levels progress[34]. The review enjoyed the multiplayer modes, noting that while Kururin Squash! might not have the same party-game fame as titles like Super Monkey Ball, it provided “some hours of fun for four” players and could easily be picked up by anyone due to its intuitive concept[19]. Kikizo also complimented the level design and pacing: early levels ease the player in, but later stages become “devilishly difficult in practice,” testing the player’s skill and reflexes despite the straightforward goal[35][36]. This gradual difficulty curve was seen as well-balanced. On the downside, the Kikizo review implied that the boss battles – a new feature in Squash – felt a bit out of place or forced (“boss battles that fit as well as you’d expect in a game where you play as a giant propeller” was the tongue-in-cheek remark)[10]. In other words, while the bosses added variety, they were perhaps not the strongest element of the game’s design. Nevertheless, the overall verdict was that Kururin Squash! offered “concentrated gameplay genius” presented in a charming way, and the reviewer “heartily recommend[ed]” the game to fans of Nintendo’s quirky puzzle titles[37][38].
Across the board, those who reviewed or imported Kururin Squash! agreed that it was a high-quality puzzle/action game. Its unique mechanic of navigating a spinning stick through obstacles was seen as easy to learn but tough to master, providing a satisfying challenge. The colorful, whimsical presentation and smooth controls were frequently cited as strengths. Some critics and players did comment on the game’s difficulty being a bit more forgiving than its GBA predecessors – for example, the larger screen and analog control on the GameCube made precision a little easier, and one could argue the game is slightly more accessible as a result. However, others appreciated this approach, noting that Kururin Squash! remained engaging rather than frustrating, and still offered plenty of optional challenges for experts (such as trying to perfect-clear every stage or compete in time trials). Overall, Kururin Squash! earned a reputation as a “hidden gem” on the GameCube, with retrospective coverage often regretting that it was never officially released globally.
## Legacy
As of its 2004 release, Kururin Squash! is the final entry in the Kururin series. No further sequels or spin-offs have been produced, making it the last game to feature Kururin and the Helirin. The series thus concluded with three main titles (the two Game Boy Advance games and this GameCube installment)[3]. The character of Kururin and the Helirin did not entirely disappear, however. In 2008, Nintendo included the Helirin as an Assist Trophy in the crossover fighting game Super Smash Bros. Brawl for the Wii[39]. When summoned in the battle, the Helirin trophy spawns the familiar rotating propeller stick on the stage, where it can spin and knock fighters around – a direct nod to the gameplay of Kuru Kuru Kururin. This cameo in Super Smash Bros. marked the first time many players outside Japan were exposed to the Kururin character. Later Smash Bros. titles continued to acknowledge the series with trophies and spirits, indicating a lasting, if quiet, legacy within Nintendo’s franchise history.
The unique game mechanics of the Kururin series have also inspired other developers. Notably, the 2014 indie game Roundabout (by No Goblin) cited Kuru Kuru Kururin as a major inspiration[40]. Roundabout is a driving/puzzle game in which the player controls a constantly rotating limousine navigating through environments – a clear homage to Kururin’s rotating-stick gameplay, translated into a different setting. The existence of such a title demonstrates how Kururin Squash! and its predecessors left a creative mark, despite their limited release. Critics have pointed out that Kururin Squash! in particular would have been well-suited for a wider audience; its simple controls and minimal text mean that language is not a barrier, and puzzle games generally have global appeal[28]. Over the years, Western fans have occasionally included Kururin Squash! in wishlists of games they’d like to see localized or re-released. However, the prospects of an official localization are very slim – for example, a 2015 Game Informer feature rated the “chances of localization” for Kururin Squash! as “Very Low”, noting that the GameCube platform is not supported on modern Virtual Console services and that the series has no current momentum[3].
While Kururin Squash! remains a Japan-only title, the advent of the Wii U Virtual Console in the mid-2010s did bring the earlier Kururin games to new audiences (the original Kuru Kuru Kururin was finally released in North America via the Wii U’s eShop in 2016). This gave hope to some fans that Kururin Squash! might someday resurface, potentially through a remaster or inclusion in a compilation. As of 2025, no such re-release has occurred, and the game is only playable on original hardware or via emulation. Nonetheless, Kururin Squash! enjoys a small but enthusiastic cult following. It is often remembered as a standout example of a creative Nintendo puzzle game, and a piece of the GameCube’s diverse library. The game’s legacy lives on through its influence on later games and the fond memories of those who experienced its spinning, maze-navigating challenge.
