# Ang Totoong Narcolist
Ang Totoong Narcolist (Filipino for “The True Narco List”) is a 2019 online video series in the Philippines featuring an anonymous narrator, alias “Bikoy,” who alleged that President Rodrigo Duterte’s family and associates were involved in the illegal drug trade[1]. The videos, released on social media in April 2019, presented documents and code names purportedly showing drug payoffs to Duterte’s son Paolo Duterte, his long-time aide (and future senator) Christopher “Bong” Go, and other individuals[2][3]. Malacañang (the presidential palace) dismissed the accusations as “black propaganda”[4], and Bong Go publicly bared his back to prove he did not have the dragon tattoo that the videos claimed was a secret triad identifier linking him to a narcotics syndicate[5]. The videos sparked a political firestorm in the midst of Duterte’s war on drugs and the May 2019 elections. Weeks later, the man behind “Bikoy” surrendered and dramatically retracted his claims, asserting that the narcolist videos were a fabrication orchestrated by opposition figures – a reversal that led to sedition charges against various Duterte critics (later dismissed in court)[6][7]. In 2023, Bikoy (identified as Peter Joemel Advincula) was convicted of perjury for falsely implicating people in the supposed anti-Duterte plot[8].
## Background and Video Series Content
The emergence of “Ang Totoong Narcolist” came against the backdrop of President Duterte’s campaign against illegal drugs and his public naming of officials allegedly tied to the drug trade. In March 2019, Duterte released a “narco list” of 46 politicians accused of drug links, unprecedentedly exposing local officials purportedly involved in narcotics[9]. Just weeks later, starting on April 2, 2019, a series of anonymously produced videos began circulating on YouTube and Facebook under the title Ang Totoong Narcolist, turning the tables by accusing Duterte’s own relatives and allies of drug involvement[9]. The videos were posted on a channel reportedly associated with a certain “Metro Balita” news page and were released episodically in April 2019, in the run-up to the Philippine midterm elections.
Each episode featured a hooded figure who identified himself by the alias “Bikoy.” Speaking in Tagalog with his face hidden, Bikoy claimed to be a former member of a major drug syndicate operating in Luzon and the Visayas regions[10]. He described his role as a recorder of the cartel’s financial transactions – keeping an internal ledger of narcotics payoffs, known in underworld jargon as “TARA” (an allotted bribe or share). To bolster his allegations, Bikoy showed what he asserted were confidential documents: spreadsheets and bank transaction records of drug money deposits, complete with code names and signatures[11][2]. According to Bikoy, these documents detailed monthly deposits of drug money to bank accounts of certain “principals” identified by code. He explained that each code corresponded to a real individual, and he pointed to the involvement of figures in Duterte’s inner circle.
In Ang Totoong Narcolist – Episode 1, Bikoy’s narration focused on a person codenamed “POLO DELTA – TSG 01” and “ALPHA TIERRA – 0029,” whom he insinuated was Paolo Duterte (the president’s eldest son)[12][13]. He alleged that tens of millions of pesos in drug money were funneled into bank accounts linked to Paolo Duterte on a monthly basis via intermediaries. Bikoy identified one supposed intermediary as Waldo Carpio, an Agriculture department official who is the brother of Duterte’s son-in-law (Manases Carpio)[2]. The narration claimed that in 2018, Waldo Carpio’s account received ₱30 million per month in drug funds, which were then transferred to an account allegedly belonging to Paolo Duterte[14]. Episode 2 continued with further claims, including a mention of another code name “VERA REGA” tied to a certain “Veronica” – a detail that was widely interpreted as alluding to Duterte’s young daughter Veronica “Kitty” Duterte (although the videos did not explicitly name the child)[15]. These first two episodes primarily targeted the Duterte family, prompting immediate denials.
Episode 3 of Ang Totoong Narcolist shifted focus to Christopher “Bong” Go, President Duterte’s closest aide and a candidate for senator in 2019. The video accused Go of being linked to the drug syndicate under the code name “TESOROGOLF – TSG 002,” and claimed he had four bank accounts receiving drug payoffs totaling as much as ₱3 billion[3]. Most sensationally, Bikoy alleged that Bong Go bore a tattoo of a dragon on his back containing an alphanumeric code, supposedly proof of his membership in a Chinese triad involved in the narcotics trade[16]. (Go’s own middle name is “Tesoro,” a fact the code name seemed to play on[17].) This dramatic accusation led Bong Go to respond by publicly exposing his back at campaign rallies and on national television – twice – to show that he had no such tattoo, thereby refuting Bikoy’s claim[5]. Photographs of a tattoo-less Bong Go were widely circulated, and Duterte’s spokesman seized on them as evidence that the narcolist videos were “mere black propaganda”[4][5].
Subsequent episodes (Episodes 4 and 5) continued to name alleged players in the supposed drug-money scheme. In these videos, Bikoy implicated other individuals both in and out of government. Notably, in Episode 5, he accused a businessman identified as Elizaldy Co of being a central drug lord heading the syndicate, a claim that prompted at least one legislator allied with Co to call for an investigation into the videos’ dissemination[18]. By the end of the five-part video series (the final episode was released on April 22, 2019), Bikoy had painted an elaborate conspiracy in which Duterte’s family members (including Paolo Duterte) and associates like Bong Go, as well as certain businessmen and lawyers, were raking in proceeds from the illegal drug trade. These explosive allegations, coming amid a heated election season, put the administration on the defensive and led to swift efforts to track down the source of the videos.
## Identification of “Bikoy” and Initial Investigation
As the Ang Totoong Narcolist videos went viral, Philippine authorities moved quickly to investigate their origin. In late April 2019, the National Bureau of Investigation (NBI) arrested Rodel Jayme, an I.T. professional who was suspected of helping upload or spread the videos online. Jayme was the developer of a website (metrobalita.net) where the videos first appeared. He denied authoring the content, but on May 6, 2019, the Department of Justice filed charges against Rodel Jayme for inciting to sedition in connection with the Bikoy videos[19]. This marked the first official legal action stemming from the video scandal. Jayme later pleaded not guilty, maintaining that he only uploaded content provided to him, but the case signaled the government’s determination to pursue those behind the “Ang Totoong Narcolist” series.
Even as investigators hunted for the mastermind, the mystery of Bikoy’s identity quickly unraveled. On May 6, 2019, the same day charges were announced against Jayme, a man named Peter Joemel Advincula dramatically surfaced at the national headquarters of the Integrated Bar of the Philippines (IBP) in Pasig City. In a press conference held at the IBP, Advincula announced that he was “Bikoy,” the hooded whistleblower in the videos[20]. Removing his hood, he faced the media and affirmed the veracity of the allegations made in Ang Totoong Narcolist. Advincula provided additional detail about himself: he claimed to have been a former marketing executive of a drug syndicate and said this background gave him direct knowledge of the drug payoffs and bank accounts mentioned in the videos. Notably, he brought documents to the IBP event that he said corroborated the videos’ contents, including what he described as copies of the syndicate’s ledgers.
During this appearance, Advincula also denied any political motivation or backing, portraying himself as a concerned citizen and whistleblower. Speaking partly in Filipino, he insisted, “Wala akong kaugnayan sa kahit sinong kandidato o partido sa politika” (“I have no links to any candidate or political party”), emphasizing that he was not being used by the opposition[21]. At the time, the Duterte administration had hinted that the videos could be part of a broader plot by its critics to discredit the President ahead of the elections. Advincula, however, flatly denied being an agent of any such scheme on May 6. The Integrated Bar of the Philippines, a nationwide lawyers’ association, said Advincula had come to them seeking free legal assistance to sue those he implicated in the drug trade; the IBP declined to represent him, reportedly wary of being politicized in the controversy[22].
Advincula’s outing of himself as Bikoy made headlines and lent a short-lived credibility boost to the narcolist videos. His story dominated news cycles for a few days, and opposition figures called for the allegations to be investigated rather than dismissed outright. Even Senator Leila de Lima – a detained opposition senator and noted Duterte critic – argued that Bikoy’s claims “should be probed” instead of reflexively labeled as fake, given their gravity[23]. Meanwhile, administration allies continued to assail Advincula’s character. It soon emerged that Peter Joemel Advincula had a criminal past: records showed he had previously been convicted of estafa (financial fraud) and had spent time in prison[24]. This revelation, which Advincula himself acknowledged, raised doubts about his credibility. Duterte’s spokesperson mocked Bikoy as an “ex-convict” peddling lies for money, and the Philippine National Police (PNP) initially indicated skepticism about the veracity of his documents. Nevertheless, at that moment in early May 2019, Advincula was sticking to his story – and the Duterte family was under the shadow of serious accusations.
## Retraction and Allegations of Opposition “Plot”
Just over two weeks after his public appearance at the IBP, Advincula abruptly changed his story. In a stunning turnaround, the man who had stood by the narcolist allegations recanted everything. On May 23, 2019, Advincula surrendered to the Philippine National Police (PNP) and was presented in a press briefing at Camp Crame (the PNP headquarters). Flanked by top police officials, he read out a new sworn statement and issued a public confession: all of the allegations in the Ang Totoong Narcolist videos were “fabricated lies.” Advincula declared that the videos were not a genuine exposé of Duterte’s drug links, but rather a manufactured propaganda plot orchestrated by opposition politicians[6].
In this press conference, Advincula (Bikoy) claimed that he had been recruited by members of the Liberal Party (LP) and other Duterte critics to create and spread the narcolist videos as part of a scheme to undermine the administration. He specifically accused Senator Antonio Trillanes IV, a prominent opposition figure and vocal Duterte antagonist, of being the mastermind of the video production[25]. He also implicated the camp of Vice President Leni Robredo (the leader of the opposition) and senators aligned with the Liberal Party, such as Senator Risa Hontiveros and former Senator Bam Aquino, as having knowledge of or involvement in the plot[26]. According to Advincula’s new narrative, the elaborate plan was code-named “Project Sodoma.” Its supposed aim was to oust President Duterte from power by spreading scandalous misinformation, thereby boosting the opposition’s chances in the elections and paving the way for Robredo to become President[7].
Advincula provided a dramatic backstory for how the videos were allegedly concocted. He claimed that starting in August 2018, he was taken in by a group of priests and opposition supporters, and was hidden in a convent in Manila where the videos were later recorded[27][28]. He alleged that “everything in the videos was scripted” – he was fed lines and fake documents to recite on camera, all under the direction of Trillanes and other conspirators. The hooded outfit and voice distortion in the videos, he said, were meant to conceal that he was acting out a role. Advincula asserted that the true objective of the exercise was to smear administration candidates (particularly Bong Go, who was then running for senator) and “divert the public’s attention” by stirring up outrage against Duterte’s family[29][30].
He further explained his motive for coming forward with the supposed truth. Advincula said that after the opposition’s Otso Diretso slate was defeated in the May 13, 2019 elections (none of the opposition’s senatorial candidates won a seat), the plotters abandoned him and he feared for his life[31]. With the Liberal Party’s plan failing and Duterte’s allies victorious, Advincula claimed he was no longer useful to those who enlisted him and that he even suspected they might “silence” him to erase evidence. This, he stated, prompted him to surrender to the police and reveal the scheme before he could be harmed[31].
The PNP and the Duterte administration heralded Advincula’s retraction as a vindication. Government officials said his new testimony proved that the Ang Totoong Narcolist videos were a “weapon of fake news” used by desperate political enemies. Police quickly pivoted to building a case based on Advincula’s claims of an opposition-led conspiracy. Critics, however, pointed out the extreme turnaround in Advincula’s statements and questioned whether his recantation was credible or made under duress. Media watchdogs and fact-checkers noted inconsistencies in Advincula’s various versions of events[6][32]. Nonetheless, his May 23 flip-flop had immediate legal repercussions for those he accused.
## Legal Aftermath
### Sedition Complaint and Dismissal
Armed with Advincula’s new affidavit, the PNP’s Criminal Investigation and Detection Group (CIDG) filed a criminal complaint in July 2019 against numerous opposition figures. The complaint charged over 30 individuals – including Vice President Leni Robredo, Senators Leila de Lima and Risa Hontiveros, former Senator Antonio Trillanes IV, several Catholic bishops, lawyers, and members of the Liberal Party – with sedition, inciting to sedition, cyber libel, libel, harboring a criminal, and obstruction of justice, among other offenses[33]. The centerpiece of the complaint was Advincula’s claim that these respondents had conspired to produce the Ang Totoong Narcolist videos as part of “Project Sodoma” to oust Duterte[33]. Essentially, the police accused the opposition of plotting a coup d’etat via propaganda – a charge the accused all vehemently denied.
The Department of Justice convened a panel to evaluate the complaint. In January 2020, after several months of investigation and hearings, the DOJ panel released a resolution partly affirming and partly dismissing the allegations. The DOJ dropped all major charges against most of the respondents, including Vice President Robredo, finding no sufficient evidence of a seditious plot to topple the government[34]. Prosecutors noted that while the Ang Totoong Narcolist videos contained “libelous imputations” against President Duterte and his family that could stir public outrage, they did not find any clear exhortation for the populace to rise up in mass protest or violence[35]. Thus, the element of inciting a rebellion or insurrection (required for sedition) was deemed lacking. All respondents were cleared of the sedition, inciting to sedition, and other complaints like libel and obstruction of justice due to lack of probable cause[34].
However, the DOJ did move to indict a smaller subset of individuals on a lesser charge. In February 2020, it formally indicted 11 people, including Peter Joemel Advincula himself, for “conspiracy to commit sedition.” Those charged in court comprised Advincula (alias Bikoy), ex-Senator Trillanes, two Catholic priests (Fr. Albert Alejo, SJ and Fr. Flaviano Villanueva), a dismissed police official (Eduardo Acierto), and several noted opposition figures and activists such as Yolanda Ong (a political communications strategist), Vicente Romano III (former tourism official), Jonnell Sangalang, Joel Saracho, “Boom” Enriquez, and a certain alias “Monique”[36]. Notably, Vice President Robredo and most elected opposition politicians were not included in the indictment[34]. The DOJ’s indictment stated that these 11 individuals, acting in concert, had coordinated to spread the Bikoy videos and incite public hatred against Duterte as part of a “grand conspiracy”[37]. The charge of conspiracy to commit sedition under the Revised Penal Code is a felony, but less severe than actual sedition; it essentially alleged that the accused planned to sow unrest even if no uprising occurred.
Those indicted surrendered or voluntarily appeared in court to post bail in early 2020, maintaining their innocence. The case then proceeded to trial at the Quezon City Metropolitan Trial Court. Over the next couple of years, however, the sedition-conspiracy case faltered. The prosecution’s case relied heavily on Advincula’s testimony, yet Advincula did not take the witness stand during the trial[38]. By some accounts, he could not be contacted or was unwilling to repeat his claims under oath in open court. Without Bikoy’s live testimony, the remaining evidence (such as gadget receipts and meeting photos presented by the DOJ) was largely circumstantial. In September 2023, the court dismissed the conspiracy to commit sedition case against Trillanes, Advincula, and all the other accused due to insufficient evidence[39]. In its ruling, the court noted that the prosecution failed to prove that any of the accused had actually conspired to spread the videos to incite sedition. It emphasized that much of the government’s case was hearsay without Advincula’s direct verification in court[40][38]. The judge concluded that there was no way to establish the truth of Advincula’s allegations if he himself did not substantiate them under oath, and thus a guilty verdict could not be supported. All of the accused were accordingly acquitted or cleared, and the sedition plot case was put to rest.
### Perjury Conviction of Advincula (Bikoy)
Even as the sedition case against the opposition unraveled, Peter Joemel Advincula (Bikoy) faced his own legal troubles for the conflicting stories he had told. In July 2019, when Advincula submitted his sworn statements accusing various opposition members (the Project Sodoma affidavit), he directly implicated three human rights lawyers – Jose Manuel “Chel” Diokno, Theodore Te, and Erin Tañada III – as alleged co-conspirators who masterminded or facilitated the Bikoy videos. These three lawyers, all respected figures and coincidentally opposition-aligned (they were part of the Free Legal Assistance Group and two had run as opposition senatorial candidates), vehemently denied Advincula’s accusations. After the DOJ cleared them of the sedition complaint in early 2020, Diokno, Te, and Tañada turned the tables on Advincula by filing a perjury case against him. They asserted that Advincula willfully lied in his affidavits, falsely implicating them in a crime, which is a criminal offense (perjury) since those statements were made under oath.
The perjury case went to trial in Manila. Advincula was accused of making false testimonies in his July 2019 sworn statement – specifically, the lie that Diokno, Te, and Tañada had met with him at Ateneo de Manila University on a certain date to plan ousting Duterte[41][42]. (In reality, the lawyers were attending an election forum at Ateneo and said they never met Advincula there, as confirmed by event records[43].) During the trial, evidence showed that Advincula’s assertions were untrue, and the court noted that the DOJ itself had earlier observed that Advincula was probably the one committing falsehoods about the supposed conspiracy[44].
On May 24, 2023, the Metropolitan Trial Court of Manila found Peter Joemel Advincula guilty of perjury beyond reasonable doubt[8]. In its decision, the court cited the “falsehood” of Advincula’s claims against the three lawyers and stressed that his sworn statements were refuted by solid evidence[45]. Advincula was sentenced to a minimum of 3 months and 1 day to a maximum of 1 year and 1 day in prison for the offense[46]. The conviction effectively affirmed that his allegations of an opposition plot were lies. Advincula’s camp indicated he would appeal the verdict, but the judgment was hailed by the falsely accused individuals as a measure of justice. It also cast a definitive cloud over the veracity of the original Ang Totoong Narcolist claims.
