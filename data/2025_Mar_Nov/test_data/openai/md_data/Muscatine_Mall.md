# Muscatine Mall
Muscatine Mall is an enclosed shopping mall in Muscatine, Iowa, United States. Built in 1971, it was originally known as Muscatine Plaza and featured Montgomery Ward and a Woolworth store as its first anchor tenants. The mall enjoyed success for several decades, housing over 50 stores at its peak, but it entered a prolonged decline in the early 2000s after the loss of multiple major retailers. As of the mid-2020s, Muscatine Mall is largely vacant and has been repurposed with a few non-traditional tenants, leading many observers to characterize it as a “dead mall”[1]. Its remaining anchor establishments include a furniture outlet, a home furnishings store, and an indoor sports training center in lieu of traditional department stores.
## Opening and Expansion (1970s–1980s)
Plans for Muscatine Mall were announced in 1970 by General Growth, and construction began that year on a site along Park Avenue (formerly part of U.S. Route 61) on Muscatine’s north side[2]. Montgomery Ward was the first anchor to open, launching its new 55,000 sq ft department store at the mall site on November 27, 1970[3]. The mall’s official grand opening followed in August 1971 with a ribbon-cutting ceremony and community festivities[4]. A twin-screen cinema (the Plaza Theatre) also opened in 1971 as part of the complex[5][6]. Early major tenants included a F.W. Woolworth variety store (which later operated under the Woolco name) and a Hy-Vee supermarket, as well as a Walgreens drugstore[7]. By its first anniversary in 1972 the mall housed 27 stores, and local leaders noted it had quickly become a regional shopping hub[8].
A third department store, J.C. Penney, was added in 1975. JCPenney built a 48,000 sq ft store on the mall’s east end, relocating from Muscatine’s downtown and giving the mall a trio of anchors[9]. Around this time the name “Muscatine Mall” came into common use in place of Muscatine Plaza[10]. The late 1970s brought significant changes: Montgomery Ward closed its Muscatine Mall store in September 1978, as the company deemed the location too small for its full merchandise line[11][12]. The following year, Davenport-based Petersen Harned Von Maur (now known simply as Von Maur) took over the vacated Wards space, opening a remodeled department store in August 1979[12]. During the same period, Hy-Vee relocated to a larger stand-alone location nearby, and Muscatine Mall changed ownership – in 1980 General Growth sold the property (along with nine other malls) to Aetna Life Insurance, which retained the Rouse Company as manager[13].
In the 1980s, Muscatine Mall continued to expand and update its offerings. Woolworth’s discount division, Woolco, operated in the north anchor spot until the chain’s nationwide shutdown in early 1983[14]. That space was quickly filled by Walmart, which opened on July 1, 1983[14]. This Walmart was notable as the company’s first store in Iowa and its northernmost U.S. location at the time, nicknamed the “Yankee” store by Walmart executives[15]. The arrival of Walmart, alongside existing anchors JCPenney and Von Maur, helped draw additional national retailers to the mall in the mid-1980s. Interior renovations were made, new mall entrances were added, and popular chain stores such as County Seat, Maurices, and Foot Locker joined the tenant mix[16][17]. By 1990 Muscatine Mall spanned roughly 345,000 sq ft and housed space for about 50 stores, solidifying its role as Muscatine’s primary shopping destination[18].
## Decline and Changes (1990s–2000s)
The mall’s fortunes began to turn in the 1990s as several anchors and longtime tenants departed. Local department store Spurgeon’s closed in 1992 after its parent company went bankrupt[19]. In 1994, two major blows came: Walmart announced plans to relocate to a new standalone Supercenter, and Von Maur likewise decided to exit Muscatine Mall when its lease expired[20][21]. Von Maur’s closure (completed by 1995) and Walmart’s relocation in mid-1997 resulted in the loss of two key anchors within a short period[21][22]. The mall’s occupancy dropped sharply – by 1998 it was only around 60% occupied, reflecting the downturn in traffic after these exits[23].
To fill the vacant anchor spaces, the mall sought new tenants. Home improvement retailer Menards was recruited to take over the former Walmart space, opening in the summer of 1998[24]. That same year, ownership changed hands: the Rouse Company (which had become full owner in 1989) sold Muscatine Mall to a Chicago-based real estate firm, Landau & Heyman[25]. Landau & Heyman proposed a major overhaul to combat the mall’s decline – a plan to partially demolish the enclosed mall and convert it into an open-air strip center facing Park Avenue[26]. In 1999, the mall underwent some interior remodeling and prepared for this reconfiguration. During this time, a new junior anchor, Staples, opened in April 2000, occupying 24,000 sq ft of the old Von Maur store[27].
Despite these efforts, the redevelopment plan faltered. Staples experienced weak sales and closed after only two years (shuttering in early 2002), which halted momentum for the broader “de-malling” project[28][29]. Landau & Heyman ultimately canceled the conversion to a strip mall, opting to keep the remaining mall intact. Meanwhile, the exodus of national tenants continued. Walgreens, an original tenant, left the mall in 2000 for a freestanding store, and other chain stores like B. Dalton Bookseller, Payless ShoeSource, and a secondary Maurices clothing shop all closed around the same period[29].
The early 2000s did see one positive development: the addition of a new department store. In November 2003, Elder-Beerman opened in the anchor space created by combining the former Von Maur and Staples units[30]. Elder-Beerman, a chain under the Bon-Ton Stores umbrella, represented the mall’s only traditional department store after its arrival. (It was also the first Elder-Beerman location west of the Mississippi River[30].) The boost was short-lived, however. In 2005, Menards revealed plans to build a larger store elsewhere in Muscatine[31]. By November 2007, Menards had completed its new store and closed its Muscatine Mall location, leaving another anchor spot vacant[32][33]. Around the same time, Muscatine Mall was sold again – local investors (operating as Muscatine Mall Management LLC) purchased the property from Landau & Heyman in late 2007, bringing the mall under local ownership as it entered the 2010s[34].
## Recent Years (2010s–present)
In the 2010s, Muscatine Mall continued to lose its remaining traditional retail anchors, even as a few new uses emerged. Bon-Ton consolidated its store brands in 2008, rebranding the Muscatine Elder-Beerman as Younkers, which was a more familiar department store name in Iowa[35]. After the departure of Menards, the vacant north anchor building was filled in 2010 by Slumberland Furniture, a regional furniture retail chain[36][37]. The mall also gained some smaller new tenants around this time: a Jimmy John’s sandwich shop and a Shoe Sensation footwear store both opened in the late 2000s or early 2010s, each with exterior entrances at the mall[36][38].
A significant change came in 2013 with the closure of the mall’s longtime movie theater. Fridley Theatres shut down the aging Plaza 4 cinema (which had been in operation since 1971) and opened a brand-new 10-screen multiplex, the Palms 10, at an off-site location in Muscatine[39]. The loss of the only movie theater at Muscatine Mall further reduced foot traffic. The following year, J.C. Penney closed its Muscatine Mall store in May 2014 as part of a corporate plan to eliminate underperforming locations[40][41]. JCPenney’s departure ended its nearly 40-year presence at the mall and left another anchor space vacant. Local officials and residents voiced concerns as these closures left Muscatine with very limited in-town shopping options for apparel and general merchandise[42][43].
In 2018, the mall’s last remaining department store closed when Bon-Ton Inc. went bankrupt. The Muscatine Younkers (formerly Elder-Beerman) was shuttered along with all other Bon-Ton stores nationwide in August 2018[44][45]. The collapse of Younkers marked the end of traditional retail anchors at Muscatine Mall. By this time, the interior of the mall had many empty storefronts, and only a handful of national chains or local shops were still operating in the corridors.
Muscatine Mall’s ownership sought alternative tenants to utilize the vacant anchor buildings. In March 2019, Compton’s Furniture & More, a Missouri-based furniture retailer, opened in the former Younkers/Elder-Beerman space[46]. This store was short-lived; by 2021 the large south anchor spot had been taken over by Muscatine Warehouse Bargains, a local discount furniture and appliance outlet that now occupies that space[47]. The east anchor wing, left empty by JCPenney, found new life not as retail but as a community sports facility: in 2022 it was converted into The Lab, an indoor training center for youth baseball and softball operated by local coaches[48]. The Lab’s opening in the former department store spot underscored the mall’s transition away from conventional shopping use.
As of 2025, Muscatine Mall remains open but functions with a very limited retail presence. Slumberland Furniture continues to operate in the old Walmart/Menards anchor location, and Muscatine Warehouse Bargains and The Lab anchor the other ends of the mall[49][50]. A few smaller businesses also persist, such as a shoe repair shop that changed hands in 2024 to avoid closure[51]. However, large sections of the interior concourse are dark and unused, with many storefronts permanently closed. In April 2025, a JOANN Fabrics and Crafts store (one of the last national chain tenants in the mall) shut down as part of that chain’s nationwide store closures[52]. The mall has occasionally hosted special events – for example, car show expos were held in the mostly vacant halls in 2024 and 2025 – but regular shopper traffic is very sparse[53][54]. With the loss of traditional anchors and most inline stores, Muscatine Mall is widely regarded as a “dead mall,” kept alive by a few niche uses and local businesses[1]. City officials have acknowledged the mall’s struggles and have explored revitalization efforts, but due to Muscatine’s proximity to larger retail centers in the Quad Cities and Iowa City, attracting new retail development to the mall has proven challenging[43][55].
