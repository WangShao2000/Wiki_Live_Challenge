# Walther Dobbertin
Walther Alexander Dobbertin (28 August 1882 – 12 January 1961) was a German photographer and publisher, known for his work in the colony of German East Africa[1]. He documented life in East Africa and served as the only German combat photographer during World War I in that theater[2]. After the war, Dobbertin returned to Germany, where he published an influential photo book about the East African campaign in 1932[3]. Many of his photographs are preserved in archives and remain important visual records of the German colonial period.
## Early life and career in East Africa
Dobbertin was born in Berlin in 1882[1]. In 1903, at age 21, he emigrated to German East Africa and began working in the photography business in Dar es Salaam[4]. He initially trained under photographer Carl Vincenti but left to establish his own studio in 1906[4]. Over the next decade, Dobbertin opened book and art shops in Dar es Salaam, Tanga, and Moshi, and became the colony’s most prominent professional photographer[5][6]. He captured a wide range of subjects, from landscapes and city views to portraits of local people and colonial officials, providing an important visual record of the region.
## World War I documentation
When World War I reached East Africa in 1914, Dobbertin joined the German Schutztruppe (colonial forces). He served as the only combat photographer on the German side during the East African campaign[2], capturing images of the troops (including the askari, the African soldiers) in action. He continued this work until he was taken prisoner by British forces in 1916[2]. At war’s end in 1918, Germany lost its East African colony; German civilians (including Dobbertin) were expelled and their property was confiscated[7]. Despite these setbacks, Dobbertin managed to preserve a large collection of his glass-negative plates, ensuring that his East African photographs survived the war.
## Later life, publications, and legacy
After his release from captivity, Dobbertin returned to Germany in 1920[7]. He settled in Wiedenhof near Buchholz (in northern Germany) and opened a bookshop, later also establishing a small publishing and printing business in nearby Harburg[7]. Drawing on his colonial-era photos, he produced and sold postcards and prints that catered to public nostalgia for the lost German colonies[8][7]. For example, in 1920 he issued a set of picture postcards titled “Deutsche Arbeit in Ostafrika” (“German Work in East Africa”), depicting plantation and industrial scenes with patriotic captions urging Germans to remember their colonies[8].
In 1932, Dobbertin self-published a photographic book titled Die Soldaten Lettow-Vorbecks (“The Soldiers of Lettow-Vorbeck”)[3]. Subtitled “a book of German fighting spirit and military honor,” it contained 120 of his wartime photographs from the East African campaign[9]. The images celebrated the German Schutztruppe askaris and officers, documenting their experiences during the war. Dobbertin’s photographs were also used in other works, such as the 1933 book Schwarz-Weiß-Rot über Ostafrika by Alfred Funke, which commemorated Germany’s colonial army[10]. During the Nazi era, Dobbertin became a member of the Nazi Party’s paramilitary wing (the SA) and served as a local leader (Kreisleiter) of the Reichskolonialbund, an official colonial league in the Third Reich[11]. After World War II, these organizations were disbanded; Dobbertin lived quietly in West Germany. He died in January 1961 in Wiedenhof (Jesteburg), south of Hamburg, at the age of 78[1].
Dobbertin left behind a vast photographic oeuvre. His original glass negatives were later acquired by the German Federal Archives (Bundesarchiv) in Koblenz, which have made digital scans of his images available to the public[12]. Hundreds of his historical photographs of German East Africa can now be accessed online, thanks to the Bundesarchiv’s digitization efforts[13]. Modern historians recognize Walther Dobbertin as a key visual chronicler of German East Africa, and his photographs continue to be reproduced in publications and exhibitions about the colonial era and World War I in Africa[14][12].
