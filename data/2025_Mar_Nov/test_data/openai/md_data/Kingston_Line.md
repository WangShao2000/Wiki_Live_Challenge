# Kingston Line
The Kingston Line is a commuter rail service operated by the Massachusetts Bay Transportation Authority (MBTA) in southeastern Massachusetts. It runs approximately 35 miles (56 km) from Boston’s South Station to Kingston, Massachusetts, serving communities on the South Shore and in Plymouth County[1]. Along the route, the line makes stops at several intermediate stations – including major transfer points at JFK/UMass (Boston), Quincy Center, and Braintree – before continuing through suburban towns to its terminus in Kingston[2]. The Kingston Line is part of the MBTA’s "Old Colony" group of commuter rail lines that were restored in the late 20th century. Originally opened in the mid-19th century and later shut down for several decades, the line was reactivated in 1997 after extensive reconstruction[3]. A short branch to Plymouth was also operated as part of this line until 2021, when Plymouth station was closed indefinitely due to low ridership. All trains on the line now terminate at Kingston.
## History
19th Century Beginnings: The Kingston Line traces its history to the Old Colony Railroad, one of the earliest railroads in Massachusetts. The Old Colony Railroad opened service from Boston to Plymouth on November 10, 1845[4]. This rail line, running via towns like Kingston and North Plymouth, provided an important transportation link for the South Shore region in the 19th century. In 1893, the Old Colony Railroad was leased by the New York, New Haven and Hartford Railroad (the New Haven Railroad) as part of a large consolidation of New England railroads[5]. Under New Haven ownership, passenger trains continued to serve the South Shore for many decades.
Decline and 1959 Closure: By the mid-20th century, passenger rail travel on the South Shore had sharply declined due to the rise of automobile use and new highways (such as Route 3 and the Southeast Expressway) paralleling the rail route[6]. The New Haven Railroad eventually terminated all Old Colony Division commuter service effective June 30, 1959[3]. This closure ended passenger train service between Boston and towns like Plymouth and Kingston for nearly four decades. (An emergency state subsidy had briefly kept the trains running into 1959, but service ceased once the subsidy lapsed and the new highway opened.) Although passenger operations ended, the rail corridors were not abandoned: freight service continued on the former Old Colony lines under the New Haven’s successors, Penn Central and later Conrail, which served remaining freight customers in the Plymouth area. This ongoing freight traffic kept the tracks intact through the 1960s–1980s[7].
Restoration as “Old Colony” Lines: In the late 20th century, growing traffic congestion and environmental concerns led Massachusetts to reconsider commuter rail for the South Shore. As part of the Central Artery/Tunnel Project (the “Big Dig”) mitigation commitments in the early 1990s, the state agreed to restore commuter rail service on several Old Colony railroad routes[8]. The Kingston/Plymouth Line and the Middleborough/Lakeville Line were selected for reactivation in the first phase. Reconstruction of the rail infrastructure began in the mid-1990s, including new tracks, signals, and stations built to modern standards (with full high-level platforms for accessibility). The historic rail line into downtown Plymouth was not fully re-used; instead, the MBTA built a new Plymouth station at Cordage Park in North Plymouth (near the site of the former Cordage factory), shortening the line’s terminus. In addition, a new branch was constructed to reach a large park-and-ride station in Kingston (located near Route 3) just north of the original right-of-way[9].
After a 13-year planning and construction effort, MBTA commuter rail service on the Old Colony lines officially resumed on September 29, 1997[3]. The inaugural schedule provided peak-period trains from Boston to Kingston, with off-peak and weekend trains extended to the Plymouth station at Cordage Park[10]. (Kingston station, with its extensive parking facilities, became the primary terminus for most rush-hour trips, while Plymouth saw only limited service outside of rush hours.) The restoration was a major expansion of the MBTA system – adding over 60 miles of commuter rail – and cost on the order of $535–537 million according to contemporary reports. The Kingston/Plymouth route and the Middleborough route together attracted high initial ridership, marking a successful return of commuter rail to the South Shore[3]. In 2001, an infill stop at JFK/UMass was added to the Old Colony lines, allowing Kingston Line trains to stop in Dorchester for connections to the Red Line subway and local buses[11].
Recent Developments: The Kingston Line continued to operate through the 2000s and 2010s with steady ridership. A third Old Colony branch – the Greenbush Line to Scituate – opened in 2007 as a separate project, completing the trio of restored South Shore commuter lines[12]. Throughout this period, Plymouth station remained lightly used compared to Kingston. Service to Plymouth was limited to a subset of off-peak trains, and the station’s out-of-the-way location (north of Plymouth Center and accessible only via local roads) kept ridership low[13]. In late 2020, as part of system-wide pandemic-related budget cuts, the MBTA proposed suspending service to several low-ridership commuter rail stations, including Plymouth. This plan was enacted the following year. Plymouth station was indefinitely closed beginning April 5, 2021, with the MBTA citing operational challenges, low utilization, and the availability of nearby Kingston station as justification[14]. The shutdown was described as a temporary measure at the time, but as of 2025 the station remains closed with no announced reopening date. Since the closure, all trains on the Kingston Line now terminate at Kingston, and the “Plymouth branch” is out of regular service.
## Route and Stations
Route: The Kingston Line uses the former Old Colony Railroad mainline running south from Boston. From South Station, trains travel through Dorchester and Quincy on tracks shared with other MBTA commuter lines. The first stop on the line, JFK/UMass, lies in Boston and provides a connection to the MBTA Red Line and several bus routes. Continuing south, the line reaches Quincy Center, another major intermodal station (with Red Line subway service). Just beyond Quincy, the Old Colony corridor splits: one branch (the Greenbush Line) diverges east toward Hingham, while Kingston Line trains continue on the main line to Braintree. Braintree Station is the last shared stop for the Kingston Line and the Middleborough/Lakeville Line; it likewise offers transfers to the Red Line and has a large park-and-ride garage. After Braintree, the Kingston Line veers southeast onto the former Plymouth branch of the Old Colony. The route passes through suburban South Shore communities in Norfolk and Plymouth counties, including South Weymouth, Abington, Whitman, Hanson, and Halifax[2]. Each of these towns is served by an MBTA station of the same name. The line’s terminus, Kingston station, is located off Marion Drive in Kingston near Route 3. Kingston station features a high-level platform and extensive parking facilities, as it was built in 1997 as a park-and-ride hub for the region[15]. (The now-closed Plymouth station is located about 7 miles further south of Kingston, near Cordage Park in North Plymouth.)
Stations: The active stations on the Kingston Line, from Boston outbound to Kingston, are:
South Station (Boston) – northern terminal, in downtown Boston (South Boston Waterfront).
JFK/UMass (Boston/Dorchester) – connects to MBTA Red Line and bus routes.
Quincy Center (Quincy) – connects to Red Line; serves Quincy’s downtown.
Braintree (Braintree) – major transfer to Red Line; large parking garage.
South Weymouth (Weymouth) – serves South Weymouth and adjacent towns.
Abington (Abington) – serves Abington.
Whitman (Whitman) – serves Whitman.
Hanson (Hanson) – serves Hanson.
Halifax (Halifax) – serves Halifax.
Kingston (Kingston) – southern terminus of the line (park-and-ride facility).
(Plymouth station, formerly located beyond Kingston in North Plymouth, is closed as of 2021.)
All stations on the Kingston Line are fully accessible (ADA-compliant), featuring high-level platforms for level boarding. They were constructed new during the 1990s Old Colony restoration project, in many cases at sites different from the original 19th-century depot locations. Because the restored stations were designed to serve modern commuter patterns, most are located outside town centers and offer ample parking for park-and-ride commuters[16][17].
Operations: The Kingston Line is part of the MBTA’s commuter rail network operated under contract by Keolis Commuter Services. Trains are typically pulled by diesel-electric locomotives and consist of bilevel commuter coaches. Service is provided seven days a week. On weekdays, the Kingston Line offers multiple trips during the morning and evening peak periods oriented toward commuters traveling to or from Boston, supplemented by off-peak trains at approximately hourly intervals. Weekend service is also available, generally on an hourly schedule. (During certain budget crises or emergencies, such as in 2020–21, the MBTA has temporarily reduced or suspended weekend and off-peak service, but normal schedules have since been restored.) The end-to-end travel time from Kingston to South Station is on the order of one hour. Major transfer opportunities are available at South Station (for MBTA subway lines, buses, and Amtrak intercity trains) and at Quincy Center/Braintree (for the rapid transit Red Line to Cambridge and downtown Boston).
Ridership: The Kingston/Plymouth Line has become a key transit link for Boston’s outer South Shore suburbs. In Fiscal Year 2013, the line carried approximately 1.67 million passenger trips over the course of the year[18]. This ridership placed it around the middle range of MBTA’s commuter rail lines in terms of usage. Kingston Station typically handles the majority of the line’s passenger traffic, given its role as a regional park-and-ride terminus. By contrast, the now-closed Plymouth Station saw relatively low daily boardings – one reason it was targeted for service cuts. Following the 2021 closure of Plymouth, Kingston remains the sole southern terminus and continues to serve as the primary boarding point for commuters from the Plymouth area (with some former Plymouth riders driving a short distance to Kingston to catch the train[13]).
Looking ahead, the Kingston Line is expected to remain an integral part of the MBTA Commuter Rail system. Ongoing system-wide improvement efforts (such as track upgrades and new signal systems) are being applied to the Old Colony lines to improve reliability and capacity[1]. While there are currently no announced expansion plans specific to the Kingston Line, the MBTA has long-term aspirations to modernize the commuter rail network with elements like increased frequency and potential electrification – changes that could affect the Kingston route in the future. Any such developments will depend on funding and policy decisions in the coming years. In the meantime, the Kingston Line continues to connect Boston with the South Shore, fulfilling the promise of restored rail service that was realized in 1997.
