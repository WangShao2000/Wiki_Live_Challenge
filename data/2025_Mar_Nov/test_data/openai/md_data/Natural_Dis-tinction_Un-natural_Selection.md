# Natural Dis-tinction, Un-Natural Selection
Natural Dis-tinction, Un-Natural Selection is the Spring/Summer 2009 women’s ready-to-wear collection designed by British fashion designer Alexander McQueen. The collection was presented on October 3, 2008 during Paris Fashion Week at Le 104 in Paris – a venue formerly used as a city morgue[1]. True to its title (a play on Charles Darwin’s On the Origin of Species), the show’s theme centered on nature and evolution, reflecting McQueen’s concerns about human impacts on the environment[2][3]. The runway staging underscored this concept: models walked amid a “zoo” of antique taxidermy animals (including a giraffe, elephant, tiger, zebra, polar bear, and lion) surrounding a projection of the Earth rotating on its axis[2][4]. Natural Dis-tinction, Un-Natural Selection is noted for its pioneering use of digitally engineered prints and its blend of romantic natural imagery with futuristic, synthetic elements[5]. Critics praised the collection’s imaginative presentation and wearable design, and it is often regarded as one of McQueen’s most commercially successful late-2000s collections[6].
## Background and Inspiration
Alexander McQueen developed Natural Dis-tinction, Un-Natural Selection as an artistic commentary on evolution and industrialization. According to the show’s notes, McQueen was inspired by Charles Darwin’s theory of natural selection and “the survival of the fittest,” as well as the deleterious effects of industrialization on the natural world[7]. The collection’s title intentionally evokes Darwin’s ideas – Natural Selection is the title of a chapter in Darwin’s 1859 work – while the phrase “Un-Natural Selection” hints at human-driven evolution or artificial interference in nature[8][9]. This thematic duality of natural vs. unnatural is woven throughout the collection’s design and presentation. McQueen described the project as a statement on humanity’s destructive impact on Earth, although he insisted “he doesn’t want to preach” outright[6]. The venue itself reinforced the theme: Le 104’s history as a morgue provided a “fitting venue” to confront the fragility of life and nature in the face of human-caused destruction[4]. Notably, the show’s invitation featured a lenticular image (created by McQueen’s nephew, Gary James McQueen) morphing Alexander McQueen’s face into a human skull – a memento mori symbol that hinted at the vulnerability of life and the collection’s ominous ecological message[10].
## Runway Presentation
The Natural Dis-tinction, Un-Natural Selection runway show was a theatrical tableau that dramatized its environmental narrative. The set was staged as a surreal Garden of Eden or Noah’s Ark: the catwalk was populated with lifelike stuffed animals, including endangered species such as an elephant, giraffe, polar bear, lion, rhinoceros, and stags[2][11]. These taxidermy figures stood on a sloped concrete runway around a large metallic globe mounted at the back of the stage[4]. Onto this globe, visual projections of celestial imagery (a glowing sun, a silver moon) and a slowly rotating Earth were cast throughout the show[12]. As the models emerged “from the mouth of the runway,” the spinning planet backdrop and encircling animals immediately signaled the collection’s eco-evolutionary theme[2].
The show was divided into two distinct sequences, each with its own mood and visual style to illustrate the narrative of untouched nature versus human-altered nature[9]. In the first half, representing a world “untouched by man,” the atmosphere and styling were ethereal and romantic rather than menacing[13]. The lighting and makeup were kept soft, and the soundtrack (along with the tranquil Earth imagery) established a reverent tone toward nature. McQueen’s models appeared in a palette of delicate, organic colors – pale earth tones, soft pastels – and even wore jeweled diadems resembling natural motifs (according to contemporary reports) to complement the theme. By contrast, the second half of the show grew darker and more surreal, symbolizing the negative impact of modern humanity on the environment[6]. The staging in this segment took on a harsher edge: as the projections on the globe shifted and lighting intensified, the gentle ambience gave way to an ominous, “futuristic” vibe. The juxtaposition of the two halves – one celebrating untouched nature, the other warning of its disruption – was visually striking yet, as critics noted, executed with restraint rather than didactic horror[14]. In fact, Women’s Wear Daily remarked that McQueen showed “unexpected restraint” in this collection, delivering a powerful message without the shock tactics that marked some of his earlier shows. Overall, the presentation earned praise as a “powerful show” and one of the most intriguing of the season, thanks to its blend of spectacle and substance[6].
## Design and Themes in the Collection
A dress from the collection’s second sequence, featuring a digitally engineered kaleidoscopic crystal print. McQueen’s strategic print placement creates a symmetrical motif that aligns with the garment’s seams and silhouette.
McQueen’s collection was acclaimed for its innovative design techniques, especially the use of digitally engineered prints to merge natural imagery with high fashion. In developing Natural Dis-tinction, Un-Natural Selection, McQueen and his team pioneered new digital printing methods that significantly influenced the wider fashion industry[5]. They used computer-manipulated photographs of natural materials – such as wood grain, minerals, and flowers – and printed these images onto fabric in symmetrical, kaleidoscopic patterns[15][16]. The prints were not random; they were carefully engineered to match each garment’s pattern pieces, so that when a dress was constructed, the motifs would form perfectly mirrored designs across seams and curves[17]. This technique produced artful, multidimensional visual effects on the clothing that had previously been unseen in dressmaking[17]. The digital prints were central to conveying the collection’s themes of nature vs. artifice – natural images were manipulated through technology to create something dramatically new.
In line with the show’s two-part structure, the garments themselves fell into two thematic groups. The first sequence of looks was inspired by an idyllic vision of nature. These designs featured organic silhouettes, soft colors, and natural fabrics, evoking a romantic, pre-industrial world[18]. McQueen employed materials like silk, linen, and cotton, which he embellished with digital prints of Earth’s own textures. For example, some tailored pieces used prints of wood grains and meadow flowers, rendered in gentle sepia or pastel tones and symmetrically arranged across the fabric[19]. The tailoring itself, while maintaining McQueen’s signature sharpness, was slightly relaxed and fluid here – for instance, structured jackets in a wood-grain print had a softer drape[20]. A number of dresses in this segment were cut almost in one piece of cloth, suggesting historical simplicity and a oneness with natural materials[21]. Understated embellishments reinforced the organic feel: whitework embroidery (delicate white-on-white stitching) and iridescent beetle-wing sequins added subtle shimmer reminiscent of insects and petals[22]. In one notable design, McQueen enclosed silk wildflowers under fine tulle overlays, giving the illusion of blossoms preserved in Victorian specimen jars[23]. This motif directly referenced the 19th-century practice of collecting and displaying natural specimens – a poetic nod to Darwin’s era and an early hint of mankind’s attempt to possess nature. Overall, the first half’s fashion combined McQueen’s classic shapes (frock coats, fitted hourglass dresses, and skinny trousers) with a newfound lightness and “unbuttoned romanticism,” as one reviewer observed[13]. The effect was graceful and almost whimsical: soft pink and lemon-yellow floral appliqués could be seen “trapped” beneath sheer nude netting on some dresses, and silvery frayed fringes draped gently from others, conjuring an image of dew-laden cobwebs or willow fronds[20]. By designing these looks, McQueen celebrated the pristine beauty of nature before modern intervention.
The second sequence of the collection presented a dramatic contrast, illustrating nature transformed (and in some ways distorted) by human technology and modernity. While this later group of outfits still drew inspiration from natural forms – featuring motifs of flowers, crystals, insects, and minerals – the treatment was much more hard-edged and “un-natural”[24]. The color palette shifted to bold and high-contrast tones: stark blacks and whites were prominent, accented by vivid synthetic colors like electric pinks, sapphire blues, and metallic sheens[25]. The digital prints in this sequence became more complex and geometric. McQueen introduced angular, fractured patterns that invoked images of crushed crystals and geological formations, as well as man-made structures. One striking print, for example, was inspired by the lattice of the Eiffel Tower – a symbol of the Industrial Age – and another print incorporated a mountain landscape photograph by Dan Holdsworth (a contemporary artist) to bring a raw natural element into a modern context[16]. In perhaps the most provocative print, a jeweled diamond pattern gradually morphed into the image of a human skeleton, literally blending a natural mineral motif with a human anatomical form[26]. This skeleton print underscored the collection’s Darwinian undertones, suggesting evolution or extinction at the hands of man.
The silhouettes in the second half also became more sculptural and assertive. McQueen built dresses with exaggerated, sharper shapes – for instance, bell jar-shaped dresses that ballooned outwards at the hips, reminiscent of crinoline or bell-jar containers[27]. These pieces were a dramatic evolution of the panniered shapes hinted at earlier in the collection: whereas the first half’s pannier-like skirts were soft and draped, the second half’s had a stiff, almost armored quality, as if the garments themselves were artifacts encasing something precious or dangerous. Many looks featured sheer or fitted bodysuits encrusted with crystals and metallic elements, bringing an armored, futuristic feel to the models’ appearance[28]. McQueen collaborated with Swarovski to achieve some of these dazzling effects: jet-black, gold, and silver Swarovski crystals were densely applied to fabrics, creating glimmering carapaces on evening dresses and catsuits[28]. Despite their sparkle, these crystal-encrusted designs conveyed a sense of harshness – the crystals formed jagged lines and crusts, suggesting the glitter of oil slicks or the sharpness of broken glass as much as luxury glamour. The use of synthetic materials further emphasized the “unnatural” concept: the collection incorporated modern textiles like Lycra, polyamide blends, and bonded metallic leather to give a high-tech sheen and structure to the garments[29]. For example, one sheath dress was made of a slick, plasticized fabric printed in a multi-color crystal pattern, paired with a matching cape – blending couture with the look of a futuristic survival suit. Accessories and styling complemented this aesthetic: shoes and ankle boots were covered in matching prints and often trimmed with lace or crystal details, and a few looks were topped with stiff sculptural headpieces. The overall impression of the second half was of evolution run amok – beautiful yet uncanny creations that blurred the line between the natural and the artificial.
## Reception and Influence
Natural Dis-tinction, Un-Natural Selection was met with critical acclaim for its creativity, craftsmanship, and thought-provoking theme. Fashion critics noted that McQueen struck a careful balance between fantasy and reality in this collection. Writing for Vogue, Sarah Mower highlighted the unexpected romantic delicacy in the first half of the show, remarking that the usually dark-edged McQueen instead revealed “the strongest side of [his] appeal” through soft colors and fluid designs inspired by an untouched natural world[13]. She praised details like the wood-grain tailoring and flower-strewn dresses under tulle for their exquisite execution, and pointed out that even when the collection transitioned to its darker, crystal-studded finale, it “never descended into melodrama”[30]. Importantly, reviewers observed that McQueen moderated his tendency toward theatrical extremity in service of a more cohesive message. Women’s Wear Daily, in its review, noted that “McQueen worked with unexpected restraint” in this show, delivering a potent statement “within relatively realistic parameters” – a decision that resulted in one of the standout collections of a somewhat lackluster season. In interviews, McQueen expressed that he did not wish to moralize or “lecture” the audience, and critics generally felt he succeeded in conveying an environmental cautionary tale implicitly, through the beauty and contrast of the clothes themselves[14].
Beyond its thematic impact, the collection was also recognized for its technical innovation and commercial potential. The digitally engineered prints were especially influential; the Los Angeles County Museum of Art later described Natural Dis-tinction, Un-Natural Selection as a “pioneering exploration of digital print design” that helped drive the widespread adoption of digital printing in fashion[5]. At the time of its release, such all-over photographic prints – precisely fitted to each garment – were novel on major Paris runways. McQueen’s successful use of this technique (for instance, the vibrant mirrored crystal patterns on jackets, leggings, and dresses) was noted as part of a broader trend that would soon flourish in the industry[31]. Commercially, the collection was expected to perform well. Vogue commented that amid the global financial downturn of 2008–09, McQueen had delivered a lineup that was not only artistically inspired but also “one of his most commercially viable” to date, thanks to its slightly eased tailoring and accessible yet still stunning pieces[6]. Indeed, many of the printed dresses, tailored suits, and accessories from Spring 2009 proved popular with buyers, bridging the gap between McQueen’s high-concept runway vision and wearable fashion. The collection’s mix of spectacle and sellability marked an evolution in McQueen’s approach – as he himself stated around this time, “it’s no longer about shock tactics, it’s about the purity of aesthetics… [while still being] fundamentally sellable”[14].
Several designs from Natural Dis-tinction, Un-Natural Selection have since attained iconic status and have been displayed in museum exhibitions. In 2011 and 2015, the retrospective Alexander McQueen: Savage Beauty (at the Metropolitan Museum of Art and the Victoria & Albert Museum, respectively) showcased pieces from this collection, underscoring its significance in McQueen’s body of work. One of the most celebrated outfits is the so-called “Bell Jar” dress: a short black dress encrusted entirely with glittering Swarovski crystals, named for its bell-like shape. This dress appeared in the collection’s finale, embodying the fusion of natural motif (its crystal embellishment reflecting geological forms) and human artifice. The Bell Jar dress was meticulously conserved and put on display in Savage Beauty, where it captivated visitors with its otherworldly sparkle[32]. Notably, the Bell Jar piece also crossed into popular culture; actress Cate Blanchett wore the original Swarovski crystal bell-jar dress from this collection to the Los Angeles premiere of The Curious Case of Benjamin Button in late 2008[33]. Blanchett’s appearance in McQueen’s dramatic creation (even before the runway show’s official Spring 2009 season began) garnered media attention and illustrated the collection’s appeal beyond the runway.
In retrospect, Natural Dis-tinction, Un-Natural Selection is remembered as a pivotal McQueen collection that melded conceptual artistry with technical innovation. It addressed timely environmental themes – a year that coincidentally marked the 150th anniversary of Darwin’s Origin of Species – through the evocative language of fashion, without resorting to didactic imagery. The collection’s influence can be seen in the subsequent wave of designers experimenting with digital prints and in McQueen’s own later works (most famously, his Spring 2010 Plato’s Atlantis collection would further explore evolution and climate change, using even more advanced digital printing)[34][35]. By the end of the Paris runway show in October 2008, as the final model in a glittering crystal-and-black ensemble walked past the menagerie of silent animals to the fading strains of the soundtrack, McQueen had delivered a haunting message: that nature’s fate lies in the balance, and that beauty and destruction now stand unnaturally hand in hand. The success of Natural Dis-tinction, Un-Natural Selection affirmed Alexander McQueen’s reputation for infusing fashion with profound narratives and cemented the collection’s place in fashion history as an example of high artistry meeting environmental consciousness.
