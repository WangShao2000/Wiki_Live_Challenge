# Grupo Frontera political controversy
Grupo Frontera political controversy refers to a public backlash and debate in early 2025 surrounding the American regional Mexican band Grupo Frontera over a perceived political endorsement. The controversy began when a TikTok video featuring a family member of the band (nicknamed “La Abuela Frontera” in media reports) dancing to the song “Y.M.C.A.” – a song closely associated with former U.S. President Donald Trump’s political rallies – went viral[1][2]. The video, posted around the time of Trump’s 2024 election victory, led many fans to allege that Grupo Frontera was celebrating or endorsing Trump’s return to the presidency. Given Trump’s hardline anti-immigration rhetoric and policies, the notion of a popular Mexican American band appearing to support him sparked immediate outrage on social media[3]. In the ensuing fallout, the band faced calls for boycott, a petition to remove them from a major Latino music festival, and widespread criticism from portions of their fanbase. Grupo Frontera ultimately issued public statements denying any political affiliation against the immigrant community and affirming their support for their heritage and fans[4][5]. The incident and its aftermath highlighted deep political polarization among Latino audiences in the United States, as well as the challenges for cultural figures caught in politically charged controversies[6].
## Background
Grupo Frontera is a Mexican American band from the Rio Grande Valley region of Texas, known for its blend of regional Mexican (norteño and cumbia) music. The band’s name “Frontera,” meaning “border” in Spanish, reflects their roots near the U.S.–Mexico border. Since their formation in 2022, Grupo Frontera quickly rose to fame with viral hits like “No Se Va” and high-profile collaborations (including with artists such as Bad Bunny and Peso Pluma), culminating in a Latin Grammy win in 2024[7][8]. Despite their binational fanbase and the political connotations of their name, the members had generally avoided political topics publicly. In a January 2025 interview, drummer Juan Javier Cantú noted that “we rarely get involved in politics. It’s very controversial for us and we just want to make music”, while emphasizing the group’s support for immigrant communities through charitable acts[9]. This apolitical stance set the stage for the surprise and conflict that followed the viral video incident.
## TikTok video and allegations of Trump support
In late 2024, shortly after the U.S. presidential election, a TikTok video began circulating which featured a relative of lead singer Adelaido “Payo” Solís (widely referred to as “La Abuela Frontera”) performing a dance to the Village People’s 1978 hit song “Y.M.C.A.”[1]. The song “Y.M.C.A.” had been famously used as an anthem at Donald Trump’s campaign rallies, where Trump himself often danced to it on stage. Because of this strong association, many viewers interpreted the family member’s celebratory dance as an implicit endorsement of Trump by Grupo Frontera or its inner circle[3]. The timing of the video – apparently posted when it became clear that Trump had won the 2024 election – reinforced this perception among fans that it was “celebrating his election victory”[2].
Although the TikTok clip itself did not contain any explicit political statements or slogans, its content alarmed a significant segment of the band’s fanbase[10]. Followers on social media accused Grupo Frontera of “sympathizing with the former president” and betraying their community[11]. Trump had been a polarizing figure in the Latino community due to his past comments about Mexican immigrants and plans for strict immigration enforcement. As a result, seeing a member of the band’s family dancing to a Trump-linked song was enough for some to conclude that the group was aligning with a politician “voicing hatred openly towards the Hispanic community”, as one disappointed fan put it[12]. Within days, comment sections on the band’s posts were flooded with angry messages, including remarks like “In Mexico we don’t forgive” and calls to “stop supporting a group that is anti-Mexican”[13]. Some sarcastically suggested the band should “start singing in English” if they were supporting Trump’s agenda[13].
## Fan backlash and festival petition
The backlash quickly moved beyond comments and tweets. On February 6, 2025, an online petition was launched on Change.org urging organizers of the Sueños Music Festival in Chicago to drop Grupo Frontera from the event’s lineup[14]. (Grupo Frontera had been announced as one of the headliners for the 2025 Sueños festival, a large Latin music festival scheduled for May in Grant Park.) The petition argued that a band perceived as supporting Donald Trump should not be elevated at a Latino-centered festival, invoking Trump’s plans to deport undocumented immigrants as antithetical to the spirit of the event[3]. The campaign gained traction rapidly: within a few days it amassed over 9,700 signatures from fans and community members supporting the call to “Cancel Grupo Frontera” at Sueños[14]. By the following week, local media were reporting on the petition and the controversy surrounding the band’s political stance.
Individual fans voiced a mix of anger and disappointment. “Sueños Festival is a time to celebrate our culture and heritage. Hate has no place at this event. Cancel Grupo Frontera,” wrote one petitioner in a comment on the petition site[15]. Another supporter of the petition wrote, “They are making money off the Mexican people, yet they support a man who has been openly voicing his hatred towards the Hispanic community since 2016.”[12] A Chicago man who started the petition (identified by the alias “Espíritu”) said he had been a fan of Grupo Frontera and had spent hundreds of dollars on festival tickets, but now felt betrayed and wanted a refund[16]. He and others questioned the authenticity of the band’s eventual apology post (since it was only up briefly as a story), expressing skepticism about whether the band genuinely disavowed the political message[17]. The controversy even prompted a response from the festival organizers: one of Sueños’ co-founders posted a message to the “Latino community” on Instagram, stressing that the festival was created to “celebrate, uplift and bring joy” to Latinos and noting that neither he nor his co-founder supported Trump[18]. Notably, this statement did not mention Grupo Frontera by name, but it was widely seen as an attempt to calm the uproar without directly intervening in the band’s scheduled appearance.
## Band’s response and clarifications
Facing the growing furor, Grupo Frontera broke their silence to address the situation. On February 7, 2025, the band issued a statement via Instagram Stories explicitly denying the political inferences being made. In this first public response, the group wrote: “We want to clarify that Grupo Frontera has no affiliation or alliance with any political party that’s against immigrants and the Latino community”[4]. The statement went on to emphasize the band members’ own immigrant roots and loyalty to their fanbase: “Like many of you, our families and [group] members have fought and struggled for a better future, and we will always take our people’s side, defending our roots and values.”[5] Additionally, Grupo Frontera made it clear that the controversial TikTok did not speak for them as a band. “It’s important you know that the opinions of our friends and family don’t represent Grupo Frontera,” the message stressed. “We are immigrants, we are from the border, and Grupo Frontera will always be by and for the people.”[19] This was an implicit reference to the role of a family member (the “abuela”) in the video, distancing the group’s official position from any relative’s personal actions. Notably, the band’s statement did not mention Donald Trump by name, focusing instead on core principles and values.
Despite this initial clarification, the band continued to navigate a tense public relations situation throughout February 2025. In a high-profile moment on February 20, Grupo Frontera attended the Premio Lo Nuestro music awards in Miami. On the red carpet, television host Raúl De Molina directly asked the members a politically charged question: given that their name means “border,” what did they think about the situation at the U.S.-Mexico border?[20] One of the band’s percussionists, Julián Peña Jr., answered diplomatically, “We dedicate ourselves to music. We love everyone and want peace and love. Take care of yourselves.”[21] The group clearly intended to steer away from political commentary. However, this non-committal answer drew its own backlash. Prominent immigration activist Carlos Eduardo Espina (a social media personality with millions of followers) lambasted the response on TikTok, calling it “one of the worst answers… ever seen to a question” and suggesting that saying nothing was itself a telling stance[22]. The band suddenly found itself criticized not only for an alleged pro-Trump lean, but also for appearing evasive when pressed on immigrant issues – a sign of how difficult it was for them to appease all sides.
In light of the continued criticism, Grupo Frontera decided to make a more direct and comprehensive public statement. On February 22, 2025, the six-member band released a video message on Instagram, in which each member took turns addressing the controversy point by point[23][24]. Speaking in Spanish directly to their followers, they unequivocally aligned themselves with immigrant communities and denied supporting any anti-immigrant politics. “We are migrants; our parents are migrants. And we want to tell you directly that we don’t support any political party that is against migrants,” Julián Peña Jr. said at the start of the video[23]. Another member, Alberto Acosta, condemned the “false news” he saw circulating about the band, saying that rumors were unfairly “putting us against our raza (people)”[25]. To dispel one of the central misconceptions, accordionist Juan Javier Cantú addressed the infamous “Y.M.C.A.” dance. He explained that during their recent tour, Grupo Frontera often did a light-hearted dance routine to various songs (including “Y.M.C.A.”) as a pre-show warmup, noting “we’ve danced to that song since primary school and it had nothing to do with any political party”[26]. This was meant to clarify that the band’s use of the song was based on nostalgia and fun rather than any endorsement of its political connotations.
Lead vocalist Payo Solís then spoke about the TikTok video featuring his grandmother dancing. Without naming his grandmother or Trump explicitly, Solís reminded viewers that only the band members speak for Grupo Frontera: “I want to tell you all that the only opinions that count from Grupo Frontera are those that come from the group itself,” he stated. “Any person that’s not one of the six members of Grupo Frontera can’t have an opinion on our behalf, nor speak for us, nor express our emotions.”[24] By emphasizing this, Solís diplomatically repudiated his relative’s apparent celebratory post. Finally, Peña Jr. concluded the video with a reaffirmation of the group’s identity and mission: “We are migrants. We’re for migrants. We always have been… You guys know what we’ve done and have supported our raza in the U.S. and Mexico and wherever we are. That’s what this group represents, not what’s been said about us.”[27] This closing statement underscored that Grupo Frontera saw itself as an advocate for their community and wanted to put the controversy behind them by clarifying their true values.
## Aftermath and impact
Grupo Frontera’s candid February 22 video largely succeeded in quelling the worst of the backlash, and the band did not face any formal “cancellation.” The organizers of Sueños Music Festival kept Grupo Frontera on the 2025 lineup as planned. When the festival took place in Chicago in May 2025, the band performed their headlining set to a packed crowd at Grant Park[28]. On May 25, 2025, Grupo Frontera took the stage dressed in black western wear and delivered a high-energy show, indicating that many fans continued to support them despite the prior controversy[29][30]. Reporters on site noted the lawn was filled with attendees in cowboy hats singing along to the group’s hits, and even gifts (such as flowers) were handed to the lead singer from the adoring audience[28]. This positive reception suggested that the group’s popularity endured. However, not everyone was appeased: even on the festival weekend, a Sueños fan community page on Facebook still posted denunciations of the band’s participation[31], showing that a segment of fans remained critical.
Media observers have pointed to the Grupo Frontera incident as illustrative of larger divisions among Latino Americans in the political realm[6]. Latino voters in recent years have shown a range of views, with some increasing support for Trump and others strongly opposing him[32]. The clash over Grupo Frontera – a band of young Mexican Americans who symbolize cultural pride – being linked (rightly or wrongly) to Trump’s movement became a flashpoint in this divide. Some commentators noted that attempts to associate beloved cultural figures with partisan politics can backfire and “generate adverse reactions” in a polarized community[6]. While a portion of Trump supporters may have welcomed the video as a “favorable nod” from a popular band, the harsh backlash from many Latino fans demonstrated a powerful counter-reaction[6].
By the end of 2025, the “Grupo Frontera political controversy” had largely settled down, and the band continued its musical career without further political incidents. In retrospect, the controversy underscored the careful balance that artists with diverse fanbases must maintain in politically charged times. Grupo Frontera’s experience shows how even an ambiguous social media post can erupt into a major public controversy – especially when it touches on sensitive issues of identity, immigration, and politics – and how swift, transparent communication is often required to address fans’ concerns. The band’s public avowal of their pro-immigrant stance and neutral political position helped restore their reputation for many, even as debate persists in the community about the broader implications of the episode.
