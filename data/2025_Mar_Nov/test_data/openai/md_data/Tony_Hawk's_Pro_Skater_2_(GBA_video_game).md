# Tony Hawk’s Pro Skater 2 (GBA video game)
Tony Hawk’s Pro Skater 2 is a 2001 skateboarding video game developed by Vicarious Visions and published by Activision for the Game Boy Advance[1]. It is a handheld port of the second game in the Tony Hawk’s Pro Skater series, released as a launch title for the GBA in June 2001[2]. The game recreates the core gameplay of the PlayStation original (career mode, timed runs, and challenge goals) on the portable system by using an isometric 3D viewpoint with real-time polygonal skater models[3][4]. It includes all of the console game’s stages and objectives (collecting the letters S-K-A-T-E and hidden video tapes, scoring goals, etc.), but omits the multiplayer modes and the level editor due to handheld limitations[5][6]. The licensed punk/rock soundtrack of the original is replaced by new sample-based music tracks and sound effects. The GBA version was critically acclaimed: reviewers praised its technical achievement in bringing a true 3D Tony Hawk game to a handheld. For example, GameSpot called the port “an amazing feat” and “one of the best games available for the GBA’s launch”[7], and Nintendo World Report noted that “if you can live without the multiplayer and level editor, this GBA cart can literally and justifiably replace all other versions” of the game[5]. The game even won a BAFTA award for Best Mobile (Interactive) Game[8].
## Gameplay
The gameplay of Tony Hawk’s Pro Skater 2 (GBA) closely mirrors the console versions’ mission-based structure. Players choose from a roster of professional skaters (Tony Hawk and others) and attempt to complete a set of ten goals in each level within a two-minute time limit. These goals include high scores, tricks like manuals and flips, collecting five hidden items or letters, and finding a secret videotape in the stage. Completing goals earns money to purchase higher attributes (speed, jump, etc.) and unlocks new levels. All ten levels from the PlayStation game are included, plus one exclusive bonus stage (“Rooftops”) unique to the GBA version[9]. The scoring system and trick list are unchanged, though many button mappings had to be adjusted for the GBA’s fewer buttons (for example, the L/R shoulder buttons perform flip or grab tricks, while A and B handle jumps and grinds[10]).
Visually, the game uses a fixed isometric perspective (a tilted overhead 3/4 view) to simulate 3D. The player’s skater is rendered as a 3D polygonal model (about 300 polygons each), which animates smoothly through all tricks[4]. The environment is drawn with large 2D tiles and pseudo-3D graphics (using height-maps and Mode-7 style scaling) so that ramps, rails, and hazards form a cohesive stage. Reviewers noted that the graphics were “clear and detailed” and that the skaters’ 3D animations look “incredible” for a handheld[11][4]. Controls are mostly responsive, though players must adapt to limited screen visibility (obstacles can occlude items, requiring use of shadows to judge depth)[12].
Notable features from the console game that were removed include the two-player competitive modes and the park creator, which were omitted due to hardware and cartridge size limits[5][6]. All other core modes are present: a single-session mode, free skate, and the main career mode with its ten objectives per stage[13][5]. The absence of the editor and wireless play was a common comment, but critics agreed that the remaining content was still very deep for a handheld game.
## Development
Porting Tony Hawk’s Pro Skater 2 to the GBA was an ambitious technical challenge. Vicarious Visions (known for other handheld ports) decided early on not to make a simple side-scrolling version. Instead, they settled on an isometric viewpoint and reused much of the console game’s logic. According to the studio, they adapted the original Neversoft code where possible, and built a custom engine using math-based height maps for the geometry[14][4]. The skater models were rendered with a lightweight real-time 3D engine: the team prototyped a Gouraud-shaded polygon renderer that could fit in a 64×64 sprite, allowing the skater to spin and turn freely[15][4].
The choice to use polygonal characters (instead of 2D sprites) meant the game did not have thousands of pre-rendered frames; instead, it calculates animations on the fly. This design resulted in skaters that move fluidly and resemble miniature versions of the console models[4]. The static isometric view (with objects drawn on a grid) allowed the game to use tile-based backgrounds and preserve most level layouts from the original. In development interviews, the team noted that nearly all level objectives and assets had to be reconstructed within this new system[14][4].
For audio, licensed tracks were not possible on GBA, so the composers at Shin’en Multimedia (using the GAX sound engine) created a new synthesized soundtrack. Reviewers found the music to be serviceable: GameSpot called it a “pretty solid sample-based digitized soundtrack” that replaces the console’s licensed songs[16]. Many original sound effects (skate noises, etc.) were sampled from the PlayStation version to maintain authenticity[17].
Development of the port began in mid-2000, shortly after Vicarious Visions saw early hardware demos of the GBA. The studio even created mock-up art to pitch the idea at E3 2000. After securing the license, the team worked intensively to make a faithful handheld version. According to retrospective accounts, the GBA title pushed the system’s limits and led to new techniques (later used in other Vicarious ports) to achieve 3D graphics on the console[2][15].
## Release
Tony Hawk’s Pro Skater 2 for GBA was released in North America on June 11, 2001, as a launch title for the new handheld[2]. It followed shortly in Europe on June 22, 2001[2]. In Japan, the game was published by Success under the title SK8: Tony Hawk’s Pro Skater 2 and released in December 2001[18]. This Japanese edition added three exclusive local skaters in place of some of the Western unlockable characters[18].
The game was bundled with a wrist strap and promotional materials highlighting its technical achievement. It later appeared on various Nintendo eShop sales compilations, but has not been ported to newer systems apart from fan emulation.
## Reception
Critics generally gave Tony Hawk’s Pro Skater 2 (GBA) very high marks, often calling it a breakthrough for handheld gaming. On review aggregator Metacritic, it holds a score in the mid-80s (based on dozens of reviews). Outlets praised the game’s depth and how well it replicated the console experience. GameSpot’s Jeff Gerstmann scored it 9.3/10, applauding that the developers “captured the essence” of the original and calling the handheld title “nothing short of the best portable skateboarding game ever made”[3]. He noted the game’s “absolutely amazing graphics” and deemed it “an amazing feat” for the hardware[11][7]. Nintendo World Report also gave it 9/10, commenting that apart from the missing modes, the GBA cart could “literally and justifiably replace all other versions”[5].
GameCritics awarded an 8.5/10, calling it a “monumental achievement” and praising Vicarious Visions for “remaking almost from scratch” to bring Tony Hawk’s Pro Skater 2 to GBA[19][6]. The accuracy of the adaptation – including all skaters and tricks – drew particular acclaim[13]. Some reviews mentioned the steep learning curve and awkward camera, but these were minor complaints in light of the overall quality.
In summary, Tony Hawk’s Pro Skater 2 on GBA is often cited as one of the high points of early GBA software. It won numerous awards (including a BAFTA for Best Mobile Interactive Game[8]) and is remembered as a standout launch title that proved the GBA could handle complex 3D games.
