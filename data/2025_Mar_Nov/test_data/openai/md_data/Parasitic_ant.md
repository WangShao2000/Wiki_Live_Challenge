# Parasitic ant
Parasitic ants are species that exploit the social structure of other ant colonies for their own survival and reproduction. In these species, one ant queen invades a host colony of another species and uses its workers or resources to rear her offspring【1】. Only a small fraction of ants are parasitic – on the order of a few hundred out of over 12,000 described species【1】 – but these parasites have evolved a variety of specialized behaviors. They include temporary social parasites (queens that infiltrate a host colony only when founding a new nest), permanent inquilines (species that live alongside the host queen indefinitely, often without producing workers), and slave-making (dulotic) ants (which conduct raids to steal brood and use the captured larvae as a worker force). In all cases, parasitic ants must infiltrate a host colony, tolerate or eliminate the host queen, and have the host workers raise their brood【1】. Notable examples of parasitic ants include the Amazon ants (Polyergus spp.) that raid Formica colonies, and Lasius parasites such as L. umbratus and L. orientalis that infiltrate nests of other Lasius species. Recent research has even documented extreme strategies: for example, queens of Lasius orientalis spray host queens with formic acid, chemically deceiving the host workers into killing their own queen【2】【3】.
## Forms of parasitism
Ant social parasites fall into three main categories. Temporary social parasites found only during colony founding. A newly mated parasite queen invades a host colony (typically by killing or displacing the host queen) and has the host workers immediately begin raising her brood【1】. Over time the original host workers die out, leaving a colony composed entirely of the parasitic species. Permanent social parasites (inquilines) coexist with the host queen indefinitely. Inquilines generally do not produce any worker offspring; instead they live in the host nest, cohabiting peacefully with the host queen while the host workers care for both queens’ sexual brood【1】. Many inquiline queens have lost normal worker traits – for example, some have concave gasters and specialized claws to cling onto the host queen【1】 – because they rely entirely on the host colony’s labor. Finally, slave-making (dulotic) ants combine temporary parasitism with aggressive raids. Like temporary parasites, a slave-maker queen invades a host nest to start the colony, but thereafter workers of the parasite repeatedly raid neighboring host colonies to steal pupae【1】【3】. The abducted brood then matures into workers (often chemically imprinted to the parasite) that help run the slave-maker’s colony. In slave-maker colonies, workers of the enslaver species comprise the bulk of the workforce【1】【3】. For example, the slavemaking Polyergus ants raid Formica nests and bring back pupae; the emerging Formica workers perform all the colony labor for the Polyergus colony【1】. Facultative slavemakers (e.g. Formica subnuda) may only occasionally raid small host nests and often lack slaves, while obligate slavemakers (e.g. Polyergus breviceps) conduct large-scale raids and have slave workers make up over 90% of their colony【1】.
## Colony founding and host takeover
Parasitic ant queens face the challenge of infiltrating a host colony’s defenses. Ant colonies use elaborate chemical recognition (cuticular hydrocarbons) to detect intruders, so parasite queens use a mix of stealth and aggression to gain acceptance【1】. Some parasite queens invade by force, fighting host workers or killing the host queen outright. Others acquire the host colony’s scent by rubbing against workers or brood (chemical camouflage), or secrete appeasement pheromones that pacify the hosts【1】. Once inside, a parasitic queen almost always eliminates reproductive competition. Except for many inquilines, the parasite queen kills or ousts the host queen during or soon after invasion【1】. In some cases the takeover is indirect: for example, Lasius orientalis queens coat the host queen with a high concentration of formic acid. The host workers perceive the scent as a danger signal and become agitated; they eventually mutilate and kill their own queen, clearing the way for the parasite【2】【3】. After the host queen is gone, the parasite queen assumes her role and lays eggs that the workers (now without their mother) rear as their own brood.
## Diversity and evolution
Only a minority of ant species are social parasites. In fact, about 230 parasitic ant species are known among roughly 12,500 described species【1】. Parasitism is especially common in the ant subfamilies Formicinae and Myrmicinae, and is more frequently found in temperate regions than in the tropics【1】. Famous parasitic genera include Formica, Polyergus, Rossomyrmex, Lasius, Myrmica, and Temnothorax; each of these has multiple species that exploit close relatives as hosts【1】【3】. Notably, parasitic ants often are closely related to their hosts – a pattern called Emery’s rule – suggesting that social parasites typically evolve from ancestors similar to their host species【1】.
Because parasitic ants depend on host colonies, their evolution is closely tied to host defenses. Hosts may evolve stronger nestmate recognition or aggressive responses to intruders. In one example of coevolution, researchers found that geographic differences in climate led Temnothorax longispinosus hosts to develop stronger nest defense in cool regions, while their parasite Temnothorax americanus became more aggressive in warm regions【3】. Such arms races underscore the complex interplay between host and parasite in ant societies.
Parasitic ants remain a subject of active research, especially the chemical and behavioral mechanisms they use to manipulate hosts. For instance, studies of the newly reported Lasius parasites (e.g. L. orientalis and L. umbratus) revealed that both evolved independently the ability to induce host-worker matricide via chemical signals【2】. These discoveries highlight the striking adaptations of parasitic ants and their significance in understanding social evolution in insects.
