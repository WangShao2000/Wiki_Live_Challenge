# Belmont Glass Works
Belmont Glass Works (also known as the Belmont Glass Company) was an early glass manufacturer in Bellaire, Ohio, United States, that operated from 1866 until 1890[1][2]. It was the first glass factory in Bellaire – a town that would later be nicknamed the "Glass City" for its prolific glass industry[3]. Founded by a group of glassmakers with experience at the J. H. Hobbs, Brockunier glassworks in nearby Wheeling, West Virginia, the company initially made blown glass products such as lantern chimneys, lamps, and barware[4]. It later expanded into pressed glass tableware and decorative items, becoming known for intricate patterns like its elaborate “Daisy & Button” design (Pattern No. 100)[5]. Belmont Glass Works also introduced colored glass (including “vaseline” uranium glass) by 1885, adding tinted wares to its production line[6]. The company ceased operations in 1890 after years of financial difficulty, amid a changing market where many glass manufacturers were relocating to areas with cheaper fuel and other incentives[7]. Despite its closure, Belmont Glass Works left a significant legacy in the American glass industry, having been the cornerstone of Bellaire’s glassmaking era and a training ground for glassworkers who later founded other glass factories[8].
## History
Bellaire’s location in the Ohio Valley made it a favorable site for glass production in the mid-19th century. The town sat along the Ohio River and major rail lines and was near abundant coal resources, providing easy transportation and fuel for glassmaking furnaces[9]. In 1865, a group of investors and experienced glassworkers – including Henry Faupel and John Robinson – organized a new glass company in Bellaire, originally named Barnes, Faupel & Co. Operations began in 1866 at a plant near 34th and Hamilton Streets, making it the first glassworks in the city[10]. Within a couple of years the enterprise was formally incorporated as the Belmont Glass Works, taking its name from Belmont County, Ohio, where Bellaire is located[2]. Many of the founders had honed their craft at the Hobbs, Brockunier & Co. glass factory in Wheeling, and they brought that expertise to the new venture[11]. Early production focused on oil lamp chimneys (the glass globes for lanterns) and other blown-glass utilitarian wares like bottles and bar goods[4].
As the factory grew, it quickly broadened its product range. By the 1870s, Belmont Glass Works was producing a variety of pressed glass tableware and kerosene lamp components in addition to its original blown wares[4]. The company built its own iron foundry on-site to manufacture glass molds as early as 1870, which was an unusual capability at the time[12]. Belmont not only made molds for its own use but also chipped (hand-cut) and supplied molds to other glass manufacturers, including well-known firms such as McKee, Central Glass, and Fostoria Glass[12]. This technical self-sufficiency allowed Belmont to develop unique designs and respond quickly to market demands.
Throughout the 1870s and 1880s, the Belmont Glass Works was a central part of Bellaire’s emergence as a glass production hub. In 1876, two of Belmont’s glassmakers – Charles Henry Over (the factory’s manager) and John Robinson – left to establish the Bellaire Goblet Company, another tableware glass factory in the city[13][14]. The proliferation of such offshoot enterprises earned Bellaire its reputation as “Glass City.” By 1880, Belmont County ranked among the top glass-producing regions in the nation[8]. Belmont Glass Works itself grew to employ several hundred workers by the mid-1880s, including men, women, and youths in its cutting shops and hot-glass departments. In 1884, industry directories list Harvey Leighton as the plant manager, overseeing a workforce that had expanded significantly since the early years[15]. That same year, Charles H. Tallman became company president, reflecting a transition in leadership as the original founders aged or pursued other ventures (Henry Faupel, the long-time president, died in 1889)[16].
Belmont Glass Works continued to modernize its offerings during the 1880s. In 1885, the factory began producing colored glass on a commercial scale[6]. Previously, most Bellaire glass had been clear or aqua; the introduction of rich colors (including a yellow-green uranium glass known as “canary” or vaseline glass) allowed Belmont to cater to new decorative trends[17]. The company’s pressed glass tableware lines grew increasingly ornate during this period, showcasing the skill of its mold-makers and glass pressers. However, the late 1880s also brought economic challenges. A national surplus of glassware led to falling prices, and newer glass plants in northwest Ohio (in cities like Findlay) lured businesses with offers of free natural gas, land, and cash subsidies[7]. Belmont Glass Works, still reliant on coal fuel and facing rising labor costs, struggled to remain profitable in this changing environment[7].
In June 1890, after nearly 25 years of operation, the shareholders of Belmont Glass Works voted to liquidate the company’s assets and close the factory[18]. The decision came amid mounting financial losses, as the firm could no longer compete effectively with larger conglomerates and the migration of glass manufacturing to gas-rich regions[7]. The plant shut down that summer, and its remaining inventory and equipment were sold off. Belmont’s valuable collection of cast-iron molds was divided among other glassmakers: local rivals like the Crystal Glass Company of Bridgeport, Ohio and the Central Glass Company acquired many of the patterns[18]. Some Belmont molds even found their way to McKee & Brothers of Pittsburgh and to the Diamond Glass Company of Montreal, Canada, where they were reused to produce similar pressed glass pieces in subsequent years[19]. By 1893, the old Belmont Glass Works buildings in Bellaire had been demolished to make room for a new Novelty Stamping Company factory on the site[18].
## Products and Patterns
Belmont Glass Works manufactured a wide array of glass products, evolving with the technology and tastes of the Victorian era. Its initial output of lamp chimneys and plain lantern globes expanded into a broad catalogue of decorative tableware, barware, lighting fixtures, and household goods. The company was especially noted for its pressed glass dinnerware and novelties, which were sold across the United States. Belmont’s pressed glass was made by pouring molten glass into molds and pressing it to imprint patterns, a technique that enabled mass-production of ornate designs at low cost. The firm’s in-house mold department produced numerous proprietary patterns identified by number.
One of Belmont’s most celebrated designs was the “Daisy and Button” pattern (catalogued as Pattern No. 100)[5]. This pattern features an intricate motif of interlocking daisies and geometric buttons, covering the surface of items like covered compotes, butter dishes, and oil lamps. Belmont’s version of Daisy & Button was especially elaborate – described as perhaps “the most ornate Daisy & Button pattern made by any company”[5] – and it was produced in various colors. Collectors note that Belmont made Daisy & Button pieces in clear glass as well as in amber, emerald green, and the distinctive canary-yellow vaseline glass which glows under ultraviolet light[5]. The complexity of these pieces often required them to be molded in multiple sections and then fused together, demonstrating the high level of craftsmanship at Belmont’s factory[20][21].
In addition to Daisy & Button, Belmont Glass Works created many other patterns that appealed to late 19th-century consumers. For example, the factory produced “Zipper” pattern tableware – a design with repeating V-shaped wedge cuts resembling a zipper – which was popular in the 1880s and is now prized by antique glass collectors[22]. The company’s product line included goblets, tumblers, decanters, bottles, pitchers, lamps, and assorted dishes (such as butter dishes, pickle castors, and sugar bowls), often decorated with fluted, faceted, or hobnail-like patterns. Belmont also made press-molded lamp bases and fonts, some of which were then fitted with metal hardware to create finished kerosene lamps. By diversifying into colored glass, Belmont introduced blue, amber, and vaseline hues to some of these patterns after 1885, adding visual variety to its catalog[6]. The quality and detail of Belmont’s pressed glass ensured that many of its patterns continued to be manufactured (by other companies using Belmont’s molds) even after the original firm closed[19]. Today, authentic Belmont pieces – identifiable by their patterns and certain mold marks – are sought after by collectors of Early American Pattern Glass.
## Legacy
Although the Belmont Glass Works shut its doors in 1890, its influence persisted in the American glass industry. The company is credited as the catalyst for Bellaire’s glassmaking boom: it was Bellaire’s first glassworks and proved that quality glass could be produced in the region, thereby attracting additional glass factories to the city in the late 19th century[8]. During Bellaire’s “Glass City” heyday (circa 1870–1890), as many as six or seven glass plants operated there simultaneously, and Belmont County rose to become one of the leading glass manufacturing centers in the United States (ranked sixth nationally by 1880)[8]. Belmont Glass Works played a major role in developing the skilled workforce that fueled this industry.
Many glassmakers who trained or worked at Belmont went on to establish or manage other important glass companies. For instance, veterans of Belmont helped found the Bellaire Goblet Company in 1876, which became a prominent goblet and tableware producer[14]. After Belmont’s closure, its former plant superintendent Henry Crimmel partnered in launching the Novelty Glass Company in Fostoria, Ohio (taking some of Belmont’s talent and techniques to the new natural-gas glass region)[23][24]. Others from Belmont’s ranks joined national firms or the U.S. Glass Company combine in the 1890s. In this way, the knowledge and craftsmanship fostered at Belmont Glass Works continued to spread. The Daisy & Button and Zipper patterns, among others, remained in production through other glasshouses that acquired Belmont’s molds, contributing to the enduring popularity of those designs[19].
Today, Belmont Glass Works is remembered by glass historians and collectors as a pioneering Ohio glass manufacturer that bridged the period between hand-blown traditional glass and the golden age of pressed glass. Examples of Belmont glassware can be seen in museum collections and antique shops, often admired for their intricate detail and historical significance. An Ohio historical marker in Bellaire commemorates the city’s glass industry heritage – a legacy that began with the Belmont Glass Works in 1866 and helped earn Bellaire the title of “Glass City”[3].
