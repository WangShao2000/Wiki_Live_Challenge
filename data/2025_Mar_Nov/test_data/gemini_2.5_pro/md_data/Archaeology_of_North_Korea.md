# Archaeology of the Democratic People's Republic of Korea: Historiography, Material Culture, and State Ideology
## 1\. Introduction: The Intersection of Excavation and Ideology
The archaeology of the Democratic People's Republic of Korea (DPRK) constitutes a distinct and insular tradition within East Asian scholarship. Unlike the archaeological practices observed in much of the contemporary world, where the primary objective is often the reconstruction of the past through empirical data and cross-cultural comparison, the discipline in North Korea is intrinsically linked to the validation of the state's official historiography and the Juche ideology. This report provides an exhaustive analysis of the discipline's history, its methodological frameworks, and its key findings across the Paleolithic, Neolithic, Bronze Age (Gojoseon), Goguryeo, and Koryo periods.

The development of archaeology in North Korea cannot be understood apart from the political context of the post-Korean War era. The state's need to establish legitimacy, both domestically and in competition with South Korea, drove a historiographical shift toward emphasizing the antiquity, continuity, and ethnic homogeneity of the Korean people. This has resulted in a unique theoretical landscape where archaeological data is interpreted to support narratives of independent cultural evolution and the centrality of Pyongyang in the development of East Asian civilization.

Key to this interpretive framework is the rejection of diffusionist theories—which attribute cultural changes to external migrations or influences—in favor of indigenous development models. This report will explore how this "Juche archaeology" manifests in the interpretation of specific sites, from the contested dating of the Komunmoru Paleolithic site to the reconstruction of the Tomb of King Tangun and the denial of the Han Commandery of Lelang. Furthermore, it will examine the notable achievements of North Korean archaeology, particularly the preservation and study of the Goguryeo mural tombs, which have garnered international recognition through UNESCO.

## 2\. Historical Development and Institutional Framework
The trajectory of North Korean archaeology has been shaped by the interplay between the legacy of colonial-era scholarship, the influence of Soviet methodologies, and the eventual dominance of indigenous nationalist ideology.

### 2.1 Pre-Liberation Foundations and the Colonial Legacy
Prior to the introduction of modern archaeological practices in the late 19th century, Korea did not possess a systemic tradition of archaeology; early antiquarian texts were largely uninterested in prehistory. The introduction of the discipline coincided with the expanding influence of the Empire of Japan. During the colonial period (1910–1945), Japanese archaeologists conducted extensive surveys of the peninsula. However, their motivations were often rooted in imperialist objectives, seeking evidence of ancestral links between the Yamato people and ancient Koreans to justify colonial rule, or simply engaging in treasure hunting that led to the looting of significant sites[1] .

Despite this colonial imposition, a generation of Korean scholars emerged who sought to reclaim the national past. Following the division of the peninsula after World War II, the foundations of North Korean archaeology were laid by scholars who had been educated in Europe and Japan, bringing with them rigorous methodological training. Two figures stand out in this formative period: To Yu-ho and Han Hung-su.

To Yu-ho (1905–1982), often revered in early narratives as the "father of North Korean archaeology," studied in Vienna. His European education provided him with a sophisticated understanding of stratigraphic excavation and typological analysis, which he sought to apply to the Korean context[2] . Similarly, Han Hung-su (1909–?), who had spent twelve years in Central Europe (1936–1948) studying archaeology and ethnography in Vienna, Bern, and Freiburg, played a pivotal role. Han had previously organized excavations of dolmen sites during the colonial era and was deeply influenced by the nationalist currents he encountered in Europe, as well as the preservationist ethics of his Czech and Austrian colleagues[3] . Upon his return to North Korea via Moscow, Han taught at Kim Il Sung University and helped establish the early academic infrastructure of the state[2] .

### 2.2 The Post-War Institutionalization
The formal institutionalization of the discipline occurred rapidly after the Korean War. On December 1, 1952, the Academy of Sciences (later the Academy of Social Sciences for humanities disciplines) was established, housing the Institute of Archaeology[4] . This centralized body became, and remains, the primary organization responsible for conducting excavations, publishing research, and managing cultural heritage[1] .

In the early post-war period (1950s–early 1960s), North Korean archaeology was heavily influenced by Soviet advisors. Figures like Terenty Shtykov and Nikolai Lebedev advised the North Korean leadership on implementing a Stalinist regime, which extended to the cultural sphere[6] . The curriculum and research focus initially mirrored Soviet models, emphasizing materialist history and class struggle. However, the seeds of a distinct national approach were already being sown.

### 2.3 The Juche Turn and the Purges
A profound transformation occurred in the mid-1960s as Kim Il Sung consolidated his power and the Juche (self-reliance) ideology became the paramount guiding principle of the state. The academic community faced intense pressure to align with the new political orthodoxy, which increasingly viewed dependence on foreign ideas—whether Western or Soviet—as a threat to national sovereignty[7] .

This ideological shift had severe consequences for the founding generation of archaeologists. Kim Il Sung criticized academics for being too influenced by "bourgeois thought" and "flunkeyism" (subservience to great powers). Consequently, To Yu-ho was purged in 1965 and removed from influence. By 1967, almost all academic work was temporarily halted as the state reorganized its intellectual resources to serve the Juche ideology exclusively[1] . Han Hung-su’s fate remains largely obscure in North Korean records, a common outcome for intellectuals of that era who had foreign ties[2] .

When archaeology re-emerged in the 1970s, it was fundamentally transformed. The new "Juche archaeology" was tasked with providing material evidence for three core tenets:

1. **Chaju (Independence):** The Korean people developed their culture and civilization independently, rejecting interpretations that emphasized Chinese influence or diffusion from the continent[1] .  
2. **Continuity and Antiquity:** The Korean Peninsula has been continuously inhabited by the same ethnic group (the Korean Minjok) since the Paleolithic era[8] .  
3. **Centrality:** Pyongyang and the Taedong River basin were the center of this civilization, serving as one of the cradles of humankind[9] .

### 2.4 Modern Institutional Structure
Today, the Institute of Archaeology under the Academy of Social Sciences and the Faculty of History at Kim Il Sung University remain the primary engines of archaeological research[1] . These institutions orchestrate all excavation projects, ensuring that findings are interpreted within the approved ideological framework. They publish academic journals such as *Choson Kogo Yongu* (Archaeological Study of Korea) and reports like *Palaeolithic Sites of Northern Korea* (2009)[1] .

The educational system reinforces this narrative. Since the implementation of the "First Comprehensive 12-Year Compulsory Education Regulation" in 2013, middle schools exclusively teach the history of "orthodox states," effectively removing any historical ambiguity and widening the historiographical gap between North and South Korea[10] . Prominent modern archaeologists, such as Seo Guktae (Neolithic/Bronze specialist) and Choi Seungtaek (Goguryeo specialist), continue to work within these parameters, producing scholarship that is technically detailed yet ideologically rigid[1] .

## 3\. The Paleolithic Period: Constructing the "Origins"
The investigation of the Paleolithic period is not merely a scientific endeavor in North Korea; it is a "national enterprise" of the highest political importance. The regime views the presence of deep human antiquity on the peninsula as essential for establishing the legitimacy of the Korean nation. Kim Jong Il explicitly instructed that "the presence of the Paleolithic period in Korea is far beyond a simple academic matter; it is a very important matter which is directly related to our nation's perpetual history, in particular, our nation's origin"[11] .

### 3.1 The Komunmoru Site and the Lower Paleolithic
The cornerstone of the North Korean claim to a Lower Paleolithic presence is the Komunmoru (Black Anvil) site, located in Hugu-ri, Sangwon County, near Pyongyang. Excavated in 1966, this cave site yielded stone tools and fossilized animal remains that North Korean scholars argue date back approximately one million years[9] .

Lithic Assemblage and Typology:  
The stone tools recovered from Komunmoru are described as heavy, crude implements made of siliceous limestone and vein quartz. The assemblage includes large flake tools, heavy points, trapezoidal tools, and choppers[13] . North Korean typologists emphasize that these tools were produced by a "hurling technique" followed by direct percussion to form cutting edges, with little secondary retouch[13] .  
Crucially, North Korean scholars argue that these tools represent a distinct technological tradition that developed independently of the Acheulean handaxe tradition found in Africa and Europe. They assert that the "knuckle axe-shaped stone implement" found at the site does not follow a standardized form like the Acheulean handaxe but is nonetheless a "typical tool of a primitive man"[14] .  
Faunal Remains and Dating:  
The dating of Komunmoru relies heavily on biostratigraphy. The cave contained the fossilized bones of 29 animal species, 17 of which are extinct[12] . The fauna includes the thick-jawed deer (Megaloceros pachyosteus) and rhinoceros, indicative of a warmer, humid climate corresponding to the early Middle Pleistocene[15] . Based on this faunal association, North Korean archaeologists date the site to roughly 1 million years ago, placing it firmly in the Lower Paleolithic[12] . This aggressive dating underpins the claim that "apemen" (Homo erectus) inhabited the Taedong River basin at the dawn of human history.

### 3.2 The Evolutionary Sequence and the "Taedong River Culture"
Building on the Komunmoru findings, North Korean archaeology posits a linear, unilineal model of human evolution occurring entirely within the Korean Peninsula. This model rejects the "Out of Africa" theory of replacement, instead favoring a multiregional evolution where the Korean people evolved locally from these early hominids.

The proposed evolutionary sequence is categorized into three stages:

| Stage | North Korean Term | Scientific Equivalent | Representative Sites/Fossils |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Apemen** | *Wonin* | *Homo erectus* | Komunmoru (1M years ago) |
| **Paleoanthropic Man** | *Goin* | Archaic *Homo sapiens* | Ryokpho Man, Tokchon Man, Hwadae Man (300k BP) |
| **Neanthropic Man** | *Shinin* | Modern *Homo sapiens* | Sungnisan Man, Mandal Man, Hwangju Man (Upper Paleolithic) |

Recent Discoveries:  
This narrative is continuously reinforced by new discoveries. In 2020, North Korean media announced the discovery of the Hangmok-ri site in Gangdong County, Pyongyang. Artifacts from this cave were scientifically dated to between 34,000 and 36,000 years ago, placing them in the Upper Paleolithic[11] . North Korean sources claim this site as further evidence of the continuous occupation of the Taedong basin during the critical transition to modern humans, linking the "Shinin" directly to the modern Korean ethnicity[11] .  
The Taedong River Culture Theory:  
In 1998, North Korean scholars formally synthesized these findings into the "Taedong River Culture" (Taedonggang Culture) theory. This theory asserts that the Taedong River basin is one of the world's five independent cradles of ancient civilization, comparable to the Nile, Tigris-Euphrates, Indus, and Yellow River civilizations[9] . This theory was politically expedient, emerging during the "Arduous March" famine and the 50th anniversary of the regime; it served to bolster national pride and internal cohesion by presenting North Korea as a culturally superior and ancient nation[17] .

## 4\. The Neolithic and Bronze Age: Gojoseon and the Myth of Tangun
The transition from the Paleolithic to the Neolithic (Jeulmun) and Bronze (Mumun) periods is framed not as a result of migration or technology transfer from China or Siberia, but as an indigenous acceleration of culture. The central focus of this period is **Gojoseon** (Ancient Korea) and its mythical founder, **Tangun**.

### 4.1 Indigenous Origins of Metallurgy and Agriculture
North Korean archaeological thought strictly denies that bronze metallurgy was introduced to the peninsula from the outside. Instead, it argues for an independent invention of bronze working in the Taedong basin during the late 4th millennium BCE[12] .  
Key artifacts supporting this view include:

* **Pipha-shaped Daggers:** The mandolin-shaped bronze dagger is considered the diagnostic artifact of the Gojoseon state. North Korean scholars map the distribution of these daggers across the Korean Peninsula and the Liaodong region of China to delineate the territorial extent of Ancient Korea[16] . The high concentration of these daggers is used to argue that the political center, Wanggeom-seong, was always located in Pyongyang[18] .  
* **Dolmens:** The Taedong basin is described as having an immense concentration of dolmens (megalithic tombs). While North Hamgyong and Ryanggang provinces have fewer (100–500), the Taedong basin is claimed to have about 14,000 dolmens, including "extra-large" types not found elsewhere[16] . This density is cited as proof of a powerful, centralized state capable of mobilizing massive labor forces.

### 4.2 The Excavation and Reconstruction of the Tomb of King Tangun
The most controversial and politically charged event in North Korean archaeology was the "discovery" of the Tomb of King Tangun. Traditionally, Tangun was regarded as a mythical figure who founded Gojoseon in 2333 BCE. However, Kim Il Sung insisted that Tangun was a real historical person and ordered archaeologists to find his remains[19] .

The Excavation (1993–1994):  
In October 1993, the Academy of Social Sciences announced that they had excavated a tomb on the slope of Mount Taebak in Kangdong County, Pyongyang. The excavation report, titled "Excavation reports of the mausoleum of Dangun," claimed the discovery of the skeletons of a male and a female[19] .  
Dating Controversy:  
To validate the antiquity of the remains, North Korean scientists used electron spin resonance (ESR) dating rather than the more conventional radiocarbon dating. They announced a date of 5011 years ago (±267 years), which conveniently placed the burial around 3000 BCE[19] . This date allowed North Korea to claim that Gojoseon was a contemporary of ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia, and that the Korean nation had a 5,000-year history as a "homogeneous nation"[20] . International scholars strongly suspect the date was manipulated to match the legendary founding date, noting that the artifacts found in the tomb were stylistically from the much later Goguryeo period[19] .  
Political Consecration:  
Following the discovery, a massive reconstruction project was launched. The site was transformed into a monumental ziggurat-style mausoleum, 22 meters high and 50 meters on each side, faced with white stone[19] . The complex includes statues of officials and mythical beasts, serving as a national shrine. Kim Il Sung provided detailed instructions on the restoration, emphasizing that the site should demonstrate the "homogeneity of the nation" and be publicized globally[20] . This project effectively canonized Tangun as the biological ancestor of the North Korean people, with the Kim leadership implicitly positioned as his modern successors.

### 4.3 The Denial of the Lelang Commandery
A critical historiographical battleground involves the **Lelang (Rakrang) Commandery**. Established by the Chinese Han Dynasty in 108 BCE after the conquest of Gojoseon, Lelang is widely accepted by international historians to have been administered from a seat near modern Pyongyang.

The Revisionist Theory:  
North Korean ideology finds the idea of a Chinese colonial administration in the heart of the "sacred" capital region unacceptable. Consequently, North Korean scholars have developed a revisionist theory that:

1. **Displacement:** The "Lelang" mentioned in Chinese chronicles was actually located in the Liaodong region of China, not on the Korean Peninsula[21] .  
2. **Indigenous Kingdom:** The rich archaeological remains in Pyongyang—such as timber-chambered tombs, lacquerware, and seals traditionally attributed to the Chinese commandery—are reinterpreted as belonging to an independent Korean kingdom called **"Rakrang" (Nakrang)**. This kingdom is said to have existed concurrently with the Chinese commandery but was politically distinct[1] .  
3. **The Census Tablets:** In the 1990s and 2000s, crucial evidence emerged in the form of wooden tablets (household registers) from a tomb in Pyongyang. These tablets, dated to 45 BCE, contain detailed census data for Lelang counties[22] . While this would seem to confirm the location of the commandery in Pyongyang, North Korean historian Son Yongjong published analyses arguing that the population statistics on the tablets prove the commandery was located elsewhere, or that the presence was merely diplomatic[22] . This "Juche-oriented" interpretation demonstrates the malleability of archaeological data when it conflicts with state imperatives[21] .

## 5\. The Goguryeo Kingdom: The Martial Ancestor
If Gojoseon provides the biological origin of the nation, the **Goguryeo Kingdom (37 BCE – 668 CE)** provides its spiritual and military template. North Korea identifies itself as the successor to Goguryeo, emphasizing the kingdom's martial prowess, its resistance against Chinese dynasties (Sui and Tang), and its control over vast territories in Manchuria[21] .

### 5.1 The Complex of Goguryeo Tombs (UNESCO World Heritage)
The most significant archaeological legacy of this period is the **Complex of Goguryeo Tombs**, inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage site in 2004\. The site comprises over 30 individual tombs, many containing exquisite wall paintings that provide a vivid window into ancient Korean life[24] .

#### 5.1.1 Anak Tomb No. 3
Discovered in 1949 in Anak, South Hwanghae Province, this tomb is one of the most structurally complex and artistically rich sites in the peninsula.

* **Architecture:** The tomb is built of stone and features an entrance, an antechamber, a main chamber, and side chambers with stone pillars, resembling an underground palace[26] .  
* **Murals:** The walls are decorated with vibrant frescoes depicting daily life. The eastern chamber features scenes of a kitchen with meat hanging on hooks, a barn, a carriage shed, and household staff[27] . A massive procession scene depicts 250 figures, including soldiers and musicians, demonstrating the occupant's high status[28] .  
* **The Inscription Controversy:** An ink inscription found in the tomb identifies the occupant as **Dong Shou**, a refugee general from the Chinese state of Former Yan who died in 357 CE. While international scholars accept this, North Korean scholars argue that Dong Shou was merely a vassal and that the tomb's grandeur implies it is the mausoleum of a Goguryeo monarch, likely **King Gogugwon** or **King Micheon**.27 This interpretation minimizes the role of "refugees" in favor of indigenous royal agency.

#### 5.1.2 Tokhung-ri Tomb
Excavated in 1976 in Nampo, this tomb is crucial for its extensive inscriptions and astronomical depictions.

* **Inscriptions:** The tomb contains over 600 Sinograms providing biographical data on the occupant, **Chin**. The inscription states he was a governor of Yuju and died in 408 CE[29] .  
* **Geopolitical Implications:** The reference to "Yuju"—a region historically covering parts of modern Beijing and Hebei—is seized upon by North Korean historians to claim that Goguryeo's administrative reach extended deep into China[31] .  
* **Art:** The ceiling features a sophisticated mural of the Milky Way and constellations, demonstrating advanced astronomical knowledge. The walls depict Chin receiving homage from 13 county governors, reinforcing the narrative of a powerful, centralized bureaucracy[31] .

#### 5.1.3 The Tomb of King Tongmyong
Located in Ryongsan-ri, Pyongyang, this is the mausoleum of Jumong (King Tongmyong), the founder of Goguryeo. The site was extensively "restored" (rebuilt) in the 1990s for political reasons[24] .

* **Significance:** The complex includes the royal tumulus and the graves of loyal vassals. Although the original tomb was looted by Japanese archaeologists in 1941, the site is revered as a patriotic shrine. The UNESCO listing recognizes the site for its "artistic elegance" and as testimony to the burial practices of the region[24] .

### 5.2 The "History War" and Conservation
The archaeology of Goguryeo is the central theater of the "history war" between Korea and China. China's "Northeast Project" (2002) attempted to frame Goguryeo as a regional ethnic minority government of ancient China. In response, North Korea accelerated its efforts to register the Goguryeo tombs with UNESCO to assert "ownership" over the history[23] .  
Conservation remains a challenge. The murals face threats from humidity and microbial growth (white mold). The UNESCO project facilitated rare technical cooperation, with international experts installing monitoring systems in tombs like Anak No. 3 and Susan-ri to permit scientific preservation[32] .

## 6\. The Koryo Period: Manwoldae and Inter-Korean Cooperation
While Goguryeo represents military strength, the **Koryo Dynasty (918–1392)** represents the first unified state on the peninsula (as North Korea rejects Unified Silla as a "incomplete" unification due to Silla's alliance with Tang China). The Koryo capital, **Kaesong**, is located just north of the Demilitarized Zone (DMZ), making it a unique venue for cross-border archaeology.

### 6.1 The Manwoldae Palace Excavations (2007–2015)
Manwoldae ("Full Moon Terrace") was the main royal palace of Koryo, burned down during the Red Turban invasions in 1361\. From 2007 to 2015, it was the site of a historic joint excavation project between North and South Korea[33] .

* **Findings:** The joint teams uncovered the palace's unique layout, which does not follow the strict north-south axis of Chinese palaces but adapts to the topography of Mount Songak. This adaptation is interpreted as an expression of "Pungsu" (geomancy) and independent aesthetic sensibility[34] . Thousands of artifacts, including cylindrical celadon ceramics and roof tiles with detailed inscriptions, were recovered[35] .  
* **Significance:** This project was a rare instance where the "shared history" of the Koryo Dynasty allowed for scientific collaboration despite political tensions.

### 6.2 The Metal Movable Type Discovery
In 2015, the joint excavation team announced a potentially world-changing discovery: a piece of metal movable type found at the Manwoldae site[36] .

* **The Claim:** North Korean and some South Korean scholars suggested this type could date to the 12th or early 13th century. If verified, this would predate the Gutenberg Bible (1455) by centuries and potentially even the *Jikji* (1377), the oldest confirmed book printed with metal type[36] .  
* **Debate:** The type was found in a disturbed layer, complicating precise dating. While the National Forensic Service in the South later assessed some related finds as potentially later reproductions or requiring more study, the discovery remains a potent symbol of Korea's claim to be the pioneer of printing technology[37] .

## 7\. Political Usage of Archaeology: Nationalism and Legitimacy
In North Korea, archaeology is never neutral. It is a tool for "Socialist Patriotism" and the consolidation of the regime's legitimacy.

### 7.1 "Kim Il Sung Nation" and Bloodline Nationalism
Since the 1980s, North Korean ideology has evolved from orthodox Marxism-Leninism to a race-based nationalism. The concept of the "Kim Il Sung Nation" defines the North Korean people not just by citizenship, but by a shared, ancient bloodline[8] . Archaeology provides the biological proof for this via the "Taedong River Culture" theory, which asserts a separate, superior evolution of the Korean race[11] . By linking the current leadership to Tangun and the "Apemen" of Komunmoru, the regime claims a mandate to rule that is 5,000 years old.

### 7.2 Anti-Colonial Narratives
Excavation reports frequently emphasize the destruction caused by Japanese "imperialist" archaeologists and tomb raiders. The narrative of "restoring" sites like the Tomb of King Tongmyong is framed as healing the wounds of colonialism and reclaiming national dignity[1] .

## 8\. Conclusion
The archaeology of North Korea is a discipline of stark contrasts. On one hand, it has produced remarkable discoveries—particularly the Goguryeo murals and the Koryo palace architecture—that are genuine treasures of world heritage. The state's investment in museums and preservation, despite economic hardships, underscores the high value placed on cultural heritage.

On the other hand, the field is constrained by the rigid requirements of state ideology. The "Taedong River Culture" theory, the aggressive dating of the Tangun Tomb, and the denial of the Lelang Commandery demonstrate how empirical data is often bent to serve the narrative of "Juche." For the international academic community, engaging with North Korean archaeology requires a careful sifting of data: valuing the descriptive richness of their excavations while critically deconstructing the chronological and interpretive frameworks imposed by the state. Nevertheless, projects like the Manwoldae excavation prove that archaeology remains one of the few viable bridges for engagement between the divided halves of the Korean Peninsula.
