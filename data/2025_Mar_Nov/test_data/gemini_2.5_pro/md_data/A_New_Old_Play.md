# A New Old Play
**A New Old Play** (Chinese: 椒麻堂会; pinyin: *Jiāomá tánghuì*) is a 2021 historical drama film written, directed, and designed by the Chinese contemporary artist and filmmaker Qiu Jiongjiong. It marks Qiu's first foray into fictional feature filmmaking, following a prolific career in experimental documentary. The film is a sprawling, epic chronicle of 20th-century China, viewed through the lens of a Sichuan opera troupe and, specifically, the life of a celebrated *chou* (clown) actor named Qiu Fu. The protagonist is based on the director's own grandfather, the renowned opera performer Qiu Fuxin, anchoring the film's vast historical sweep in personal, biographical intimacy.

Spanning over five decades, the narrative traverses the tumultuous epochs of modern Chinese history, including the Warlord Era, the Second Sino-Japanese War, the Chinese Civil War, the establishment of the People's Republic of China, the Great Leap Forward, and the Cultural Revolution. However, rather than adhering to the conventions of social realist cinema, *A New Old Play* employs a highly stylized, surrealist aesthetic. Shot entirely on a soundstage in Leshan, Sichuan, the film utilizes handcrafted sets, painted backdrops, and practical effects to create a "diorama" or "tableau vivant" visual style. This aesthetic choice reflects the film's thematic preoccupation with the interplay between life and theater, reality and artifice, suggesting that history itself is a staged performance.

The film's narrative structure is non-linear, framed by Qiu Fu's journey through the underworld (Diyu) after his death in the 1980s. As he is escorted by the guardian spirits Ox-Head and Horse-Face toward the Bridge of Helplessness to drink the Soup of Forgetfulness, Qiu Fu recounts his life story. This retrospective structure allows for a fluid movement between the ghostly, bureaucratic afterlife and the vibrant, often tragic world of the living. The film engages deeply with themes of memory, oblivion, the resilience of the artist, and the specific cultural heritage of Sichuan opera.

Premiering at the 74th Locarno Film Festival in August 2021, *A New Old Play* was awarded the Special Jury Prize, garnering widespread critical acclaim for its visual inventiveness and emotional depth. Critics have hailed it as a significant work of Chinese independent cinema, noting its "Brechtian" alienation techniques and its "Sichuan-flavored" tragicomedy. Despite its success on the international festival circuit, including awards at the Festival des 3 Continents and the Hong Kong International Film Festival, the film has faced distribution challenges within mainland China due to its sensitive historical subject matter. It has been distributed in North America by Icarus Films and in France by Hippocampe Productions.

## Plot
The narrative of *A New Old Play* is a complex tapestry that weaves together the supernatural bureaucracy of the Chinese underworld with a grounded, albeit stylized, retelling of 20th-century Chinese history. The film operates on two distinct planes of existence: the monochromatic, fog-laden realm of the afterlife in the 1980s, and the vibrant, evolving world of the living from the 1920s to the 1970s. These two strands are braided together through the recollections of the protagonist, Qiu Fu.

### Descent into the Underworld
The film commences in the immediate aftermath of Qiu Fu's death. The setting is the entrance to Fengdu, the "Ghost City" of Chinese mythology, depicted here not as a place of torment but as a mundane, misty administrative zone. Qiu Fu, portrayed as an elderly man in the afterlife sequences, is met by two "psychopomps" or guides: Ox-Head and Horse-Face. In traditional folklore, these are fearsome demons, but in Qiu Jiongjiong's interpretation, they are weary, bickering low-level bureaucrats concerned with their quotas and working conditions.

The journey through the underworld is slow and deliberate, mirroring the pacing of a traditional opera. The landscape is surreal, constructed from obvious soundstage elements—smoke machines, painted flats, and artificial lighting—emphasizing the film's theatrical conceit. As they travel toward the court of the King of Hell (Yama), they stop at a roadside inn located in the liminal space between existence and oblivion. This inn serves as a gathering place for wandering souls. Here, Qiu Fu encounters familiar faces from his past, including his former servant Crooky, who now works as an innkeeper in the netherworld. It is in this suspended state, awaiting the mandatory consumption of the Soup of Forgetfulness (Mengpo Soup) which will erase his memories before reincarnation, that Qiu Fu begins to narrate the story of his life[1] .

### The Foundling and the New-New Troupe (1920s)
The narrative transitions to the 1920s in Sichuan province, a period defined by the instability of the Warlord Era. A young Qiu Fu (played by Chen Haoyu) is introduced living in destitution. The film depicts his early trauma: his father, also a performer, dies while on stage, a foreshadowing of Qiu Fu's own lifelong commitment to the art. His mother, impoverished and unable to care for him, abandons the boy.

The young orphan is eventually taken in by the "New-New Troupe" (*Xinxin Troupe*), a local Sichuan opera company. The troupe is a microcosm of society, led by the charismatic and imposing Pocky Liu (played by Qiu Zhimin). Pocky is a former military commander who has transitioned from the battlefield to the theater, bringing with him a martial discipline. He runs the troupe with an iron fist, utilizing corporal punishment and strict training regimens, yet he also harbors a genuine passion for the art form. Pocky serves as a protector for the artists in a lawless time, shielding them from the worst depredations of the local warlords and bandits. Under the tutelage of the troupe's masters, Qiu Fu begins his rigorous training in the *chou* (clown) role. The film details the physical and psychological demands of this specialization, which requires the actor to master acrobatics, singing, and the ability to improvise humor while wearing the characteristic white paint around the nose and eyes[2] .

### The Theater of War (1937–1949)
As Qiu Fu matures into a young adult and a leading performer (now played by Yi Sicheng), the external world intrudes violently upon the sanctuary of the theater. The outbreak of the Second Sino-Japanese War (1937–1945) brings aerial bombardments to Sichuan. The film visualizes these attacks with a deliberate lack of realism; explosions and destruction are rendered through theatrical effects, such as shaking fabrics and stylized lighting changes, highlighting the "staged" nature of the violence while not diminishing its emotional impact on the characters.

During this period, the troupe faces immense hardship. Audiences dwindle, inflation soars, and the performers struggle to find food. Despite the privation, Qiu Fu's artistry matures, and he gains renown for his wit and skill. He forms a romantic bond with Tong Huafeng (played by Guan Nan), a fellow performer in the troupe. Their marriage and attempt to build a family are depicted against the backdrop of constant insecurity.

The end of the war against Japan brings no peace, as the country immediately plunges into the Chinese Civil War (1945–1949). The New-New Troupe finds itself navigating a treacherous political landscape. Pocky Liu's background as a former military man and the troupe's occasional performances for Nationalist (Kuomintang) officers make them vulnerable as the tide turns in favor of the Communists. The anxiety of the artists is palpable; they must constantly revise their repertoire and their public allegiances to ensure their survival. The film portrays this era as a time of moral ambiguity, where the line between artist and propagandist begins to blur[1] .

### The "New Society" and Thought Reform (1950s)
With the victory of the Communist Party and the founding of the People's Republic of China in 1949, the New-New Troupe undergoes a profound transformation. The chaotic, market-driven environment of the past is replaced by rigid state control. The troupe is nationalized, and the performers are designated as "literary and art workers."

The narrative moves through the political campaigns of the 1950s, specifically the "Thought Reform" movements and the Anti-Rightist Campaign. The film depicts the absurdity of these ideological purity tests. Traditional operas are dissected for "feudal" elements; ghosts, emperors, and concubines are banned or rewritten. The clowns, whose role was traditionally to mock authority and social mores, are now instructed that their humor must serve the "workers, peasants, and soldiers."

Pocky Liu becomes a primary target during these campaigns. His history as a warlord and a "capitalist" owner of the troupe leads to his denunciation as a class enemy. Qiu Fu, although from a poor background, is also scrutinized for his adherence to "old society" artistic traditions. The film presents the struggle sessions and self-criticism meetings as grotesque parodies of theater, where everyone is forced to play a role in a script written by the state. The betrayal of colleagues and the erosion of trust within the troupe are central themes of this segment[6] .

### Famine and the Pigsty (1960s–1970s)
The narrative descends into its darkest chapter with the onset of the Great Leap Forward (1958–1962) and the subsequent Cultural Revolution (1966–1976). The film does not shy away from depicting the Great Famine that ravaged Sichuan. In scenes that blend horror with surrealism, the characters are shown catching and eating rats to survive. The artistic refinement of the opera troupe stands in stark contrast to the primal struggle for biological existence.

During the Cultural Revolution, the persecution of the artists intensifies. Qiu Fu is labeled a "monster and demon" (*niuguisheshen*). The ultimate humiliation occurs when he is forced to live in a pigsty—a literalization of his status as a social pariah. The beautiful costumes and handcrafted props of the troupe are burned or confiscated, symbolizing the destruction of culture. Yet, even in the pigsty, Qiu Fu attempts to maintain a semblance of his identity. The film suggests that his training as a clown—someone who understands the inherent absurdity of the human condition—grants him a unique form of spiritual resilience. He endures the physical abuse and psychological torture with a stoic, almost detached, observation[1] .

### The Final Curtain (1980s)
The chronological narrative concludes in the reform era of the 1980s. Qiu Fu has been "rehabilitated" politically, but he is now a broken man, physically frail and haunting a world that is rapidly modernizing and leaving traditional opera behind. He dies in an accident, bringing the story full circle to the opening scene in the underworld.

Back in the netherworld inn, Qiu Fu finishes his tale. Surrounded by the ghosts of his past—Pocky, Crooky, and others—he shares a final toast. The mood is one of resignation and melancholic acceptance. The "New Old Play" of his life has concluded. He steps onto the Bridge of Helplessness and drinks the Soup of Forgetfulness. As he consumes the broth, the memories of fifty years of struggle, art, love, and pain dissolve. He walks into the mist of reincarnation, his individual history erased, leaving only the collective memory of the film itself[1] .

## Cast and Characters
The casting of *A New Old Play* is a significant aspect of its artistic statement. Director Qiu Jiongjiong employs a mixture of professional actors and non-professionals, drawing heavily from his own family and close circle of friends. This choice imbues the film with an intimacy and a meta-theatrical layer, as descendants often play their ancestors or variations of people they knew[2] .

| Actor | Character | Role Description |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Yi Sicheng** | **Qiu Fu** | The protagonist, a renowned *chou* (clown) actor in the New-New Troupe. Based on the director's grandfather, Qiu Fuxin. Yi plays the adult and elderly Qiu Fu. He captures the physicality of the clown role—the gait, the expressions, and the tragicomic aura. |
| **Chen Haoyu** | **Young Qiu Fu** | Plays Qiu Fu as a child. Chen is the director's nephew, continuing the family lineage within the cast. |

| **Gu Tao** | **Crooky** (Tuo'er) | A hunchbacked servant of the troupe who appears in the afterlife as an innkeeper. The character is "aphasic" and speaks little, serving as a silent witness. Gu Tao is a notable independent documentary filmmaker. |

### Character Analysis
* **Qiu Fu:** As a *chou* actor, Qiu Fu is not a heroic protagonist in the traditional sense. The *chou* in Chinese opera is allowed to speak truth to power through humor, but is also a figure of low status. Qiu Fu's survival through the changing regimes is not due to ideological fervor but to his adaptability and his clown's ability to endure humiliation. He embodies the resilience of the ordinary Sichuanese people.  
* **Pocky Liu:** Played by the director's father, Pocky is a figure of patriarchal authority. His transition from warlord to theater manager represents the shift in power dynamics in early 20th-century China. His eventual persecution highlights the indiscriminate nature of the political purges.  
* **Crooky:** This character functions as a spiritual anchor. In the underworld, he provides the space for Qiu Fu's recollection. His physical deformity and silence contrast with the articulate, performative nature of the actors, grounding the film in a somatic reality[10] .

## Production
### Development and Inspiration
*A New Old Play* is deeply rooted in the director's personal history. Qiu Jiongjiong was born in Leshan, Sichuan, in 1977, into a family of opera performers. His grandfather, Qiu Fuxin, was a legendary *chou* actor. Qiu grew up in the theater, absorbing its aesthetics, sounds, and stories, which later informed his work as a painter and documentary filmmaker.

The direct impetus for the film came in 2017, the 30th anniversary of Qiu Fuxin's death. Qiu's father (who plays Pocky in the film) wrote a biography of the grandfather and asked Qiu Jiongjiong to provide illustrations. During the two-month process of drawing these scenes, Qiu realized the cinematic potential of the material. He began writing the screenplay in May 2017, completing a draft of over 100,000 words by February 2018\. The project was initially titled *The Neo-New Adventures*.

Qiu stated that he wanted to explore the "clown's perspective" as the "author's perspective." He viewed the clown not just as a role but as a spirit—one of sympathy, mischief, and critical observation. The film was conceived as a "Sichuan-flavored saga," aiming to capture the unique dialect and cultural temperament of his home province[2] .

### Set Design and Visual Aesthetic
The most striking element of *A New Old Play* is its visual style. Rejecting the location shooting common in independent Chinese cinema (often associated with the "dGritty" realism of directors like Jia Zhangke), Qiu opted for total artifice.

* **Soundstage Shooting:** The entire film was shot on a soundstage in Leshan, Sichuan.  
* **Handcrafted Sets:** The production design, led by Qiu himself, is described as "handcrafted" and "artisanal." The crew spent six months building the sets using everyday materials—wood, fabric, Styrofoam, and waste materials. This aesthetic has been compared to *arte povera* (poor art), emphasizing the materiality of the objects.  
* **Painted Backdrops:** The film extensively uses 2D painted backdrops to represent environments. These are not photorealistic but expressionist, evoking the style of traditional Chinese landscape painting and the stage flats of old opera.  
* **Miniatures:** Detailed miniatures were constructed for establishing shots, including a model village and a surreal river landscape.  
* **Practical Effects:** Visual effects were achieved practically. For example, the turbulent waters of a river were simulated by crew members shaking long pieces of white fabric, a technique borrowed directly from the Peking/Sichuan opera stage[2] .

### Cinematography
The film was photographed by **Robbin Feng** (Feng Yuchao). The cinematography complements the set design by employing a "tableau" style.

* **Aspect Ratio:** The film is presented in a **1.77:1 (16:9)** aspect ratio. This widescreen format allows for horizontal compositions that mimic the unrolling of a traditional Chinese scroll painting.  
* **Framing:** The camera often remains static or pans slowly across a scene, observing the action from a distance that mimics the proscenium arch of a theater. This reinforces the "fourth wall" and the theatricality of the presentation.  
* **Lighting:** The lighting is deliberately theatrical, often using spotlights or unnatural colors to highlight the emotional tone of a scene rather than to simulate natural light[3] .

### Sound and Music
* **Sichuan Dialect:** The dialogue is almost entirely in the Sichuan dialect. Qiu considers the dialect essential to the film's texture, noting its "tones of vivacity, vulgarity, voracity, and... ecstasy and melancholy."  
* **Score:** The music, composed by **Diao Lili**, is derived from the percussive patterns of Sichuan opera. Gongs, cymbals, and clappers are used not just during performance scenes but as a rhythmic underscore to the narrative, punctuating the dialogue and action. The sound design creates a bridge between the diegetic world of the characters and the meta-theatrical layer of the film[3] .

## Themes and Analysis
### The Clown as Witness and Survivor
The central theme of the film is the archetype of the **clown** (*chou*). In Chinese opera, the clown is unique; he is the only character allowed to improvise and speak directly to the audience. He is low-class but high-wisdom. Qiu Jiongjiong uses the clown as a vehicle to navigate the trauma of the 20th century. While heroes and generals (the *sheng* and *jing* roles) fall when their ideologies collapse, the clown survives because he is flexible, self-deprecating, and grounded. Qiu Fu's survival is not heroic resistance but a form of "existential persistence." The film argues that the view from the bottom—the clown's eye view—is the most honest perspective on history[5] .

### Memory vs. Official History
The framing device of the underworld foregrounds the tension between **individual memory** and **state-sanctioned oblivion**. The "Soup of Forgetfulness" represents the erasure of personal history required by the cycle of reincarnation, but it also serves as a metaphor for the political amnesia encouraged by the state. By recounting his life before drinking the soup, Qiu Fu performs an act of resistance. He preserves the "unofficial" history of the troupe—the petty squabbles, the moments of grace, the specific textures of life—against the grand, sweeping narratives of "Revolution" and "Liberation" that seek to flatten human experience. The film is a repository for these vanishing memories[4] .

### Theatricality and Reality
*A New Old Play* blurs the line between **life and theater**. By shooting in a deliberately artificial style, Qiu Jiongjiong suggests that for the characters, the stage is more real than the chaotic world outside. Furthermore, the film depicts political events—struggle sessions, rallies, wars—as forms of theater, often poorly scripted and violently directed. The "Brechtian" alienation effect (Verfremdungseffekt) prevents the audience from passively consuming the tragedy, encouraging a critical engagement with the mechanisms of history. The "fake" sets reveal the "truth" of the situation more effectively than a realistic reconstruction could[4] .

### Sichuanese Optimism
Despite the grim subject matter (war, famine, persecution), the film maintains a tone of **tragicomic optimism**. This is attributed to the Sichuanese character, described by the director as having a "harmony between sorrow and joy." The depiction of the afterlife is a prime example: it is not a terrifying hell but a bureaucratic extension of life, where friends can meet for a drink. The "Ba Ba banquets"—feasts held for both weddings and funerals—symbolize this acceptance of the cycle of life and death. The film treats death not as a tragedy but as a curtain call[2] .

## Historical Context
### Sichuan Opera (*Chuanju*)
The film serves as a love letter to **Sichuan opera**, a major school of Chinese opera that originated in the late Ming and early Qing dynasties. Unlike the more formal Peking opera, Sichuan opera is known for its humor, lively dialogue, and distinct high-pitched singing. It features five main roles: *Sheng* (male), *Dan* (female), *Jing* (painted face), *Mo* (middle-aged male), and *Chou* (clown). The film meticulously reconstructs the training methods, backstage rituals, and performance styles of the early 20th century. It also documents the decline of the art form, from the vibrant, chaotic market-town theaters of the 1920s to the sterile, state-controlled troupes of the 1950s, and finally to its marginalization in the modern era[1] .

### Political Movements
The film provides a critical view of several key events in modern Chinese history:

* **The New Life Movement:** Briefly alluded to in the Nationalist period attempts to "civilize" the theater.  
* **The Anti-Rightist Movement (1957–1959):** Depicted through the purging of Pocky Liu. The film highlights the arbitrary nature of the labeling of "Rightists."  
* **The Great Leap Forward (1958–1962):** Portrayed through the famine. The scene of actors eating rats is a stark representation of the desperation caused by agricultural mismanagement.  
* **The Cultural Revolution (1966–1976):** The attack on the "Four Olds" (Old Customs, Old Culture, Old Habits, and Old Ideas) directly impacted the opera, which was seen as a vessel of feudal superstition. The humiliation of Qiu Fu reflects the widespread persecution of artists and intellectuals during this decade[2] .

## Release and Reception
### Festival Run
*A New Old Play* premiered at the **Locarno Film Festival** on August 13, 2021, in the International Competition. It was a breakout success, winning the **Special Jury Prize**. It subsequently played at major festivals worldwide:

* **Festival des 3 Continents (Nantes):** Won the Young Jury Award.  
* **Hong Kong International Film Festival (HKIFF):** Won the Firebird Award for Best Film (Young Cinema Competition).  
* **International Film Festival Rotterdam (IFFR).**  
* **New York Film Festival (NYFF).**  
* **Singapore International Film Festival.**  
* **Busan International Film Festival.**

### Critical Reception
The film has received overwhelmingly positive reviews from international critics.

* **Visuals:** *The New York Times* described it as "visually magnificent," while *The New Yorker*'s Richard Brody called it "daring and imaginative." The handcrafted aesthetic was frequently cited as a refreshing departure from digital effects.  
* **Narrative:** *Variety* (via The Film Verdict) praised it as a "uniquely refined first film" but noted its "leisurely pace" might challenge some viewers. *Cinema Scope* championed Qiu as the "brightest light" in Chinese independent cinema.  
* **Thematic Depth:** Critics lauded the film's "political courage" and its poignant exploration of memory. *Screen Daily* noted the "blatant but effective" commentary on the politically reinforced changes in the arts[3] .

### Distribution
* **North America:** Acquired by **Icarus Films** for the dGenerate Films Collection. It received a limited theatrical release (e.g., Anthology Film Archives, Museum of the Moving Image). A Blu-ray was released by **Vinegar Syndrome** under the **Kani** label in 2023\.  
* **France:** Distributed by **Hippocampe Productions**, with a theatrical release scheduled for **April 30, 2025** (or June 2025 depending on the source)[2] .  
* **China:** The film has not received a general theatrical release in mainland China due to censorship restrictions regarding its historical content. It circulates through private screenings and the "grey market" of cinephile file sharing.
