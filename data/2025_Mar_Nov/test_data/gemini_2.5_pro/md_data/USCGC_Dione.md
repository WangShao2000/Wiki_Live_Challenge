# USCGC Dione (WPC-107): An Operational, Technical, and Historical Analysis of a Thetis-Class Cutter
## Abstract
The naval history of the mid-20th century is frequently characterized by the clash of titan fleets—battleships, aircraft carriers, and heavy cruisers. However, the littoral and convoy battles that secured the Allied supply lines were often fought by vessels of much more modest displacement. Among these, the USCGC *Dione* (WPC-107) stands as a singular case study in endurance, adaptability, and the evolution of maritime defense. Commissioned in 1934 as a 165-foot patrol boat designed for the constabulary duties of Prohibition enforcement, the *Dione* was thrust by geopolitical circumstance into the vanguard of the anti-submarine war in 1942\. For a critical window of time, this cutter served as the sole major surface asset defending the "Torpedo Junction" of the North Carolina coast against the Kriegsmarine's Operation *Paukenschlag*. Her career trajectory—from rum-chaser to U-boat hunter, to search-and-rescue platform, and finally to a geophysical exploration vessel in the Persian Gulf—offers a unique lens through which to view the technological and strategic shifts of the maritime domain from the 1930s to the 1990s. This report provides an exhaustive analysis of the *Dione*, reconstructing her operational life through deck logs, technical specifications, and historical correspondence.

## ---

**1\. Genesis and Construction: The Manitowoc Legacy**

### 1.1 The Strategic Imperative: The End of Prohibition and the "Rum War"
To understand the design philosophy of the *Dione*, one must first examine the operational environment of the early 1930s. The United States Coast Guard was engaged in what was popularly known as the "Rum War," a massive maritime interdiction effort aimed at enforcing the Volstead Act. Smuggling syndicates had evolved from utilizing small, fast contact boats to deploying larger "mother ships" that loitered just beyond the territorial limit—initially three nautical miles, later extended to twelve. These mother ships could remain on station for weeks, necessitating a Coast Guard counter-force capable of similar endurance and seakeeping[1] .

The answer to this requirement was the 165-foot (A) Class cutter, widely referred to as the *Thetis* class. These vessels were designed to bridge the gap between the smaller 125-foot patrol boats, which lacked the speed and habitability for extended offshore patrols, and the larger cruising cutters. The design called for a vessel that was robust, relatively fast, and capable of operating in the heavy weather of the North Atlantic where the smugglers often operated[1] .

### 1.2 The Manitowoc Shipbuilding Corporation
The contract for the construction of the *Dione* was awarded to the Manitowoc Shipbuilding Corporation, located on the western shore of Lake Michigan in Manitowoc, Wisconsin[1] . The selection of a Great Lakes shipyard was significant. Manitowoc had established a reputation for building vessels capable of withstanding the unique and violent hydrodynamics of the Great Lakes, where short-period waves can inflict severe structural stress on a hull. This rugged construction ethos was transferred directly into the *Dione*'s frame.

The *Dione* was laid down on **10 November 1933**, designated with the builder's hull number **P-13**.3 Her construction proceeded through the winter and spring, a period of economic despondency in the United States. The construction of the *Thetis*\-class cutters represented not just a naval procurement program but a component of the New Deal-era industrial sustainment, keeping skilled shipwrights employed during the Great Depression.

### 1.3 Launch and Commissioning
The *Dione* was launched on **30 June 1934**.1 The christening was performed by her sponsor, **Ms. Unita Risch**, a ceremony that marked the transition of the hull from an industrial project to a living ship[3] . Following her fitting out—a process involving the installation of her engines, habitation spaces, and initial armament—she was declared ready for service in the autumn of that year.

Commissioning Date: 5 October 1934.1  
Total Cost: $258,000.1  
By a twist of historical irony, the primary mission for which *Dione* was built—Prohibition enforcement—had evaporated prior to her commissioning. The 21st Amendment, repealing Prohibition, had been ratified in December 1933\. Thus, the *Dione* entered service as a ship without a war, transitioning immediately into a general-purpose cutter role, focusing on maritime safety, law enforcement, and winter cruising[2] .


**2\. Technical Specifications and Engineering Analysis**

The *Dione* was a technological hybrid, bridging the gap between the steam-powered past and the diesel-electric future. Her specifications reveal a vessel optimized for endurance rather than sprinting speed, a trait that would prove vital during the convoy escort duties of World War II.

### 2.1 Hull and Dimensions
The hull of the *Dione* was constructed of steel, featuring a flush deck design that enhanced longitudinal strength and provided a drier foredeck in rough seas. The raked stem and cruiser stern gave her a distinctive, yacht-like profile that belied her utilitarian purpose.

| Dimension | Specification (1934) | Specification (1945 Refit) |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Length Overall (LOA)** | 165 feet | 165 feet |
| **Beam** | 25 feet, 3 inches | 25 feet, 3 inches |
| **Draft (Mean)** | 7 feet, 8 inches | 10 feet |
| **Displacement (Full Load)** | 337 tons | 350 tons |

The increase in draft and displacement by 1945 reflects the extensive addition of wartime equipment, including heavier armament, radar masts, and anti-submarine gear, which significantly altered her stability characteristics and required careful ballast management[1] .

### 2.2 Propulsion Systems
At the heart of the *Dione* lay her propulsion plant. Unlike the steam turbines common in Navy destroyers of the era, the *Thetis* class utilized diesel propulsion.

* **Engines:** Two **Winton Model 158** 6-cylinder diesel engines[1] .  
* **Output:** 1,340 brake horsepower (bhp) combined[1] .  
* **Propellers:** Twin 3-bladed screws[1] .

This powerplant provided *Dione* with exceptional range, a critical factor for a patrol vessel.

* **Maximum Speed:** 16.0 knots.  
* **Cruising Speed:** 11.0 knots (Range: 3,000 statute miles).  
* **Economic Speed:** 6.0 knots (Range: 6,417 statute miles)[1] .

The ability to cruise for over 6,000 miles at economic speed meant that *Dione* could theoretically cross the Atlantic and back without refueling, or more practically, remain on station off the Outer Banks for weeks at a time hunting U-boats.

### 2.3 Armament Evolution
The armament of the *Dione* underwent radical changes mirroring the escalation of global hostilities.

#### The Peace Configuration (1934-1940)
Upon commissioning, the *Dione* carried a light armament suitable for signaling and stopping merchant vessels.

* 1 x 3-inch/23 caliber gun: A dual-purpose weapon, but of low velocity and limited effectiveness against modern aircraft or surface raiders.  
* 1 x 1-pounder gun: Primarily a saluting battery[1] .

#### The Neutrality Patrol Configuration (1941)
As tensions rose in Europe, the Coast Guard began to up-gun its cutters.

* Retained the 3-inch/23 caliber gun.  
* Added **1 x Y-gun**: A depth charge projector capable of throwing charges to the side of the ship.  
* Added **2 x Depth Charge Tracks**: Located on the fantail for rolling charges off the stern[1] .

#### The Hunter-Killer Configuration (1945)
By the end of the war, *Dione* had been transformed into a dedicated anti-submarine platform.

* **Main Battery:** 2 x **3-inch/50 caliber guns** (single mounts). These offered superior ballistics and a higher rate of fire than the 3"/23.1  
* **Anti-Aircraft:** 2 x **20mm/80 Oerlikon cannons**. These rapid-fire guns provided close-in defense against aircraft and could be used to suppress the decks of surfaced submarines[1] .  
* **Advanced ASW:**  
  * **2 x Mousetrap Launchers:** A forward-throwing anti-submarine rocket system. This was a critical upgrade, as it allowed the cutter to fire at a submerged contact without passing directly over it, thereby maintaining sonar contact during the attack run.  
  * **2 x Y-Guns**: Increased from one to two, allowing for a broader pattern of depth charges.  
  * **2 x Depth Charge Tracks**: Retained[1] .


**3\. The Gathering Storm: Operations 1935–1941**

### 3.1 The Norfolk Years
Following her commissioning, *Dione* was homeported in **Norfolk, Virginia**.2 This strategic location at the mouth of the Chesapeake Bay placed her at the center of Mid-Atlantic maritime traffic. For the remainder of the 1930s, her logs reflect the routine but hazardous work of the Coast Guard:

* **Winter Cruising:** Patrolling specific "stations" in the Atlantic during the winter months to assist merchant traffic beset by gales.  
* **Regatta Patrols:** Enforcing safety zones during civilian maritime events.  
* **Search and Rescue (SAR):** Responding to distress calls from fishing trawlers and commercial steamers.

### 3.2 The Shift to Defense
With the outbreak of World War II in Europe in September 1939, the mission of the *Dione* shifted. Under the "Neutrality Patrol" orders issued by President Roosevelt, Coast Guard cutters were tasked with tracking belligerent shipping and preventing unneutral acts within the US maritime exclusion zones.

* **Identification Signal:** NRGV[2] .  
* **Designation:** In 1941, the cutter was formally integrated into the **Eastern Sea Frontier (EASTSEAFRON)**, the naval command responsible for the defense of the East Coast[1] .


**4\. The Crucible: 1942 and the Battle of Torpedo Junction**

### 4.1 The Strategic Context
The entry of the United States into World War II following Pearl Harbor was immediately followed by Germany's Operation *Paukenschlag* (Drumbeat). In January 1942, Admiral Karl Dönitz dispatched his long-range Type IX U-boats to the US East Coast. They found a target-rich environment. Coastal cities were not blacked out, providing silhouetted targets for U-boats at night. Convoy systems were not yet organized.

The waters off Cape Hatteras, North Carolina, became the killing grounds. Merchant ships rounding the cape were forced into predictable sea lanes by the shoals, creating a bottleneck that the U-boat commanders christened "Torpedo Junction."

### 4.2 The "Only Ship"
In early 1942, the Fifth Naval District, headquartered in Norfolk, faced a critical shortage of escort vessels. The US Navy's destroyers were committed to the Atlantic convoy routes to Britain or the Pacific theater.  
Historical records provide a stark assessment of the situation: "The U.S. Coast Guard Cutter Dione was the only ship patrolling the 5th Naval District... capable of opposing German U-boats."[2] .  
For a terrified merchant marine, the 165-foot *Dione* represented the only immediate hope of rescue or defense against the "Grey Wolves" prowling the Outer Banks.

### 4.3 Combat Operations Log: Spring 1942
The operational tempo of the *Dione* during the spring of 1942 was relentless. She was constantly at sea, oscillating between hunting contacts and rescuing survivors from burning tankers.

#### 20 March 1942: Air-Surface Coordination
*Dione* demonstrated early attempts at combined arms warfare. While on patrol, she established contact with an aircraft from the **Coast Guard Air Station Elizabeth City**.

* **Action:** The cutter directed the aircraft to the contact location.  
* **Engagement:** The aircraft dropped two depth charges.  
* **Result:** Unknown. No debris or oil was confirmed, but the presence of air-surface coordination forced the U-boat to break off any potential attack and remain submerged[1] .

#### 5 April 1942: The *Byron D. Benson* Tragedy
The vulnerability of independent shipping was brutally demonstrated on the night of 5 April. The American tanker **SS *Byron D. Benson*** (7,953 gross tons) was carrying crude oil from Texas to New Jersey.

* **The Attack:** *U-552*, commanded by the ace Erich Topp, torpedoed the tanker off Currituck Inlet[5] .  
* **The Inferno:** The torpedo struck the starboard side, igniting the cargo. The ship did not sink immediately but became a blazing torch.  
* **Dione's Response:** *Dione* was in the vicinity and closed at maximum speed. Maneuvering near a burning tanker is incredibly dangerous due to the risk of secondary explosions and spreading oil fires.  
* **The Rescue:** Despite the chaos, *Dione* managed to rescue one survivor from the water[1] . Other vessels in the area assisted, and 27 of the 37 crew members ultimately survived. The *Benson* drifted and burned for three days, a visible symbol of the carnage of "Torpedo Alley"[5] .

#### 18 April 1942: Persistent Hunting
Following a submarine attack on another tanker, *Dione* was observed by a friendly aircraft dropping depth charges on a sonar contact.

* **Outcome:** No visible results[1] .  
* **Significance:** These engagements, while often not resulting in a "kill," were vital in suppressing U-boat operations. A U-boat under depth charge attack is a U-boat that cannot fire torpedoes.

### 4.4 The Convoy Battles: June 1942
By mid-1942, the US Navy had instituted a coastal convoy system. *Dione* transitioned from independent patrol to convoy escort.

#### 24 June 1942: The Convoy Attack
On the evening of 24 June, *Dione* was escorting a convoy through the hazardous waters off North Carolina.

* **19:00 Hours:** *Dione* made an underwater sound contact (sonar).  
* **19:05 Hours:** The cutter initiated an urgent attack, dropping one depth charge from each of her stern tracks.  
* **Result:** Negative. The U-boat evaded.  
* **19:10 Hours:** Five minutes after the drop, the U-boat successfully fired torpedoes into the convoy. Two ships, the **SS *Nordal*** (cited in some sources as *Nordland*) and the **SS *Manuela***, were struck[1] .  
* **Counter-Attack:** *Dione* immediately switched to hunter-killer mode. Leaving the rescue operations to the British ship *Norwich City*, *Dione* executed a "sweep search" on the starboard flank of the convoy (Position 34°31'N, 75°42'W)[1] .  
* **20:00 Hours:** The *Nordal* was observed sinking and afire.  
* **25 June 1942:** The following day, *Dione* regained contact and dropped five depth charges. This time, quantities of oil were observed rising to the surface[1] . While not a confirmed kill (U-boats often ejected oil to simulate destruction), the attack likely damaged the aggressor or forced a withdrawal.

### 4.5 Tactical Analysis of 1942 Operations
The failure to sink the attacker on 24 June highlights the technological limitations of the *Thetis* class in 1942\.

1. **Stern-Dropped Ordnance:** *Dione* was equipped only with stern tracks and Y-guns. To attack, she had to pass directly over the submarine. During the final approach, sonar contact is lost (the "baffle" zone). A skilled U-boat commander, hearing the screws pass overhead, would turn hard to port or starboard, moving out of the lethal radius of the sinking charges.  
2. **Lack of Forward Weapons:** The absence of "Hedgehog" or "Mousetrap" weapons meant *Dione* could not fire while maintaining sonar contact.  
3. **Sonar Conditions:** The waters off Cape Hatteras are notorious for thermal layers (thermoclines) caused by the collision of the warm Gulf Stream and the cold Labrador Current. These layers deflect sonar beams, allowing U-boats to hide acoustically.

Despite these handicaps, *Dione* performed a critical deterrence function. Her mere presence forced U-boats to remain submerged, limiting their mobility (underwater speed was slow) and reducing their ability to track convoys.


**5\. Evolution and Refit: 1943–1945**

### 5.1 Redeployment to the Northeast
As the "Battle of the Atlantic" shifted away from the US coast to the mid-ocean "air gap," the *Dione* was redeployed.

* **December 1943 – December 1944:** Stationed at **Tompkinsville, Staten Island**.  
* **Mission:** Escorting **HK** and **KN** convoys (New York to Key West routings)[2] .

### 5.2 The 1945 Refit
To address the tactical deficiencies exposed in 1942, *Dione* underwent a massive refit in 1945\. This modernization transformed her capabilities.

**Sensor Upgrades:**

* **Radar:** Installation of **SF Radar**. This surface-search radar allowed *Dione* to detect surfaced submarines at night or in fog, neutralizing the U-boat's favorite tactic of surface attacks under cover of darkness[1] .  
* **Sonar:** Upgrade to the **QCN-1** system, offering better sensitivity and bearing resolution[1] .

**Weapons Upgrades:**

* **Mousetraps:** Two forward-throwing rocket launchers were installed. These fired contact-fuzed projectiles ahead of the ship. If a rocket struck the sub, it detonated; if it missed, it did not, leaving the water undisturbed for continued sonar tracking[1] .  
* **3-inch/50 Caliber Guns:** The old 23-caliber gun was replaced with two 50-caliber dual-purpose guns. These high-velocity weapons were far more effective against the newer, faster U-boats and aircraft[1] .


**6\. The Surrender Group: May 1945**

With the death of Hitler and the collapse of the Third Reich in May 1945, Grand Admiral Dönitz ordered all U-boats to surrender. The US Navy established a specialized task force to intercept and process these vessels: the **Surrender Group**.

### 6.1 Composition of the Surrender Group
The group operated in the **First Naval District** (New England) and was comprised of six patrol vessels. The core of this group were three veteran *Thetis*\-class cutters:

* **USCGC *Dione* (WPC-107)**  
* **USCGC *Nemesis* (WPC-111)**  
* **USCGC *Argo* (WPC-100)** (Flagship of the group, under Lt. Eliot Winslow)[2] .

### 6.2 The Capture of U-1228
One of the most significant operations for *Dione* was the surrender of **U-1228**.

* **The Target:** *U-1228* was a Type IXC/40 U-boat. It had received surrender orders on 9 May while at sea. Initially ordered to St. John's, Newfoundland, the commander (Oberleutnant zur See Friedrich-Wilhelm Marienfeld) diverted to the US coast due to poor weather[2] .  
* **The Intercept:** On **17 May 1945**, *Dione* rendezvoused with *U-1228* off the New England coast. The destroyer escort **USS *Neal A. Scott*** was also involved in the initial intercept[2] .  
* **The Escort:** *Dione* assumed the role of close escort. Photographs from the National Archives show *Dione* steaming alongside the surfaced U-boat, which flew the black flag of surrender from its conning tower. The cutter shepherded the submarine into the **Portsmouth Navy Yard**, New Hampshire[2] .

### 6.3 Processing the "Grey Wolves"
The Surrender Group was also responsible for processing other high-profile U-boats, including:

* **U-858:** The first German warship to surrender to US forces after the capitulation.  
* **U-234:** The notorious transport submarine carrying a cargo of uranium oxide (destined for the Japanese nuclear program), jet aircraft plans, and two Japanese officers (who committed suicide before surrender). While *Argo* and USS *Sutton* are primarily credited with the initial boarding of *U-234*, *Dione* was an integral part of the screening force that secured these dangerous prizes[2] .

This period represented the culmination of *Dione*'s war. The ship that had been hunted in 1942 was now the jailor of the defeated enemy.


**7\. The Cold War and the Gulf of Mexico: 1951–1963**

### 7.1 Decommissioning and Reactivation
Following the war, the Coast Guard faced a severe personnel shortage. *Dione* was decommissioned on **23 July 1947** and placed in reserve[3] . However, the onset of the Korean War and the deepening Cold War necessitated a fleet expansion. *Dione* was recommissioned on **4 February 1951**.1

### 7.2 The Freeport Station
In 1952, *Dione* was assigned a new homeport: **Freeport, Texas**. She was the first Coast Guard cutter to be stationed there[3] .

* **Mission Profile:** The mission shifted from ASW to Search and Rescue (SAR) and Law Enforcement (LE) in the Gulf of Mexico.  
* **Notable Operations:**  
  * **January 1957:** *Dione* towed the disabled cutter **USCGC *Iris*** to New Orleans. This operation (2-5 January) demonstrated the towing utility of the 165-foot class[1] .  
  * **Command:** From August 1959 to January 1961, the cutter was commanded by **Robert H. Scarborough** (then likely a Lieutenant Commander). Scarborough would go on to achieve the rank of Vice Admiral and serve as Vice Commandant of the Coast Guard[14] .

This era was defined by routine but vital service—rescuing shrimpers in hurricanes, enforcing fisheries treaties, and maintaining a federal presence in the Gulf.


**8\. The Commercial Afterlife: 1964–1992**

Most warships are scrapped shortly after decommissioning. The *Dione* proved exceptionally resilient.

### 8.1 Sale and Conversion
*Dione* was decommissioned for the final time on **8 February 1963** and sold on **24 February 1964**.1 She entered the merchant fleet, beginning a second career that would last nearly 30 years.

### 8.2 The Three Lives of a Merchant Ship
The vessel operated under three commercial names:

1. **MV *Big Trouble*:** Owned by Big Trouble Inc. (New Orleans). The name likely reflected the maintenance challenges of a 30-year-old military hull[2] .  
2. **MV *Delta I*:** In 1968, she was renamed *Delta I*.  
   * **The 1969 Fire:** On **5 April 1969**, while transiting from Kingston, Jamaica, to Port of Spain, Trinidad, the ship suffered a major fire. The superstructure was destroyed, but the steel hull survived—a testament to the Manitowoc shipbuilders of 1934[2] .  
3. **MV *Al Rashid*:** Following the fire, the hull was salvaged and rebuilt in Tampa, Florida. She was renamed *Al Rashid* in late 1969[2] .  
   * **New Role:** She was converted into a supply ship and "jack-of-all-trades" vessel (crew boat, oil rig tender) for the Middle East oil industry[2] .  
   * **Operations:** She deployed to the Persian Gulf, supporting the offshore oil boom.  
   * **IMO Number:** 7515080[2] .  
   * **Final Status:** *Al Rashid* was listed in Lloyd's Register until **1992**, at which point she disappears from the record, likely scrapped[2] .

**10\. Conclusion**

The USCGC *Dione* (WPC-107) serves as a paradigm of the Coast Guard's 20th-century experience: doing more with less. Built for a law enforcement mission that vanished before she was commissioned, she adapted to become a frontline warship in the nation's hour of direst need. When the US Navy's destroyers were absent in 1942, *Dione* stood alone against the U-boat offensive in the Fifth Naval District. She survived the "Battle of Torpedo Junction," evolved through technology to become a hunter-killer in 1945, and then served another decade in the Cold War. Even her commercial afterlife—surviving a catastrophic fire to work for another twenty years in the Persian Gulf—speaks to the quality of her American engineering. While she may not possess the fame of a fleet carrier, *Dione*'s history is a testament to the endurance of ship and crew, embodying the Coast Guard motto *Semper Paratus*—Always Ready.
