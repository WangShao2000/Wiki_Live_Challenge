# Joy Snihur Wyatt Laking: A Comprehensive Study of Life, Art, and Advocacy
## Executive Summary
Joy Snihur Wyatt Laking (born 1950\) stands as a seminal figure in the landscape of contemporary Canadian realism. For more than five decades, Laking has dedicated her professional life to the documentation and interpretation of the Nova Scotian environment, establishing herself as a preeminent chronicler of the Bay of Fundy’s distinct geography and cultural vernacular[1] . Operating primarily from her studio and gallery in Portapique, Colchester County, Laking has cultivated a formidable reputation for her technical mastery across multiple mediums—most notably watercolor, oil, and acrylic—while simultaneously expanding the boundaries of her creative practice into literature and theatre[2] .

Laking's career is characterized by a dual commitment to aesthetic excellence and community advocacy. As a founding member of the Professional Living Artists of Nova Scotia (PLANS), she has been instrumental in revitalizing the status of realist art within the Atlantic Canadian critical discourse[4] . Her artistic contributions have been recognized with high honours, including the Queen Elizabeth II Diamond Jubilee Medal and the Halifax Progress Club Woman of Excellence Award[5] . Beyond the visual arts, Laking has demonstrated a profound engagement with social issues, authoring the play *Invisible Prisons* to address the pervasive reality of domestic violence[2] .

This report provides an exhaustive biographical and critical analysis of Joy Laking’s career. It synthesizes data regarding her early influences, her idiosyncratic technical approaches to watercolor, her extensive exhibition history, her literary output, and her enduring impact on the cultural fabric of Nova Scotia.

**1\. Biography and Formative Years**

### 1.1 Origins and Familial Influence
Joy Snihur Wyatt Laking was born in 1950 in Owen Sound, Ontario[7] . Her entry into the world of creative expression was arguably predetermined by a rich familial environment that valued artistic endeavor. She was born into a household where art was not merely a pastime but a vocation; her mother was a professional painter, and her father was a skilled craftsman known for creating intricate marionettes and toy trains[8] . This early exposure to the rigors and rewards of creative production laid the foundation for Laking's future career.

The influence of her mother was particularly catalytic, serving as both a pedagogical model and a source of existential motivation. Laking painted alongside her mother from a young age, absorbing lessons in observation and technique that would later define her own pedagogy[8] . However, it was a tragedy that solidified Laking's professional resolve. Her mother passed away prematurely in her early fifties from breast cancer, a loss that occurred roughly around the time Laking was completing her university studies[7] .

In a revealing essay, Laking articulated the profound impact of this loss on her career trajectory. She noted that her mother’s death served as "an incentive to me to take the risk and practice my art with passion and commitment"[7] . Confronted with the fragility of life, Laking decided "not to put it off," rejecting the safer, more conventional career paths often recommended to fine arts graduates in favor of immediate immersion in professional practice. This decision to bypass deferral and embrace the uncertainty of an artistic life was a direct response to her mother's unfulfilled potential, as her mother "never knew that I became an artist too"[7] .

### 1.2 Education and Academic Background
Laking pursued formal academic training at the University of Guelph, where she majored in Fine Art, graduating in 1972[2] . Her time at Guelph provided her with a theoretical and practical foundation in the visual arts, yet Laking maintains a distinction regarding her technical expertise. Despite her degree, she identifies as largely self-taught in the medium of watercolor[2] . This autodidactic approach to her primary medium allowed her to develop unconventional methods that diverge from academic norms, a topic explored in depth in the *Artistic Style* section of this report.

### 1.3 Migration to Nova Scotia and Geographic Adaptation
Following her graduation in 1972, Laking relocated to Nova Scotia, a move that would define the geographic and thematic scope of her life's work[8] . She settled in the rural community of Bass River and later established her permanent base in Portapique[8] .

This migration required a significant aesthetic adaptation. Laking was raised in the rugged, rock-strewn topography of Georgian Bay, Ontario—a landscape defined by open water and granite. In contrast, her new home on the shores of the Bay of Fundy presented a landscape of mudflats, salt marshes, and dramatic tidal shifts. Laking candidly admitted that she initially found the Nova Scotian terrain "very flat, no rocks... pastoral," and it did not immediately appeal to her sensibilities[10] . However, the landscape "gradually grew on her" as she began to paint it[10] . Over the ensuing forty-plus years, Laking has transformed this initial ambivalence into a profound intimacy, becoming one of the region's most dedicated visual interpreters[2] .

### 1.4 Personal Life
Laking’s personal life is deeply intertwined with her artistic practice. She is married to Jim, who is frequently mentioned in the context of her travels and gallery operations[10] . Together they have navigated the challenges of a professional artistic career, including the physical construction of her gallery. Laking is also a mother to three children 13, including a daughter, Danica[12] . Her family often features in the periphery of her work, from the logistics of travel to the subject matter of her photography.


**2\. Artistic Style, Mediums, and Philosophy**

Laking’s artistic identity is multifaceted, spanning diverse mediums and grounded in a rigorous philosophical approach to observation. While she is best known as a watercolorist, her output includes oil painting, acrylics, printmaking, and fiber arts.

### 2.1 The Philosophy of "Seeing" vs. "Looking"
A central tenet of Laking’s artistic philosophy is the distinction between the passive act of "looking" and the active, cognitive discipline of "seeing." This distinction is not merely semantic but forms the core of her instructional and operational methodology. In an essay dedicated to her work, it was noted that Laking "observes the world around her with attentiveness, passion and keen interest," and that this "ability to see distinguishes her as an exceptional artist"[7] .

Laking advocates for a process she describes as "noticing what you see." This involves stripping away intellectual preconceptions—the idea of what a "tree" or "ocean" looks like—and instead engaging with the raw visual data of light, shadow, and color relationship[7] . When instructing others, she urges them to "just notice what you see and then draw it," emphasizing that in a "rushed world," the capacity to register visual impressions is a cultivated skill[7] .

Her subject matter is characterized by "representational realism," yet it is democratic in its scope. Laking finds aesthetic value in conventional beauty as well as the mundane or industrial, stating that she sees "beauty in shapes and colours and contrasts whether it be traditional beauty or the back of a garbage truck"[2] . This willingness to elevate the ordinary through careful observation is a hallmark of her realist approach.

### 2.2 Technical Mastery in Watercolor
Laking’s reputation was built on her watercolor landscapes, and her technique is noted for its divergence from traditional methods. Standard watercolor pedagogy typically dictates working from light to dark to preserve the luminosity of the paper for highlights. Laking, however, employs a method that works the "opposite from the usual approach," often laying in dark pigments first[10] .

She describes this idiosyncratic process as "kind of magic," noting that "you put a hint of colour here and there and suddenly the paper turns into a flower"[10] . By establishing her darks early, Laking achieves a depth and contrast that gives her watercolors a structural solidity often lacking in the medium. This technique allows for "spontaneity and lightness," particularly in her plein air work, where speed and decisiveness are required[14] .

### 2.3 The Plein Air Discipline
Laking is a devoted practitioner of *en plein air* painting, a method that requires the artist to work outdoors, directly in front of the subject, regardless of environmental conditions. Her stamina and commitment to this practice are well-documented. She is known to hike with a backpack and stool to remote locations, remaining in the same spot for up to six hours to capture the shifting light and tides[14] .

This direct engagement with nature allows her to capture the specific atmospheric conditions of the Bay of Fundy. She has spent years painting the tides, a subject that demands rapid execution and keen observation of changing water levels[2] . Her plein air work is characterized by an "engaging spontaneity," capturing the immediate energy of the landscape[14] .

### 2.4 Studio Practice and "Winter Works"
Laking’s practice is seasonally bifurcated. While the warmer months are dedicated to plein air painting, the winter months necessitate a retreat to the studio. This shift in environment precipitates a shift in process.

* **Methodology:** In the studio, Laking utilizes sketches and photographs taken from various angles to compose larger, more complex works. She engages in a process of "rearranging them and looking to see if a composition for a painting might emerge"[14] .  
* **Timeframe:** Unlike the immediate execution of plein air work, studio paintings may take days, weeks, or even months to resolve.  
* **Stylistic Shift:** Studio works often become "more generalized, less realistic" as the scale increases, allowing Laking to explore composition and abstraction more freely than in her location work[14] .

### 2.5 Diversification of Mediums: The 2007 "Explosion"
While watercolor defined her early career, Laking has consistently expanded her technical repertoire. In 2007, following her recovery from a serious health issue and inspired by extensive travel and a new marriage, she experienced what she described as an "explosion of creativity"[14] . This period marked a significant broadening of her artistic output:

* **Oils and Acrylics:** She began working increasingly in oils and acrylics, enjoying the "slippery delicious medium" of oil sticks and the ability to "push and shove" the painting into being[2] .  
* **Printmaking:** The gallery features her handmade serigraphs, demonstrating her skill in print media[5] .  
* **Fiber Arts:** Laking engages in dying wool, hooking, and felting. She hooked her first rug, *Full and Starry Night over Five Islands*, during a two-month road trip to Alaska[10] .  
* **Photography:** Laking has also exhibited photography, notably in the 2007 exhibition *Here and Away — Seeing with Passionate Eyes*. Her photography is noted for focusing on people and movement, a subject matter often absent from her landscape paintings[14] .


**3\. The Joy Laking Gallery: A Model of Independence**

### 3.1 Establishment and Infrastructure
In the late 1980s, Laking and her husband took the significant step of establishing the **Joy Laking Gallery** at their home in Portapique, Nova Scotia[8] . Located at 6730 Highway 2, the gallery is a physical manifestation of Laking’s commitment to living and working in rural Nova Scotia[1] . The gallery was purpose-built to display her work, allowing her to bypass the constraints of the traditional gallery system and connect directly with her audience.

### 3.2 Operations and Commercial Activity
The gallery operates seasonally and by appointment, reflecting the rhythms of Laking’s painting schedule.

* **Summer Operations:** It is typically open daily from late June until early September[1] .  
* **Off-Season:** The gallery remains accessible "by chance or by appointment" throughout the rest of the year[1] .  
* **Inventory:** The space features a comprehensive collection of her output, including original paintings, serigraphs, prints, greeting cards, and her published books[1] .

The gallery also functions as a community hub, hosting events such as book launches where Laking serves tea and signs copies of her works, fostering a personal connection with her collectors[15] .

### 4.2 Group Exhibitions and Collaborative Projects
Laking has been a key figure in collaborative efforts to promote Nova Scotian art.

* Capture 2014: Nova Scotia Realism (2014):  
  Laking was a driving force behind this pivotal exhibition at the Dalhousie University Art Gallery. Curated by Tom Smart, the exhibition sought to validate and celebrate the realist tradition in Nova Scotia. Laking exhibited alongside notable peers such as Tom Forrestall, Steven Rhude, Shelley Mitchell, Paul Hannon, Susan Paterson, Ed Huner, and Gord MacDonald[2] . The exhibition opened in January 2014 and ran until March[2] .  
* PLANS Touring Exhibitions (2014):  
  Following the Capture 2014 show, the PLANS group continued to exhibit collectively. In April 2014, they exhibited at the Nova Scotia Archives, followed by shows in Truro and Inverness in September and October of that year[2] .  
* Painting in Situ (2015):  
  Laking participated in the "Painting in Situ" event in Parrsboro, NS. This plein air painting event and subsequent exhibition featured Laking alongside artists Susan Paterson, Susan Tooke, Richard Rudnicki, and Bill Rogers[18] .  
* Elizabeth Bishop House Residency (2021):  
  In February 2021, Laking and fellow artist Susan Paterson spent a week in residence at the Elizabeth Bishop House in Great Village, NS. Due to extreme cold, they focused on painting the interior of the historic home, capturing the light through lace curtains and the "warmth, coziness and history" of the space[19] .


**5\. Advocacy for Realism: PLANS**

A defining aspect of Laking’s later career is her activism on behalf of the realist style. Around 2009-2010, Laking became a founding member of **PLANS (Professional Living Artists of Nova Scotia)**.2

### 5.1 The Mission of PLANS
The formation of PLANS was a direct response to a perceived marginalization of realism within the established art world. Laking and her peers felt that realism was "discriminated against" by public galleries and curators who favored conceptual and abstract modalities[4] . The group’s objective was to advocate for the validity, technical difficulty, and contemporary relevance of realist art.

### 5.2 Impact
The efforts of PLANS culminated in the *Capture 2014* exhibition, which brought institutional recognition to the movement. By organizing and exhibiting collectively, Laking and her colleagues succeeded in creating a platform for realism that might not have been achieved individually.


**6\. Literary Contributions and Illustration**

Laking’s creative output extends beyond the easel into the realm of publishing. She has had a significant career as both an illustrator and an author.

### 6.1 Early Illustration Work
In the late 1970s and early 1980s, Laking illustrated children's books for the Nova Scotia Department of Lands and Forests. These works were often educational, dealing with environmental themes.

* ***The Brook and the Woodcutter*** (1979): Written by Gary Saunders and illustrated by Laking[20] . This book is noted as an "early environmental warning," reflecting a conservationist ethos[14] .  
* ***The Man Who Couldn't Stop Sneezing*** (1982): Also written by Gary Saunders and illustrated by Laking[20] .

Despite enjoying the illustrative process, Laking found the financial compensation inadequate for a full-time career. Consequently, she pivoted towards self-publishing calendars of her paintings, which proved to be a more sustainable revenue stream and helped popularize her imagery[8] .

### 6.2 Authored Publications
In recent years, Laking has published books that synthesize her visual art with her writing, offering a more holistic view of her creative vision.

#### *The Painted Province: Nova Scotia Through an Artist's Eyes*** **(2020)
Published by Pottersfield Press, this book is arguably Laking’s magnum opus in print.

* **Scope:** The book features over 200 paintings (oil, acrylic, and watercolor) depicting 40 different locations across Nova Scotia, from the southwestern shores of Meteghan to the highlands of Neil's Harbour[8] .  
* **Design and Utility:** Laking designed the book with a specific utility in mind. It is tablet-sized (9.75 x 6.75 inches) to fit conveniently in a car’s glove compartment[8] . Furthermore, she included GPS coordinates for the location of each painting, effectively turning the art book into a travel guide that encourages families to explore the province[8] .  
* **Narrative Element:** The visual content is accompanied by "word pictures"—Laking’s poetic descriptions of the locations and her painting process[10] .  
* **Reception:** Reviewers praised the book for its ability to evoke the specific "feeling" of rural Nova Scotia, with one reviewer noting that the images transported them back to their childhood[22] .

#### *The Colours of Winter*** **(2019)
Also published by Pottersfield Press, this children's book focuses on the winter landscape[5] . It reflects Laking’s long-standing fascination with winter light and snow, themes she explored in her *Winter Work* exhibition at the AGNS.


**7\. Social Advocacy and Theatre: *Invisible Prisons***

Perhaps the most striking divergence from her visual work is Laking’s contribution to social theatre. In response to the pervasive issue of domestic violence, Laking wrote the play ***Invisible Prisons***.

### 7.1 Origin and Content
*Invisible Prisons* is a collection of thirteen monologues based on true stories[3] . Laking interviewed 14 women (and two men) to construct a narrative that exposes the complex mechanics of abuse. The play illustrates how "links of alienation, domination, isolation, and violation" are forged to create an "invisible prison" around the victim[3] .

### 7.2 Production and Impact
* **Premiere:** The play was performed in Truro in March 2013, directed by Bryden MacDonald and produced by Lenore Zann, utilizing members of the Truro Theatre Society[2] .  
* **Continued Relevance:** The play continued to be produced in 2014[23] .  
* **Educational Use:** In 2020, the play was performed virtually by the Zucker School of Medicine in New York, demonstrating its relevance beyond the local context[19] . Laking has made the script available for free to community groups, schools, and police departments, viewing the work as a tool for education and social change rather than a commercial product[3] .


**8\. Community Leadership and Board Service**

Laking has maintained a rigorous commitment to community service, serving on the boards of diverse cultural and social organizations. Her leadership roles reflect her dual interests in the arts and social welfare.

### 8.1 Institutional Boards
Laking has served on the governing bodies of the following organizations:

* **Art Gallery of Nova Scotia (AGNS):** Served two terms on the Board of Governors[6] .  
* **Robert Pope Foundation:** Board member since 1997\. This foundation explores the intersection of art and medicine[7] .

* **Ship's Company Theatre:** Board member[2] .  
* **Ivar Mendez International Foundation:** Board member[2] .

### 8.2 Social Welfare Organizations
* **Third Place Transition House:** Laking has served on the board of this organization, which supports women and children fleeing violence, aligning with the themes of her play *Invisible Prisons*.2

### 8.3 Community Events
Laking is a regular participant in the **Terry Fox Run** in Great Village, Nova Scotia. She has been noted as an active supporter of this cancer research fundraiser for many years, contributing to the community spirit of the event[4] .

**11\. Conclusion**

Joy Snihur Wyatt Laking’s career is a testament to the power of sustained observation and community engagement. From her early decision to pursue art in the wake of personal tragedy to her decades-long documentation of the Bay of Fundy, Laking has built a body of work that is both aesthetically rigorous and deeply rooted in place. Her innovations in watercolor technique, her advocacy for the realist tradition through PLANS, and her courageous exploration of social issues through *Invisible Prisons* mark her as a significant cultural figure in Atlantic Canada. Through her gallery, her books, and her teaching, she continues to invite the public not just to look at Nova Scotia, but to truly *see* it.
