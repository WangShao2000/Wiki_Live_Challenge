# Skiddaw Group Site of Special Scientific Interest (SSSI): A Comprehensive Geological, Ecological, and Management Review
## Executive Summary
The Skiddaw Group Site of Special Scientific Interest (SSSI) represents one of the most significant geological and ecological conservation areas in the United Kingdom. Spanning approximately 10,256.3 hectares in the northern Lake District, Cumbria, this designated site encompasses a diverse upland landscape defined by the oldest sedimentary rocks in the region, the Skiddaw Group, and the mineralogically unique Caldbeck Fells. As a component of the Lake District High Fells Special Area of Conservation (SAC), the site is of international importance, protecting extensive tracts of blanket bog, upland heath, and montane habitats, alongside a rich industrial heritage of mining.

This report provides an exhaustive analysis of the Skiddaw Group SSSI, synthesizing geological stratigraphy, structural evolution, mineralogical diversity, ecological assemblages, and contemporary conservation management strategies. The analysis highlights the complex interplay between the site's deep-time geological origins—dating back to the Ordovician period—and the modern biological systems that overlay them. Furthermore, it examines the critical management interventions undertaken by Natural England, the Lake District National Park Authority (LDNPA), and other stakeholders to address the condition of the site, which currently balances between recovery and persistent unfavourable status due to historical pressures such as overgrazing and non-native forestry.

**1\. Introduction and Site Designation**

### 1.1 Geographic and Topographic Context
The Skiddaw Group SSSI is situated in the northern sector of the English Lake District National Park. It forms an irregular circular massif centred roughly near Great Calva, covering an official area of 10,256.3 hectares (approximately 39.6 square miles)[1] . The site is characterized by its high relief, with elevations ranging from a minimum of 126 meters to a maximum of 930 meters at the summit of Skiddaw itself[1] .

The topography is distinct from the rugged, craggy profiles of the central Lake District (dominated by the Borrowdale Volcanic Group). Instead, the Skiddaw Group landscape is defined by smooth, steep-sided convex slopes and rounded peaks. This morphological contrast is a direct result of the underlying geology; the sedimentary mudstones and siltstones of the Skiddaw Group weather more uniformly than the hard volcanic rocks to the south[2] . Prominent fells within the SSSI boundary include Skiddaw, Blencathra (Saddleback), High Pike, Carrock Fell, Knott, Great Calva, and Bowscale Fell[1] .

Hydrologically, the high ground of the SSSI functions as a major regional watershed. It separates the catchment of the River Caldew, which flows northward towards Carlisle, from the catchments of the River Ellen and the River Derwent, which flow westward towards the Irish Sea coast at Maryport and Workington respectively[1] . The site includes critical headwaters such as Dash Beck, Glenderaterra Beck, and the Glenderamackin, all of which contribute to the wider river systems of Cumbria[1] .

### 1.2 Statutory Designations
The site acts as a convergence point for multiple layers of conservation legislation, reflecting its value across different scientific disciplines.

* **Site of Special Scientific Interest (SSSI):** Notified under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, the site is designated for both its biological features (upland habitats, breeding birds, invertebrates) and its geological features (stratigraphy, igneous petrology, mineralogy)[1] .  
* **Special Area of Conservation (SAC):** The Skiddaw Group SSSI is a constituent component of the **Lake District High Fells SAC**. This European-level designation specifically recognizes the global importance of its habitats, including Northern Atlantic wet heaths, European dry heaths, Alpine and Boreal heaths, and blanket bogs[1] .  
* **Geological Conservation Review (GCR):** The SSSI contains numerous GCR sites, which are specific localities identified as being of national or international importance for earth science. These include sites for the Mineralogy of the Lake District, the Caledonian Igneous province, and Ordovician stratigraphy[1] .  
* **National Park and World Heritage Site:** Situated wholly within the Lake District National Park, the site is also part of the English Lake District World Heritage Site, inscribed as a "cultural landscape" in 2017\. This designation acknowledges the interaction between human activity (such as mining and farming) and the natural environment[5] .


**2\. Geological Framework: The Skiddaw Group**

The geological core of the SSSI is the Skiddaw Group, a thick sequence of marine sedimentary rocks that constitutes the oldest geological unit in the Lake District. These rocks provide the fundamental evidence for the early Paleozoic history of Northern England, recording depositional environments on the margin of the micro-continent Avalonia[6] .

### 2.1 Stratigraphy and Sedimentology
The Skiddaw Group ranges in age from the **Tremadoc Series** (early Ordovician) to the **Llanvirn Series**.7 The total thickness of the group is estimated to be approximately 5,000 meters, although intense folding makes precise measurements difficult[8] . The lithology is dominated by laminated siltstones and mudstones, representing turbidite deposits formed by sediment-laden currents flowing into a deep ocean basin[2] .

Recent resurveys by the British Geological Survey have established a formal lithostratigraphy for the group, replacing older, obsolete terms like "Skiddaw Slates." The succession is divided into distinct formations, described below in ascending stratigraphic order:

#### 2.1.1 Bitter Beck Formation
The Bitter Beck Formation represents the oldest exposed part of the sequence. It consists primarily of dark gray to black mudstones and siltstones. Acritarch biostratigraphy, specifically the presence of *Stellechinatum sicaforme*, helps to date this formation to the early Ordovician (Tremadoc)[6] .

#### 2.1.2 Watch Hill Formation
Overlying the Bitter Beck Formation is the Watch Hill Formation. This unit is characterized by a coarser influx of sediment, including greywacke sandstone beds interbedded with siltstones. It has yielded important fossils such as the graptolite *Araneograptus murrayi* and the acritarch *Cymatiogalea messaoudensis*.6 The *A. murrayi* Biozone is the only recognizable upper Tremadocian graptoloid biozone in the British sequence, marking the Watch Hill Formation as a key biostratigraphic marker[10] .

#### 2.1.3 Hope Beck Formation
The Hope Beck Formation marks a return to finer-grained sedimentation, comprising laminated mudstones with occasional siltstone laminae. Petrographical analysis of this formation distinguishes it from the units above and below[9] . Fossils found here include Pliomerid trilobites, indicating a marine environment that supported benthic arthropod life[6] .

#### 2.1.4 Loweswater Formation
The Loweswater Formation represents a significant event in the basin's history, characterized by a thick sequence of greywacke sandstones[7] . These coarser sediments suggest a period of active tectonism or sea-level change that increased sediment supply to the deep basin. The formation serves as a major structural unit due to its competence (hardness) compared to the surrounding mudstones. Graptolites such as *Didymograptus deflexus* have been recovered from this formation at localities like Barf, confirming an Arenig age[6] .

#### 2.1.5 Kirk Stile Formation
The youngest unit in the main Skiddaw inlier is the Kirk Stile Formation, which consists of slates exposed just below the summit ridge of Skiddaw[8] . These rocks are typically thinly laminated siltstones and mudstones. They are unconformably overlain by the volcanic rocks of the Eycott Volcanic Group, marking the end of the deep marine sedimentation phase and the onset of subaerial volcanism[7] .

### 2.2 The Buttermere Olistostrome
A unique and chaotic geological unit within the Skiddaw Group is the **Buttermere Formation**. Unlike the coherent formations described above, the Buttermere Formation is an **olistostrome**—a massive submarine landslide deposit[11] .

It comprises disrupted, sheared, and folded olistoliths (blocks) of mudstone, siltstone, and sandstone turbidites set within a mudstone matrix. The formation is estimated to be over 1,500 meters thick[11] . It contains two distinct members:

1. **Goat Gills Member:** A sedimentary breccia.  
2. **Robinson Member:** A sandstone-dominated unit[11] .

The emplacement of the Buttermere Olistostrome is inferred to have occurred in the late Arenig period, likely triggered by tectonic instability associated with the uplift of the Avalonian margin[9] . The GCR site at Hassness and Goat Crags provides a critical exposure of this formation, displaying a major anticline where the uninverted limb has suffered severe extension, creating mélange lithologies[12] .

While the Skiddaw Group is often described as "poorly fossiliferous" compared to younger limestone sequences, it contains a scientifically vital assemblage of microfossils and macrofossils that allow for international correlation of the Lower Ordovician.

**3\. Structural Geology and Tectonics**

The Skiddaw Group has undergone a complex polyphase deformation history, distinct from the overlying volcanic groups. This structural complexity is a key element of its GCR designation.

### 3.1 Deformation Phases
The rocks exhibit structures formed during the Caledonian Orogeny, specifically related to the closure of the Iapetus Ocean. The structural style is dominated by intense folding and the development of slaty cleavage, which historically gave rise to the term "Skiddaw Slates"[7] . The deformation in the Skiddaw Group contrasts with the overlying Borrowdale Volcanic Group; this difference is attributed to the "rheological" contrast between the soft mudstones and the rigid volcanic pile, as well as the impedance of thrusting by the Lake District batholith[15] .

### 3.2 Major Faulting
The **Causey Pike Fault** is a major structural boundary within the SSSI. It acts as a lineament that juxtaposes different stratigraphical successions of the Skiddaw Group against one another[16] . To the north of this fault lies the main Skiddaw inlier, while distinct successions are found to the south.

### 3.3 Metamorphism and the Skiddaw Granite
The **Skiddaw Granite** is a Devonian intrusion that does not outcrop extensively but lies shallowly beneath the surface of the SSSI. Its presence is marked by a distinct thermal aureole where the surrounding Skiddaw Group sediments have been metamorphosed into **hornfels**.17

* **Chiastolite Slate:** In the outer zones of the aureole, the mudstones contain metamorphic minerals like chiastolite (andalusite).  
* **Cordierite Hornfels:** Closer to the contact, the rocks are recrystallized into hard, spotted rocks containing cordierite.  
* **Grainsgill Greisen:** At the Grainsgill GCR site, the granite itself is modified by metasomatism into "greisen" (a rock composed of quartz and mica), associated with tungsten mineralization[18] .


**4\. Mineralogy: The Caldbeck Fells**

Within the Skiddaw Group SSSI lies the **Caldbeck Fells**, an area internationally renowned for its mineralogical diversity. This area contains six distinct Mineralogy GCR sites and has yielded over 175 validated mineral species, many of which are rare supergene minerals found nowhere else in the UK[4] . The mineralization is genetically linked to the hydrothermal systems associated with the Skiddaw Granite and the Carrock Fell Complex.

### 4.1 Carrock Mine and Grainsgill
Located in the Caldew Valley, this site exposes the contact between the Skiddaw Granite and the Skiddaw Group, as well as the gabbros of the Carrock Fell Complex.

* **Vein System:** The area is cut by north-south trending quartz veins, most notably the **Smith**, **Harding**, and **Emerson** veins[18] .  
* **Primary Ore Minerals:** These veins were historically worked for tungsten, containing wolframite ($\[Fe,Mn\]WO\_4$) and scheelite ($CaWO\_4$). Other primary sulfides include arsenopyrite, pyrite, pyrrhotite, sphalerite, and molybdenite[18] .  
* **Rare Accessory Minerals:** The site has produced rare bismuth minerals such as bismuthinite and joseite ($Bi\_4TeS\_2$), as well as native gold[18] .  
* **Alteration:** The gabbro wall rock adjacent to the veins shows intense hydrothermal alteration to quartz, biotite, and sericite (greisenization)[18] .

### 4.2 Red Gill Mine
Red Gill Mine is perhaps the most famous mineralogical locality in the fells, designated as a GCR site for its supergene mineral assemblage.

* **Primary Mineralogy:** The veins contain chalcopyrite, galena, and sphalerite.  
* **Supergene Mineralogy:** The oxidized zone of the veins is exceptionally rich in secondary copper and lead minerals. It is the **type locality** for **Redgillite** ($Cu\_6(OH)\_{10}(SO\_4) \\cdot H\_2O$), a mineral discovered here and named after the mine[19] .  
* **Other Notable Species:** The site provided the first British occurrence of **queitite** and yields rare species like **macphersonite** and **mattheddleite**.19  
* **Scientific Caution:** Historical records from this site are complicated by the legacy of A.W.G. Kingsbury, a collector who is now known to have falsified localities for many rare species. However, modern research has validated many of the unique minerals found at Red Gill[19] .

### 4.3 Roughtongill and Dry Gill
* **Roughtongill:** Known for its complex primary sulfide assemblage and colorful supergene zones containing **linarite** (a deep blue lead-copper sulfate) and **caledonite**.20  
* **Dry Gill:** The Dry Gill vein differs from others in the abundance of **barite** ($BaSO\_4$) alongside lead arsenates and phosphates (e.g., mimetite/campylite). Research suggests barite was once more widespread in the Caldbeck Fells but has been replaced by quartz in many veins[21] .

### 4.4 Mineralogical Conservation
The mineral wealth of the Caldbeck Fells has made it a target for intense collecting, leading to the depletion of accessible mine dumps and outcrops. To address this, the SSSI designation imposes strict controls. The Lake District National Park Authority (LDNPA) and Natural England operate a permit system, allowing collection only for valid scientific research, thereby prohibiting commercial or recreational mass-collection[22] .


**5\. Ecological Assets and Biodiversity**

The biological importance of the Skiddaw Group SSSI matches its geological significance. The site protects a vast expanse of upland habitats that are representative of the oceanic climate of northwest England.

### 5.1 Habitats and Vegetation Communities
The site's vegetation is classified under the National Vegetation Classification (NVC) system and includes several Annex I habitats listed under the EU Habitats Directive (via the Lake District High Fells SAC).

#### 5.1.1 Upland Heathland
Heathland covers large areas of the lower to mid-altitude slopes.

* **Northern Atlantic Wet Heaths:** dominated by cross-leaved heath (*Erica tetralix*), typically found on wetter, peaty soils (SAC code 4010)[1] .  
* **European Dry Heaths:** dominated by heather (*Calluna vulgaris*), bilberry (*Vaccinium myrtillus*), and crowberry (*Empetrum nigrum*) (SAC code 4030).  
* **Management:** Historically, these heaths have been suppressed by overgrazing, converting them to acid grassland. Restoration projects, such as those at **Dodd Wood** and **Caldbeck Common**, aim to reduce grazing pressure and remove invasive conifers to allow heathland regeneration[5] .

#### 5.1.2 Blanket Bog
Blanket bogs are found on the flatter plateaus and cols where peat has accumulated.

* **Extent:** Significant areas exist, such as the 69 hectares of bog around **Bannerdale Beck** (SSSI Unit 35)[1] .  
* **Vegetation:** Characterized by hare's-tail cottongrass (*Eriophorum vaginatum*), deergrass (*Trichophorum germanicum*), and various *Sphagnum* mosses (SAC code 7130)[1] .  
* **Condition:** Many bogs are fragmented or degraded due to historical drainage (grips). Restoration involves blocking these drains to re-wet the peat, a process crucial for carbon sequestration and water regulation[24] .

#### 5.1.3 Montane Habitats
On the highest summits (e.g., Skiddaw, Helvellyn range outliers), the vegetation transitions to **Alpine and Boreal heaths** (SAC code 4060).

* **Siliceous Scree:** The steep, unstable slopes support specialized pioneer communities including alpine rock-jasmine (*Androsace alpina*) and red hemp-nettle (*Galeopsis ladanum*) (SAC code 8110)[1] .  
* **Lichen Heaths:** High-altitude areas support *Racomitrium* moss heaths and rich lichen assemblages. Notable records include *Cladonia rangiferina* (reindeer moss) at Carl Side and *Cladonia callosa* at Carrock Fell[25] .

#### 5.1.4 Woodlands
While the fells are largely treeless, specific areas of woodland are conservation features.

* **Juniper Scrub:** *Juniperus communis* formations are found in fragmented stands (SAC code 5130)[1] .  
* **Upland Oak Woodland:** Remnant patches of sessile oak woodland exist, such as **Young Wood** near Mungrisdale. This site has been fenced to exclude grazing, allowing natural regeneration of the canopy and ground flora[26] .  
* **Dodd Wood:** A Forestry England site where non-native conifers are being selectively removed to restore ancient semi-natural woodland boundaries and heathland transitions[5] .

### 5.2 Fauna
The SSSI supports a range of fauna adapted to the upland environment.

* **Invertebrates:** The invertebrate assemblage is a notified SSSI feature.  
  * **Beetles:** The site supports the endangered ground beetle ***Leistus montanus***, found on the high ridges of Skiddaw[27] . Riverine sediments in the Caldew catchment host the rove beetle ***Thinobius newberyi***.27  
  * **Butterflies:** The Mountain Ringlet (*Erebia epiphron*) depends on the mat-grass (*Nardus stricta*) found on the fell sides[28] .  
* **Birds:** The mosaic of heath and bog supports breeding waders and raptors. Key species include the Golden Plover (*Pluvialis apricaria*), which breeds on the plateau bogs, and the Ring Ouzel (*Turdus torquatus*) in the craggy gills. The SSSI is also frequented by Golden Eagles (*Aquila chrysaetos*) and Merlins (*Falco columbarius*)[28] .  
* **Fish:** The rivers draining the massif (Caldew, Derwent) are designated for populations of **Sea Lamprey** (*Petromyzon marinus*) and **River Lamprey** (*Lampetra fluviatilis*), which spawn in the clean, gravelly headwaters[29] .


**6\. Site Condition and Conservation Management**

### 6.1 Condition Assessment
Natural England periodically assesses the condition of the SSSI units. The site faces significant challenges, primarily related to historical land management. As of recent reporting:

* **Favourable:** \~4.42%  
* **Unfavourable – Recovering:** \~46.36%  
* **Unfavourable – No Change:** \~49.22%.29

The high percentage of "Unfavourable – Recovering" reflects the implementation of long-term management agreements (e.g., Higher Level Stewardship) that have yet to yield full ecological recovery, which can take decades in upland environments.

### 6.2 Key Pressures and Threats
1. **Overgrazing:** Historically high densities of sheep have suppressed the growth of dwarf shrubs (heather, bilberry) and prevented tree regeneration. This has led to the conversion of heathland into species-poor acid grassland[23] .  
2. **Drainage:** Past agricultural practices involved cutting drainage ditches ("grips") into peat bogs to improve grazing. This dried out the peat, leading to erosion, carbon loss, and the loss of bog-specialist plants[24] .  
3. **Invasive Species:** Non-native conifers (Sitka spruce) regenerate from adjacent plantations into the SSSI. In water bodies, invasive plants like New Zealand pygmyweed (*Crassula helmsii*) pose a threat to native aquatic flora[30] .  
4. **Recreational Pressure:** Intense foot traffic on popular peaks (Skiddaw, Blencathra) causes path erosion, widening scars on the landscape and damaging sensitive scree and vegetation[31] .

### 6.3 Management Interventions and Restoration
A coordinated effort involving Natural England, the LDNPA, Forestry England, and the National Trust (who own Skiddaw Forest) is underway to address these threats.

* **Peatland Restoration:** A major project in **Skiddaw Forest** aims to restore peat bogs by blocking 16 miles of drainage channels. This "rewetting" raises the water table, encouraging *Sphagnum* growth and reducing flood risk downstream[24] .  
* **Caldbeck Common Fencing:** A pilot project on Caldbeck Common involves fencing off 36.2 hectares of SSSI land for five years. This temporary exclusion of livestock is designed to allow suppressed heathland plants to flower and set seed, restoring the seed bank and vegetation structure[23] .  
* **Forestry Restructuring:** The **Dodd Wood Forest Design Plan** outlines the removal of natural regeneration of conifers from the SSSI and SAC designated areas. The plan aims to "feather" the forest edge, creating a natural transition from high forest to open moorland[5] .  
* **Fix the Fells:** This partnership project repairs upland paths to contain foot traffic. Techniques include stone pitching and landscaping to prevent hikers from trampling wide swathes of habitat[31] .  
* **Invasive Species Control:** Active monitoring and removal programs are in place for species like *Crassula helmsii* and *Nuttall’s pondweed* in the site's water bodies[30] .


**7\. Socio-Economic and Hydrological Significance**

### 7.1 Hydrology and Flood Risk
The Skiddaw Group SSSI plays a vital role in regional water management. The peat soils act as a sponge, storing water during heavy rainfall and releasing it slowly.

* **Flood Attenuation:** Degraded peat releases water rapidly, contributing to flash flooding in downstream towns like Keswick and Cockermouth. Restoration of the bogs is a key "Nature-Based Solution" for flood risk management in the Derwent and Eden catchments[29] .  
* **Water Quality:** Erosion from damaged peat and mineral soils increases particulate loads in rivers. This sedimentation threatens the spawning grounds of lampreys and salmonids and contributes to the siltation of **Bassenthwaite Lake**, a site struggling with eutrophication and sediment accumulation[5] .

### 7.2 Mining Heritage and Industrial Archaeology
The Caldbeck Fells are not only geological sites but also monuments of industrial history. Mining for lead, copper, and tungsten occurred here from the 16th century through to the mid-20th century (e.g., Carrock Mine produced tungsten during both World Wars).

* **Archaeology:** The remains of mine workings, processing mills, and leats are preserved as part of the cultural landscape. The impact of mining is visible in the spoil heaps, which are now themselves distinct habitats for metallophyte (metal-tolerant) plants and lichens[32] .  
* **Hazards:** The legacy of mining includes heavy metal contamination in runoff (acid mine drainage), which is monitored to prevent pollution of the river systems[33] .

### 7.3 Common Land and Farming
Much of the SSSI is registered Common Land (e.g., Caldbeck Common, Uldale Common). This confers rights to local commoners (farmers) to graze livestock. Conservation management relies on the cooperation of these commoners; effectively, they are the land managers. Finding a balance between the economic needs of hill farming (maintaining flock sizes) and ecological needs (reducing grazing pressure) is the central challenge of managing the SSSI[23] .

**Conclusion**

The Skiddaw Group SSSI is a landscape of dualities: it is a site of deep geological time and active biological processes; a place of industrial exploitation and careful conservation; and a rugged wilderness that is heavily managed by human intervention. Its geological strata tell the story of the lost Iapetus Ocean, while its mineral veins reveal the fiery magmatic history of the Lake District. Ecologically, it is a vital refuge for threatened upland species, from the rare *Leistus* beetle to the expansive blanket bogs that regulate the region's carbon and water cycles.

The current state of the SSSI highlights the difficulties of reversing centuries of intensive land use. While nearly half the site is in a state of recovery, a significant portion remains in unfavourable condition, underscoring the need for continued and enhanced management efforts. Projects like the peat restoration in Skiddaw Forest and the grazing exclusions on Caldbeck Common demonstrate a commitment to this recovery. Ultimately, the Skiddaw Group SSSI serves as a critical natural asset, safeguarding the geological heritage and ecological future of Northern England.
