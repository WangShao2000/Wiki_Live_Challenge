# The Banshee (poetry collection)
**The Banshee** (full title: **The Banshee and Other Poems**) is a collection of poetry by the Irish writer and physician(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Todhunter), first published in 1888 by[1] . The volume is widely recognized by literary historians and critics as a foundational text of the(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish\_Literary\_Revival), representing a pivotal moment in the transition of Anglo-Irish literature from the romanticism of the mid-nineteenth century toward the distinct national mythology that characterized the fin de siècle[4] .

Dedicated to the historian and mythologist(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standish\_O%27Grady), whose prose reconstructions of Ireland's heroic past deeply influenced Todhunter, the collection marks the author's departure from the Shelleyan and classical themes of his earlier works, such as *Laurella* (1876) and *Forest Songs* (1881), in favor of subjects drawn from [Irish mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_mythology), folklore, and history[6] . The collection includes several of Todhunter's most enduring compositions, including the title poem "The Banshee," the bardic narrative "The Doom of the Children of Lir," and the ballad "Aghadoe," which became a staple of Irish recitation and folk song[6] .

Upon its publication, *The Banshee* elicited significant critical discourse, notably from prominent figures of the emerging Irish literary canon.(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._B._Yeats) hailed the title poem as a "noble chant over the sorrows of Ireland," comparing its free verse structure to the work of(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walt_Whitman), while(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscar_Wilde) provided a mixed but attentive review in *(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Woman%27s_World)*, praising the work's "grave and chastened beauty" while critiquing the experimental metrical forms of the longer narratives[9] .

## Background and Context
### Authorial Evolution and the Turn to Ireland
John Todhunter (1839–1916) was born in Dublin into a Quaker merchant family. Educated at Trinity College Dublin, where he studied medicine and won the Vice-Chancellor’s Prize for English Verse, he initially established himself as a figure of the English Romantic tradition[7] . His early poetry, deeply indebted to(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Percy_Bysshe_Shelley), focused on classical aesthetics, pastoral idylls, and philosophical contemplation, showing little engagement with the political or cultural dynamics of his native Ireland[5] .

The trajectory of Todhunter's work shifted dramatically in the 1880s, a period coinciding with the nascent stages of the Irish Literary Revival. This movement sought to create a distinctive Irish literature in the English language, rooted in the island's Gaelic heritage and mythology. Literary historians identify the publication of *The Banshee and Other Poems* as the definitive marker of Todhunter's "conversion" to this national cause[13] . Where his previous volumes had been occupied with *Alcestis* (1879) and *Helena in Troas* (1886), *The Banshee* turned its gaze toward the "Matter of Ireland," engaging with the cycles of the(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuatha_D%C3%A9_Danann) and the political sorrows of the Irish peasantry[6] .

### Influence of Standish O'Grady
The primary catalyst for this thematic transformation was the work of Standish O'Grady, particularly his seminal *History of Ireland: Heroic Period* (1878). O'Grady’s vivid, high-romantic retelling of the Cuchulainn cycle and other bardic legends electrified a generation of Irish writers, providing them with a heroic past that could rival the classical mythologies of Greece and Rome[6] .

Todhunter acknowledged this debt explicitly in the dedication of *The Banshee*, which is inscribed:

"To Standish O'Grady, whose epic History of Ireland first gave me an interest in our bardic tales." 1

Ernest Boyd, a chronicler of the Irish Literary Revival, noted that this dedication constitutes arguably the "earliest public record of the position of O'Grady in the Revival," signaling the shift of influence from English models to indigenous Irish sources[6] . By citing O'Grady, Todhunter aligned himself with the "younger generation" of revivalists like Yeats and Rolleston, despite being their senior in age[16] .

## Publication History
The collection has a distinct bibliographic history that reflects its reception and the evolving landscape of Irish publishing in the late 19th century.

### First Edition (1888)
The first edition was published in London by **Kegan Paul, Trench & Co.** in 1888\.

* **Physical Description:** It was issued as a small octavo volume. The binding consisted of green cloth, a color likely chosen for its national associations, with the title and author's name stamped in gold on the front cover and spine. The rear panel featured the publisher's monogram stamped in blind[2] .  
* **Pagination:** The volume contains x \+ 148 pages[3] .  
* **Scarcity:** Copies of the first edition are held in various special collections, though they are considered uncommon in the trade[17] .

### Second Edition (1891)
A second edition was published in 1891, this time in Dublin by **Sealy, Bryers, and Walker**.17

* **Significance:** The shift to a Dublin publisher for the second edition suggests a refocusing on the Irish readership and the collection's integration into the local literary ecosystem.  
* **Rarity:** OCLC records indicate that the second edition is significantly scarcer than the first, with fewer surviving copies in institutional libraries[17] .

### Subsequent Compilations
The narrative poems included in *The Banshee*—specifically "The Doom of the Children of Lir" and "The Lamentation for the Three Sons of Turann"—were later repurposed by Todhunter. In 1896, they were reprinted in a new volume titled *Three Irish Bardic Tales*, published by J. M. Dent. This later collection added a third poem, "The Fate of the Sons of Usna," thereby completing Todhunter’s treatment of the "Three Sorrows of Storytelling" (*Trí Truaighe na Scéalaigheachta*)[4] .

## Contents
*The Banshee* is characterized by its generic diversity, blending long narrative poems derived from medieval manuscripts with shorter lyrics, ballads, and folk-inspired verses. The collection effectively bridges the gap between the oral tradition of the *seanchaí* and the formal constraints of Victorian poetry.

### "The Banshee"
The title poem, "The Banshee," is a free-verse lyric that serves as the emotional and thematic centerpiece of the volume. It is an allegorical lament that personifies Ireland as a spectral, mourning woman—a "Banshee of the world"—whose grief encompasses the history of the nation and its diaspora[21] .

#### Textual Analysis
The poem is notable for its rejection of rhyme and standard meter, utilizing instead a "wild, irregular" rhythm that mimics the keen (caoineadh), the traditional Irish vocal lament for the dead[9] .

* **Imagery:** Todhunter depicts the spirit of Ireland sitting "spectre-thin" by the(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Shannon) or on a "melancholy isle" in the "foam-bearded Atlantic." She is described as an "aged desolation," a "mother of many children / Of children exil'd and dead"[21] .  
* **Symbolism:** The banshee is not merely a harbinger of individual death but a national symbol. Her wailing travels over the "sea of Milith" (mythological Milesians) and the "Ocean of Columbus" (the Atlantic), linking the ancient mythological past with the modern reality of emigration and the Irish diaspora[21] .  
* **Metrical Form:** The poem's structure was polarizing. W. B. Yeats admired it, describing it as "something between Walt Whitman and the Scotch Ossian"[9] . Conversely, Oscar Wilde criticized the lack of rhyme, suggesting that "to throw away... rhyme... is a wilful abdication of half the power... of verse"[22] .

#### Themes
The central theme is the universality of Irish suffering. The poem concludes with a rhetorical address to the spirit: "Wail no more, lonely one, mother of exiles... Thy sorrows are the world's, thou art no more alone; / Thy wrongs, the world's"[21] . This formulation elevates the specific political and historical trauma of Ireland to a plane of universal tragedy[23] .

### "The Doom of the Children of Lir"
This narrative poem is a retelling of *Oidheadh Chloinne Lir*, one of the "Three Sorrows of Storytelling" in the Fenian Cycle[24] .

#### Narrative Arc
The poem recounts the tragedy of the children of the sea god Lir—Fionnuala, Aodh, Fiachra, and Conn. Their jealous stepmother, Aoife, transforms them into swans, condemning them to wander the lakes and seas of Ireland for nine hundred years. They retain their human speech and reason, suffering the rigors of the elements until the arrival of Christianity in Ireland breaks the spell, leading to their death and baptism[24] .

#### Poetic Form
Todhunter composed the poem in unrhymed alexandrine quatrains (stanzas of four lines, each containing six iambic feet)[6] .

* **Critical Response:** The choice of meter was a point of contention. Critics like Ernest Boyd argued that the "rhymeless alexandrine quatrains" became "tiresome" compared to the "orthodox English Iambics" used by contemporaries like Douglas Hyde for similar subjects[6] . However, others found a "grave and chastened beauty" in the form, appropriate for the somber epic subject[11] .

#### Key Passages
The poem emphasizes the tension between the pagan past and the Christian future. It concludes with the swans hearing the bell of the monk Mochaom Og, which signals the end of their enchantment and the spiritual transformation of Ireland:

"Christ came to win the West. That grace the sacring bell / To wondering land and sea proclaimed with silver..."[24] .

### "The Lamentation for the Three Sons of Turann"
Continuing the engagement with the "Three Sorrows," this poem retells *Oidheadh Chloinne Tuireann*.

* **Plot:** The sons of Turann (Brian, Iuchar, and Iucharba) kill Cian, the father of the god Lugh. As an *eric* (blood-fine), Lugh demands they perform a series of impossible, perilous quests. They succeed but return to Ireland mortally wounded, dying upon their father's breast[25] .  
* **Structure:** Todhunter structures the poem as a lament spoken by the father, Turann, over the grave of his sons. The form attempts to capture the "cadence of ancient bardic poetry" through "unrhymed lyric measures"[7] .

### "Aghadoe"
"Aghadoe" differs significantly from the bardic narratives, taking the form of a ballad set during the(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish\_Rebellion\_of\_1798). It became the most popular and widely anthologized piece in the collection[8] .

#### Narrative and Context
The poem is a dramatic monologue spoken by a woman whose lover, a rebel fighter, is hiding in the glen of [Aghadoe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aghadoe), near Killarney.

* **Betrayal:** The lover is betrayed by the speaker's own brother, "Shaun Dhuv, my mother's son," for the reward money ("the traitor's gold")[23] .  
* **Tragedy:** The rebel is captured and beheaded. The speaker describes her harrowing journey from Mallow to Aghadoe to retrieve his head from the jail gate, burying it in a cairn to sleep "Like an Irish king"[8] .

#### Reception and Adaptation
"Aghadoe" was praised for its authentic emotional intensity and its successful use of the ballad tradition[27] . It transcended the printed page to enter the folk tradition, notably being set to music. A well-known recording was made by [Liam Clancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liam_Clancy) with the Irish Philharmonic Orchestra, cementing its status as a canonical rebel song[8] .

### Other Poems
The collection includes various shorter lyrics that reflect Todhunter's dual interest in peasant folklore and Christian mysticism.

* **"Our Lady's Exile"**: A poem that blends Catholic Marian imagery with the landscape of rural Ireland[13] .  
* **"The Fairy Foster-Mother"**: Explores the "changeling" motif, where fairies steal a human child and leave a replacement, a common theme in Irish folklore[13] .  
* **"The Coffin Ship"**: Addresses the trauma of the [Great Famine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Famine_\(Ireland\)), depicting the harrowing conditions on the emigrant ships[19] .

## Critical Reception
The publication of *The Banshee* occurred at a critical juncture in Irish literary history, and its reception reflects the debates surrounding the form and content of the new national literature.

### Contemporary Reviews
#### W. B. Yeats
The young W. B. Yeats was a vocal champion of the collection, reviewing it in multiple venues including *The Boston Pilot* and *United Ireland*.9

* **"A Noble Chant":** Yeats singled out the title poem, "The Banshee," for the highest praise, calling it "a noble chant over the sorrows of Ireland"[16] . He was particularly struck by its free-verse form, which he saw as a uniquely Irish expression comparable to the "Scotch Ossian" and the expansive lines of Walt Whitman[9] .  
* **Critique of Bardic Tales:** While he admired the ambition of the longer narrative poems, Yeats was less convinced by their form. He later remarked that Todhunter's "rhymeless alexandrine quatrains" were less effective than the work of others in the movement, finding them occasionally "tiresome"[6] . Despite this, he acknowledged Todhunter's pivotal role in "restoring to our hills and rivers their epic interest"[5] .

#### Oscar Wilde
Oscar Wilde reviewed the book in the November 1888 issue of *The Woman's World*.10

* **Aesthetic Appraisal:** Wilde praised the volume's "grave and chastened beauty" and the "grand manner" of its solemn cadences[11] .  
* **Defense of Rhyme:** However, Wilde took issue with Todhunter's abandonment of rhyme in the narrative poems. He argued that "rhyme gives architecture as well as melody to song," and that without it, parts of the work risked becoming "amorphous and inchoate" or "like misshapen prose"[11] . Despite these technical reservations, Wilde quoted the title poem at length, implicitly recognizing its power[22] .

### Modern Assessment
In the context of the(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish\_Literary\_Revival), *The Banshee* is viewed as a transitional but essential text. Historian Ernest Boyd argued that while Todhunter was not the most radical innovator of the period, *The Banshee* was his "most successful book of Irish verse"[19] . It is credited with bringing the influence of Standish O'Grady into poetry and paving the way for the more celebrated works of Yeats and [Austin Clarke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austin_Clarke_\(poet\))[5] .

The poem "Aghadoe" remains the most enduring piece from the collection, surviving in anthologies and folk memory long after the longer bardic narratives fell out of fashion[8] .

## Significance
*The Banshee* holds a specific significance in literary history as a bridge between the Anglicized romanticism of the mid-Victorian era and the Celtic Twilight. By adopting Irish subjects, Todhunter—a Trinity-educated Quaker—demonstrated the capacity of the Anglo-Irish writer to engage authentically with the Gaelic tradition[7] .

The collection also exemplifies the "Three Sorrows of Storytelling" motif that became a touchstone for the Revival. By retelling the stories of the Children of Lir and the Sons of Turann, Todhunter participated in the broader cultural project of establishing a distinct Irish epic canon, independent of English literary models[19] .
