# The Breton Civil War, 1341–1343: A Comprehensive Analysis of the Early Phase
## Introduction
The **Breton Civil War** (1341–1364), frequently referred to in historiography as the **War of the Breton Succession**, stands as a pivotal conflict within the broader tapestry of the Hundred Years' War. While the dynastic struggle for the Duchy of Brittany persisted for over two decades, the initial phase from **1341 to 1343** was arguably the most transformative, establishing the political, military, and diplomatic contours that would define the region for the remainder of the 14th century[1] . This period witnessed the rapid escalation of a local inheritance dispute into a high-stakes proxy war between the Kingdom of France, under the House of Valois, and the Kingdom of England, under the House of Plantagenet.

At its core, the conflict was precipitated by the death of Duke John III of Brittany without a direct heir, creating a power vacuum that invited competing claims from his half-brother, John of Montfort, and his niece, Joan of Penthièvre, who was married to Charles of Blois, a nephew of King Philip VI of France[3] . The war is occasionally termed the **War of the Two Joans** (*La guerre des deux Jeannes*), a moniker that underscores the extraordinary agency exercised by the female protagonists—Joanna of Flanders (wife of John of Montfort) and Joan of Penthièvre—who assumed leadership roles during the captivity or absence of their husbands[1] .

The years 1341–1343 were characterized by a fluid and volatile military environment. The conflict began with a preemptive administrative and military coup by John of Montfort, followed by a crushing French counter-offensive that seemingly decapitated the Montfortist leadership[7] . However, the resilience of the Montfortist faction, galvanized by Joanna of Flanders and sustained by English military intervention, prevented a total Valois victory. This period saw the first significant deployment of English longbow tactics on the continent at the Battle of Morlaix, foreshadowing the devastation of Crécy, and concluded with the complex diplomatic maneuvering of the Truce of Malestroit[9] .

This report provides an exhaustive examination of this critical three-year window. It analyzes the legal and feudal origins of the dispute, the strategic calculations of the English and French crowns, the granular details of the military campaigns, and the socioeconomic impact of the war on the Breton populace. By synthesizing contemporary chronicles with modern historical analysis, this document aims to elucidate how a provincial succession crisis became a fulcrum of European geopolitics.

## I. Geopolitical and Feudal Context
### The Duchy of Brittany in the 14th Century
To understand the ferocity and complexity of the civil war, one must first appreciate the unique position of the Duchy of Brittany in the 14th century. Unlike the royal demesne of France, Brittany was a semi-autonomous principality with a distinct Celtic heritage, particularly in its western regions[1] .

#### Cultural and Linguistic Divide
The duchy was effectively bipartite. The western portion, known as *Breton-Breton* or *Lower Brittany* (*Basse-Bretagne*), was culturally Celtic and linguistically Breton-speaking. The eastern portion, *Gallo-Breton* or *Upper Brittany* (*Haute-Bretagne*), including the cities of Nantes and Rennes, was Francophone and culturally aligned with the neighboring French counties of Anjou and Maine[5] . This cultural bifurcation played a subtle but significant role in the conflict, as the Montfortist cause initially found its strongest grassroots support in the Breton-speaking west—aided by the perception of their autonomy—while the Blois faction, backed by the French crown, dominated the Gallo-Breton east[5] .

#### Feudal Ambiguity and the Earldom of Richmond
The political status of the Breton dukes was a source of perennial tension. While they owed liege homage to the King of France for the Duchy of Brittany, they simultaneously held the **Earldom of Richmond** in England, a wealthy estate granted to the dukes by the English crown to ensure their neutrality or support[12] . This dual allegiance created a structural conflict of interest.

* **The French Bond:** As peers of France, the dukes were integral to the French feudal hierarchy.  
* The English Bond: The Earldom of Richmond provided significant revenue. In 1334, upon the death of John of Brittany, Earl of Richmond, the title and lands had reverted to Duke John III[12] .  
  This double feudal obligation meant that any Duke of Brittany had to navigate a precarious diplomatic tightrope. Siding too closely with France risked the confiscation of Richmond; siding with England risked the invasion of the duchy by French royal forces. This pre-existing tension paved the way for the internationalization of the succession crisis in 1341.12

### The Succession Crisis: A Legal and Dynastic Quagmire
The immediate catalyst for the war was the death of Duke John III (*Jean III le Bon*) on April 30, 1341, at Caen[1] . John III had been a capable ruler but was plagued by a complex marital history that failed to produce a legitimate heir.

#### The Matrimonial History of John III
John III married three times in an increasingly desperate attempt to secure the succession:

1. **Isabella of Valois:** Married in 1297\. She died childless in 1309[15] .  
2. **Isabella of Castile:** Married in 1310\. She died childless in 1328[15] .  
3. **Joan of Savoy:** Married in 1329\. John III died in 1341, leaving Joan a widow and the duchy without a direct heir[15] .

John III's failure to produce offspring was compounded by his intense personal animosity toward his stepmother, Yolande of Dreux, and her son, his half-brother **John of Montfort**. John III had spent much of his reign attempting to annul his father's second marriage to declare John of Montfort illegitimate, or otherwise disinherit him[15] . He famously (and controversially) attempted to bequeath the duchy to the French King Philip VI directly, a move that his own nobility vehemently rejected, fearing the loss of their traditional autonomy[15] .

#### The Competing Claims
Upon John III's death, two rival factions emerged, each grounding their claim in different interpretations of customary law:

| Claimant | Relation to Late Duke | Basis of Claim | Backing Power |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **John of Montfort** | Half-brother | **Proximity of Blood & Male Preference:** John argued that as the closest living male relative (brother vs. niece), his claim was superior. He invoked semi-Salic principles, arguing that women could not inherit the duchy[3] | **England:** Edward III supported this claim to destabilize France and gain a foothold[1] |
| **Charles of Blois** | Husband of Niece | **Representation:** Charles claimed the duchy on behalf of his wife, **Joan of Penthièvre**. Joan was the daughter of Guy, John III's younger full brother. They argued that Joan "represented" her father's rights, which took precedence over the younger half-brother[3] | **France:** Philip VI supported his nephew Charles to keep Brittany within the Valois sphere[1] |

The legal argument was murky. Brittany had historically allowed female succession, but the recent adoption of strictly male succession by the French royal house (to exclude Edward III of England) complicated the matter. Paradoxically, Philip VI supported the female line in Brittany (Blois) while denying it in France, whereas Edward III supported the male line in Brittany (Montfort) while claiming the French throne through the female line[1] .

**II. Phase I: The Montfortist Coup (May–September 1341\)**

### The Preemptive Strike: Seizure of the Treasury
Recognizing that the legal machinery of the French court would inevitably favor the King's nephew, Charles of Blois, John of Montfort resolved to settle the matter by force before a judgment could be rendered. His actions in May 1341 constitute a textbook example of a medieval preemptive strike[5] .

Immediately following the funeral of John III, while the other magnates were still debating the succession, John of Montfort rode to **Limoges**. Historical accounts differ on the exact location of the treasury—some sources suggest Limoges, others Nantes—but the outcome was identical. Montfort, through a combination of intimidation and perhaps the complicity of local officials, seized the accumulated wealth of the duchy[16] .

The capture of the treasury was the single most decisive event of the early war. It provided Montfort with the liquid capital necessary to:

1. **Hire Mercenaries:** He could immediately recruit soldiers, largely bypassing the slow feudal levy.  
2. **Secure Loyalties:** He used the gold to bribe wavering castellans and town burghers[5] .

With the treasury in hand, Montfort moved to **Nantes**, the ducal capital. There, he summoned the nobility to do him homage. The response highlighted the deep divisions within the Breton polity. The "good burghers" of Nantes, perhaps swayed by the promise of stability and Montfort's gold (or, as some chroniclers cynically noted, the "free meal"), accepted him as Duke in May 1341[5] . However, the great feudal families—the Rohans, Clissons, and Beaumanoirs—largely boycotted the assembly, signaling their tacit support for Charles of Blois or their desire to await the King's judgment[16] .

### "The Ride" (*La Chevauchée*): Securing the Duchy
Having secured the capital, John of Montfort embarked on a lightning military campaign in the summer of 1341, known to history as "The Ride" (*La Chevauchée*). His strategic objective was to garrison the key fortified towns and coastal ports before the French royal army could mobilize[11] .

The campaign was a remarkable feat of mobility and aggression. Montfort's forces moved rapidly through the duchy, utilizing a mix of force and diplomacy to bring towns to heel.

* **Rennes:** The administrative center of eastern Brittany was captured after a brief show of force.  
* **Hennebont:** This coastal fortress, later to become famous, was secured early, providing a vital link to the sea.  
* **Vannes and Auray:** These strategic towns on the southern coast surrendered, securing control of the Gulf of Morbihan.  
* **Brest:** Perhaps the most critical acquisition, the port of Brest was taken, ensuring a deep-water port for future English reinforcements[8] .

By August 1341, John of Montfort effectively controlled the entire western half of the duchy and the major administrative centers of the east. He had created a *fait accompli*, partitioning the duchy and forcing Philip VI to either accept his usurpation or commit to a full-scale invasion[8] .

### The Diplomatic Pivot: Alliance with England
Aware that his military success would provoke the wrath of Philip VI, Montfort sought external protection. In mid-1341, he opened secret negotiations with **Edward III of England**. He offered to do liege homage to Edward—not just for the Earldom of Richmond, but for the Duchy of Brittany itself—recognizing Edward as the rightful King of France[1] . This was a treasonous act in the eyes of the French court, but it secured Montfort the promise of English military and financial aid.

### The Arrêt de Conflans (September 7, 1341\)
While Montfort consolidated his gains, the legal process in Paris reached its conclusion. Philip VI had summoned both claimants to the **Court of Peers** (*Cour des Pairs*) at Conflans to adjudicate the succession.

The proceedings were a formality masked as a trial.

* **The Blois Argument:** Lawyers for Charles of Blois argued for the principle of representation, citing the custom of the Penthièvre apanage[12] .  
* **The Montfort Argument:** John of Montfort appeared in person (under a safe conduct) to argue for male preference, though he likely knew the verdict was preordained[19] .

On **September 7, 1341**, the Court issued the **Arrêt de Conflans**.20 The decree:

1. Formally rejected John of Montfort's claim.  
2. Recognized Charles of Blois as the lawful Duke of Brittany.  
3. Authorized the confiscation of Montfort's lands and the use of royal force to install Blois[22] .

Crucially, John of Montfort did not wait for the verdict. Suspecting that Philip VI intended to arrest him regardless of the safe conduct, Montfort fled Paris in late August, breaking his parole and returning to Nantes to prepare for the inevitable invasion[19] .


**III. Phase II: The French Counter-Stroke (October–November 1341\)**

### The Royal Army Mobilizes
Following the Arrêt de Conflans, Philip VI appointed his son, **John, Duke of Normandy** (the future King John II), to lead a royal army to execute the court's judgment. This force was substantial, composed of the flower of French chivalry, Genoese mercenaries, and the levies of the loyalist Breton nobility[5] . They were joined by Charles of Blois, eager to claim his inheritance.

The French strategy was direct: strike at the heart of the rebellion in the east before dealing with the Montfortist strongholds in the west. Their target was **Nantes**.

### The Battle of Champtoceaux (October 1341\)
The campaign opened with the Battle of Champtoceaux (also known as l'Humeau). As the French army advanced down the Loire valley, John of Montfort attempted to check their progress at the border fortress of Champtoceaux.  
The engagement was a disaster for the Montfortists. The French vanguard, superior in numbers and discipline, crushed Montfort's forces. The defeat shattered the morale of the rebel army and left the road to Nantes wide open[1] .

### The Siege and Fall of Nantes (November 1341\)
In late October, the Duke of Normandy laid siege to Nantes. John of Montfort, retreated within the city walls, hoping to withstand the siege until winter or English aid could relieve him. However, the political dynamics within the city began to shift rapidly.

The "good burghers" of Nantes, who had welcomed Montfort in May when he possessed the treasury and the momentum, now faced the prospect of starvation and the wrath of their anointed King[5] . The legal weight of the Arrêt de Conflans also played a psychological role; the citizens were no longer supporting a bold claimant, but a condemned rebel.  
Internal dissent grew. The citizens opened secret negotiations with the Duke of Normandy, seeking to surrender the city in exchange for amnesty and the preservation of their privileges. John of Montfort found his authority evaporating.  
The Surrender:  
On November 21, 1341, isolated and betrayed by his capital, John of Montfort surrendered[7] . He was granted a safe conduct to discuss terms with the King, but this was a ruse. Upon his surrender, he was arrested and transported to Paris, where he was imprisoned in the Louvre. The French crown believed the war was over: the pretender was in chains, the capital was retaken, and the rebellion was seemingly decapitated[1] .


**IV. Phase III: The War of the Two Joans (1342)**

The capture of John of Montfort should have ended the conflict. That it did not is attributable entirely to the extraordinary leadership of his wife, **Joanna of Flanders** (*Jeanne de Flandre*).

### The Character of Joanna of Flanders
Joanna was the sister of the Count of Flanders and possessed a fierce political acumen. The chronicler Jean Froissart famously described her as having "the courage of a man and the heart of a lion"[25] . Instead of negotiating a surrender, Joanna assumed full command of the Montfortist faction.  
Her first act was one of symbolic power: she presented her infant son, John (the future John IV), to the garrison at Rennes, declaring him the true heir and herself the regent. She rallied the demoralized troops, promising them that English aid was imminent[11] .  
Recognizing that the eastern cities were indefensible against the royal army, she executed a strategic withdrawal to the west, establishing her base of operations at the coastal fortress of **Hennebont**. This move was calculated; Hennebont was strongly fortified and, crucially, accessible by sea, allowing for communication with England[11] . She immediately dispatched her trusted lieutenant, **Amaury de Clisson**, to England to plead for urgent intervention from Edward III[11] .

### The Siege of Hennebont (May–June 1342\)
In the spring of 1342, Charles of Blois, having secured the east, marched west to finish the war. He laid siege to Hennebont, initiating one of the most legendary episodes of the Hundred Years' War[27] .

The siege was a desperate affair. The French forces pounded the walls with siege engines, while the defenders, inspired by Joanna, repelled wave after wave of assaults. Joanna took an active combat role, reportedly wearing armor and riding a warhorse through the streets to encourage the citizens. She mobilized the women of the town to cut their skirts short and carry stones and boiling oil to the battlements[6] .

#### The Raid of "Jeanne la Flamme"
During a particularly intense French assault, Joanna observed from a watchtower that the French camp was left lightly guarded. Seizing the opportunity, she led a sortie of 300 mounted men-at-arms out of a side gate. They rode undetected to the French camp and set it ablaze. The resulting fire destroyed tents, supplies, and siege equipment, causing panic in the French lines and forcing them to break off the assault on the walls to save their camp. This exploit earned her the sobriquet "Jeanne la Flamme" ("Joanna the Flame")[6] .  
Though she was cut off from the town during the retreat, she managed to ride to the nearby castle of Auray, gather reinforcements, and fight her way back into Hennebont five days later—a feat that further cemented her legend[28] .

#### The Arrival of English Aid
Despite her heroism, the situation in Hennebont grew critical. Supplies ran low, and the Bishop of Léon began to pressure Joanna to surrender. She begged the council for just three more days. On the morning of the third day, English sails were spotted on the horizon[6] .  
The relief force was commanded by Sir Walter Manny (Gautier de Masny), a renowned Hainaulter knight in Edward III's service. Though his force was small—comprising only a few dozen knights and a few hundred archers—their arrival broke the morale of the besiegers. Manny's troops landed and immediately engaged the French, destroying their remaining siege engines. Realizing that the town could now be resupplied by sea, Charles of Blois lifted the siege and withdrew[5] .


**V. Phase IV: English Escalation and Tactical Evolution (Summer–Autumn 1342\)**

The relief of Hennebont marked the beginning of direct English military involvement. Edward III, seeing an opportunity to open a new front against France, committed substantial resources to the Breton theater.

### The Battle of Brest (August 1342\)
Following his retreat from Hennebont, Charles of Blois moved to besiege **Brest**, the most important port in western Brittany. To ensure the city could not be relieved by sea, he employed a fleet of **Genoese mercenaries** to blockade the harbor while his army attacked from the land[29] .

In August 1342, a large English fleet commanded by William de Bohun, Earl of Northampton, arrived off Brest. Northampton commanded a force of roughly 1,350 men and 260 ships[29] .  
The ensuing engagement, often referred to as the Battle of Brest, was a decisive English victory. The English ships, likely utilizing archers on the decks to clear the enemy vessels, scattered the Genoese galleys. With the blockade broken and English troops landing to reinforce the garrison, Charles of Blois was once again forced to abandon a siege and retreat into the interior[29] . The preservation of Brest gave England a permanent bridgehead in Brittany that they would hold for decades, often calling it their "Key to France."

### The Battle of Morlaix (September 30, 1342\)
Having secured Brest, the Earl of Northampton marched inland to capture the town of **Morlaix**. Charles of Blois, determined to stop the English advance, gathered a large army—estimated at between 15,000 and 20,000 men (likely an exaggeration, but certainly vastly outnumbering the English)—and marched to relieve the town[31] .

On **September 30, 1342**, the two armies met near Lanmeur. The **Battle of Morlaix** is of immense historical significance as it was the first major land battle of the Hundred Years' War where English tactical innovations were tested against French chivalry.

#### Tactical Disposition
Northampton, commanding only about 3,000 men, adopted a defensive posture. He deployed his men-at-arms dismounted in a line, flanked by wings of longbowmen. Crucially, anticipating a French cavalry charge, he ordered his men to dig a series of concealed **pits and ditches** in front of their lines, covering them with hurdles and grass to make the ground appear solid[9] . This use of field fortifications to shape the battlefield was a precursor to the tactics used at Crécy four years later.

#### The Engagement
The French army arrived in three divisions (battles).

1. **The First Charge:** The French vanguard, led by Geoffrey de Charny, launched an impetuous mounted charge. As they hit the concealed pits, horses stumbled and fell, throwing their riders. The English archers poured volleys into the confused mass, decimating the vanguard[9] .  
2. **The Second and Third Waves:** The main body of Charles of Blois's army, seeing the disaster, attacked with more caution. The sheer weight of numbers began to tell. The English line held for hours, but eventually, threatened with encirclement, Northampton ordered a fighting withdrawal into a nearby wood[34] .

#### The Outcome
The battle ended in a tactical stalemate but a strategic English success. The French were unable to break the English formation in the woods and, having suffered heavy casualties, eventually withdrew. Northampton was able to retreat to the coast in good order. The battle proved that English longbowmen and dismounted knights could withstand the charge of the French nobility, a lesson Philip VI would tragically fail to learn[32] .


**VI. Phase V: Royal Intervention and Stalemate (Winter 1342\)**

### The Arrival of Edward III
In October 1342, the conflict escalated further with the arrival of King Edward III in person. He landed at Brest with the main English royal army, intent on forcing a decisive conclusion[1] .  
Edward's strategy was to conquer the remaining Blois-controlled strongholds in a rapid winter campaign. He divided his forces:

* **Vannes:** Edward led the main force to besiege Vannes.  
* **Rennes:** A detachment was sent to blockade Rennes.  
* **Nantes:** A third force moved toward the capital[35] .

### The Siege of Vannes and the Death of Robert of Artois
The focal point of the winter campaign became Vannes. The city had changed hands multiple times and was currently held by the Blois faction. The siege was brutal and conducted in worsening weather.  
Prominent in the English ranks was Robert III of Artois. Robert was a disaffected French nobleman whose defection to Edward III and encouragement of Edward's claim to the French throne had been a primary cause of the war[36] . During the siege operations in November 1342, Robert was commanding a force that briefly breached the walls. In the ensuing chaotic fighting, he was severely wounded. He was evacuated to London for treatment but succumbed to his wounds (complicated by dysentery) in late November 1342.37 His death deprived Edward of one of his most experienced, if controversial, commanders.

### The Standoff at Vannes
By December 1342, the situation had devolved into a dangerous stalemate. Edward III's army was spread thin, suffering from disease and the harsh winter. Meanwhile, John, Duke of Normandy, had arrived in Brittany with a massive French relief army, estimated by some sources at over 30,000 men (though likely closer to 10,000–15,000)[5] .  
The French army marched to Vannes and entrenched themselves opposite the English siege lines. Edward III was effectively trapped: he could not take the city, and he was outnumbered by the French relief force. A battle seemed imminent, one that the English were poorly positioned to win[1] .


**VII. Phase VI: The Truce of Malestroit (1343)**

### Papal Mediation
With two Christian monarchs facing each other in the mud of Brittany, Pope Clement VI (elected in May 1342\) intervened to prevent a bloodbath. He dispatched two cardinals, Annibaldo Caetani and Pierre Desprès, to mediate a truce[10] .  
The diplomatic intervention was timely.

* **Edward III** was in a precarious military position and running out of funds.  
* **Philip VI** and the Duke of Normandy were wary of attacking the English defensive positions, remembering the carnage at Morlaix, and were also facing financial strain[1] .

### The Terms of the Truce (January 19, 1343\)
Negotiations were conducted at the priory of La Madeleine in **Malestroit**, culminating in the signing of the **Truce of Malestroit** on **January 19, 1343**.10

The terms were remarkably favorable to the English/Montfortist faction, considering their tactical vulnerability at Vannes:

1. **Status Quo (Uti Possidetis):** Each side would retain the territories they currently occupied. This meant the Montfortists kept their control of western Brittany (including Brest and Hennebont), effectively legitimizing their rebellion[5] .  
2. **The Fate of Vannes:** To save face for both monarchs, Vannes was to be handed over to the Papacy. The cardinal legates would administer the city as a neutral zone for the duration of the truce[18] .  
3. **Release of Prisoners:** Philip VI agreed to release John of Montfort from the Louvre, although the conditions were vague[18] .  
4. **Duration:** The truce was scheduled to last until September 29, 1346, allowing time for a permanent peace treaty to be negotiated in Avignon[10] .

### The Aftermath: A Fragile Peace
The Truce of Malestroit halted the major campaigning, but it failed to bring peace.

* **The Betrayal of Vannes:** Late in 1343, the citizens of Vannes—likely instigated by English agents—expelled the papal garrison and returned the city to English control. This violation of the truce enraged the Pope and the French King[10] .  
* **The Captivity of Montfort:** Philip VI did not fully release John of Montfort. He was released from the dungeon but kept under house arrest in Paris and forbidden from returning to Brittany. He would not escape to resume the fight until 1345[10] .  
* **The Partition:** The truce effectively solidified the partition of Brittany. The Montfortists held the west (Breton-speaking), and the Blois faction held the east (French-speaking). This division would characterize the geography of the war for the next twenty years[1] .


**Conclusion**

The period from **1341 to 1343** was the crucible in which the Breton Civil War was forged. What began as a localized dispute over inheritance was transformed, through the calculated ambition of John of Montfort and the strategic necessities of England and France, into a protracted international conflict.

This initial phase established the enduring patterns of the war:

1. **The English Bridgehead:** The capture of Brest and the defense of Hennebont gave England a permanent gateway into France, complicating French defense planning for decades.  
2. **The Tactical Revolution:** The Battle of Morlaix demonstrated the potency of the English tactical system (dismounted knights \+ longbows) against French chivalry, a lesson that would be repeated on a grander scale at Crécy.  
3. **The Agency of Women:** The "War of the Two Joans" highlighted the capacity of noblewomen to exercise military and political command in the absence of their husbands. Joanna of Flanders' defense of Hennebont remains one of the most celebrated feats of the medieval era.  
4. **The Failure of Diplomacy:** The Truce of Malestroit, while temporarily stopping the slaughter, failed to address the root cause of the conflict. By leaving the duchy partitioned and the claimants unresolved, it ensured that the war would continue to ravage Brittany until the decisive Battle of Auray in 1364\.

Ultimately, the years 1341–1343 revealed the fragility of the feudal order when confronted with the dynastic ambitions of the great powers. The suffering of the Breton people, caught between the English longbow and the French lance, was only just beginning.
