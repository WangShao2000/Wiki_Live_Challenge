# Sarabande (collection): A Comprehensive Analysis of the Literary Press and Cultural Imprint
## Introduction
**Sarabande**—in the context of contemporary American letters—refers primarily to the curated literary output of **Sarabande Books**, a nonprofit independent press headquartered in Louisville, Kentucky. Established in 1994, the Sarabande collection has evolved into one of the most significant repositories of contemporary poetry, short fiction, and the essay form in the United States. With a catalog comprising nearly three hundred titles, the collection is defined by its commitment to "diverse forms and innovative voices," acting as a critical counterweight to the commercial imperatives of major publishing conglomerates[1] .

The designation "Sarabande (collection)" may also, in specific bibliographic or discographic contexts, refer to the **Varèse Sarabande** imprint’s anniversary compilations (e.g., the *25th Anniversary Collection*) or the specific literary works titled *Sarabande* by authors such as the novelist of the 2017 love story or the Australian poet John A. Scott. However, the most culturally pervasive use of the term in the domain of "collections" pertains to the imprint founded by Sarah Gorham and Jeffrey Skinner, which functions as a cohesive aesthetic project honoring the "gracious, elegant dance with a sensual, wild underside" that its name suggests[3] .

This report provides an exhaustive, expert-level examination of the Sarabande collection. It analyzes the press’s historical trajectory, its unique editorial philosophy, the mechanisms of its major literary prizes (the **Kathryn A. Morton** and **Mary McCarthy** prizes), and the critical reception of its key texts. Furthermore, it disambiguates the term by examining other collections bearing the Sarabande name, including the significant discography of the Varèse Sarabande label and the works of William Sarabande, providing a definitive resource on the subject.

## History and Establishment
### Founding Context and Mission (1994)
The genesis of the Sarabande collection in 1994 occurred during a turbulent period in American publishing. The early 1990s recession had prompted many commercial "Big Five" publishers to slash their mid-lists, with poetry and short fiction collections—genres traditionally viewed as commercially precarious—bearing the brunt of these reductions[4] . Founders **Sarah Gorham** (serving as President and Editor-in-Chief) and **Jeffrey Skinner** (Chair) established Sarabande Books with a specific, corrective mission: "to champion poetry, fiction, and essay" and to provide a stable home for works of high literary merit that might otherwise be orphaned by the marketplace[1] .

The press was incorporated as a nonprofit literary press, a structural decision that allowed it to prioritize aesthetic value over immediate profit margins. From the outset, the founders articulated a vision of the press not merely as a production house but as a steward of "lasting editions that honor exceptional writing"[1] . This commitment to the physical artifact—the book as an object—would become a hallmark of the Sarabande collection, distinguishing it in an era of increasing digitization and mass-market production values.

Headquartered in **Louisville, Kentucky**, with a satellite presence in New York City, Sarabande positioned itself as a bridge between the vital literary culture of the American South and the publishing capital of the Northeast[2] . This geographic duality influenced the collection's identity, fostering a sensibility that was at once cosmopolitan and deeply rooted in regional engagement.

### The Inaugural Years and Early Success
The Sarabande collection launched its first titles in 1996\. The response from the literary community was immediate and overwhelming. In its very first year, the press received approximately 1,800 poetry manuscripts and 750 short fiction manuscripts for its inaugural contests, signaling a desperate hunger among writers for outlets dedicated to these forms[4] .

Critically, the press achieved a coup rare for a nascent independent publisher: its first published short story collection was reviewed in *The New York Times Book Review*.4 This early validation from the nation’s "paper of record" established Sarabande as a serious contender in the literary field and cemented its reputation for quality control. Early distribution agreements with **Consortium Book Sales & Distribution** further ensured that the collection could reach a national readership, bypassing the logistical hurdles that often stifle small press output[2] .

### Growth and Evolution
Over the subsequent three decades, the Sarabande collection expanded from its initial cohort of four titles to a robust library of nearly three hundred works[1] . This growth was managed carefully, with the press typically adding two books a year until reaching a steady state of approximately 12 collections annually[4] .

The collection’s scope also broadened. While initially focused on the twin pillars of poetry and short fiction, the editorial vision expanded to include the **essay** and **hybrid works**—texts that defy easy categorization, blending lyricism with narrative and fact with speculation. This evolution reflected a broader shift in contemporary literature toward genre-fluidity, with Sarabande positioning itself at the vanguard of this movement[4] .

## Editorial Vision and Philosophy
### The Metaphor of the Sarabande
The name "Sarabande" is not merely a label but a governing metaphor for the collection’s aesthetic. Sarah Gorham has elucidated this philosophy by referencing the dance form itself: "The Sarabande is a gracious, elegant dance with a sensual, wild underside: gracious and elegant thanks to the Germans and French, sensual and wild thanks to its origins as an Aztec mating ritual"[3] .

This duality serves as the primary criterion for inclusion in the collection. The editorial team seeks work that embodies this tension: writing that possesses the formal control and elegance of the courtly dance but is animated by a raw, provocative, and "sensual" energy. This "wild underside" prevents the collection from becoming staid or purely academic; instead, Sarabande texts are characterized by their emotional urgency and intellectual daring[3] .

### Commitment to Genre and Form
The Sarabande collection is structurally divided into several key genres, each treated with equal weight:

* **Poetry**: The press maintains a "Series" of poets that is "eighteen strong," including luminaries such as **Yusef Komunyakaa**, **Heather McHugh**, **Khaled Mattawa**, **Kimiko Hahn**, **Sandra Cisneros**, **Lydia Davis**, **Louise Glück**, and **Robert Pinsky**.4 This roster reads like a "Who's Who" of contemporary American poetry, demonstrating the press's ability to attract established masters while cultivating new voices.  
* **Short Fiction**: In an industry that often pushes short story writers to produce novels, Sarabande has remained a staunch defender of the collection as a discrete art form. The press champions the short story not as a stepping stone but as a destination, creating a space for writers like **Edith Pearlman** and **Kyle Minor** to flourish in the short form[2] .  
* **The Essay and Lyric Essay**: Recognizing the renaissance of the essay, Sarabande has published groundbreaking nonfiction that often utilizes lyric or fragmented structures. **Lia Purpura**'s work, for instance, is cited for its exploration of how metaphors "get compromised... eroded and need updating" 8, reflecting the press's interest in the mechanics of language itself.  
* **Hybrid and Visual Texts**: The collection includes experimental works like **Sarah J. Sloat**'s *Hotel Almighty*, a collection of "visual poetry" that incorporates erasure and collage[9] . This willingness to publish works that require complex production processes highlights the press's dedication to the book as a visual medium.

### Production Values
Reviews and commentary consistently highlight the physical beauty of Sarabande books. The press is committed to "creating lasting editions," utilizing high-quality paper, distinct typography, and striking cover art that often stands in contrast to the mass-market paperbacks of larger houses. This attention to bibliography reinforces the idea that the book is an art object worthy of preservation[1] .

## Major Literary Awards and Series
The Sarabande collection is largely curated through a set of prestigious annual competitions. These prizes are not merely submission mechanisms; they are institutions in their own right, shaping the landscape of contemporary writing by elevating specific aesthetics and voices.

### The Kathryn A. Morton Prize in Poetry
Established in 1995, the **Kathryn A. Morton Prize** is the flagship poetry contest of the collection[2] .

* **Significance**: It is one of the most highly regarded prizes for a book-length collection of poetry in the United States.  
* **Mechanism**: The contest accepts manuscripts annually between January 1 and February 15[1] . Winners receive a $2,000 cash prize, publication, and a standard royalty contract[3] .  
* **Judges**: The prize is adjudicated by distinguished poets, ensuring that the selection process reflects the highest standards of the craft. Past judges and associated figures include **Ocean Vuong** and **Terrance Hayes**.11  
* **Impact**: Winning this prize frequently launches or cements a poet's career. For example, **Simon Shieh**'s *Master* (2023), selected by Terrance Hayes, went on to win the **Norma Farber First Book Award** and was a finalist for the **Los Angeles Times Book Prize**.11 Similarly, **Rick Barot**'s *The Darker Fall* (2002) was a Morton Prize winner, establishing him as a core voice in the collection[13] .

### The Mary McCarthy Prize in Short Fiction
Parallel to the poetry prize, the **Mary McCarthy Prize** is the premier fiction award of the press[2] .

* **Scope**: It is open to collections of short stories, novellas, and short novels, explicitly supporting the forms most often neglected by commercial publishers[3] .  
* **Prestige**: Like the Morton Prize, it offers $2,000 and publication.  
* **Notable Winners**: The roster of winners includes some of the most exciting voices in contemporary fiction. **Leslie Kirk Campbell** won the 2020 prize for *The Man with Eight Pairs of Legs*, a collection praised for its "vivid imagery"[14] **Hillary Behrman** was selected by Lauren Groff as the winner of the 2024 prize for her collection *Lake Effect*.15 Other significant winners include **Whitney Collins** for *Big Bad* (2019) and **Becky Hagenston** for *A Gram of Mars* (1997)[16] . The longevity of the prize—spanning from Hagenston in the late 90s to Behrman in the mid-2020s—demonstrates its enduring relevance.

### The Linda Bruckheimer Series in Kentucky Literature
This series functions as a dedicated channel for regional literature, honoring the press's home state[4] .

* **Mandate**: It publishes works by writers who are natives of Kentucky or who have lived in the state for at least two years.  
* **Genres**: The series encompasses poetry, fiction, and essays.  
* **Highlighted Works**: **Ashley Marie Farmer**'s *Dear Damage* (2022), a winner in this series, exemplifies the high caliber of regional work supported by the press. The essay collection, which deals with violence and love, won the **2022 International Rubery Book Award for Nonfiction**.6 **Ryan Ridge**'s *New Bad News* is another title, described as capturing an "anarchic" American spirit[18] .

### The Sarabande Chapbook Prize
Recognizing the importance of brevity and the specific artistic demands of the chapbook form, Sarabande recently introduced the **Chapbook Prize**, with submission windows in April and October[1] . This initiative allows the collection to include shorter, more concentrated bursts of creativity that might not sustain a full-length manuscript but are nonetheless vital.

## The Collection: Seminal Works and Critical Analysis
The Sarabande collection is vast, but certain titles serve as pillars that define its reputation. These works, spanning three decades, illustrate the press's evolution and its adherence to the "graceful yet wild" aesthetic.

### Short Fiction and Novels
#### *The Man With Eight Pairs of Legs*** **by Leslie Kirk Campbell (2022)
Winner of the 2020 Mary McCarthy Prize, this collection is a profound exploration of the body and memory. Campbell’s stories are described as a "study in compassion and in passion," featuring characters who are often pushed to physical and emotional extremes. The *San Francisco Chronicle* hailed it as a "remarkable collection," and it was selected as a **2022 WNBA Great Group Read**.6 The title itself suggests the surreal and the bodily, themes that recur throughout the Sarabande fiction list.

#### *Big Bad*** **by Whitney Collins (2021)
This collection, which won the 2019 Mary McCarthy Prize, exemplifies the press's taste for the dark and the "sensual/wild." Collins explores "dark impulses," with the title story featuring a woman who gives birth to a wolf-like entity. Reviewers have noted the "layered" nature of the stories and their ability to push the boundaries of character empathy[8] . The collection aligns with the "Southern Gothic" tradition while modernizing it for a contemporary audience.

#### *Praying Drunk*** **by Kyle Minor (2014)
One of the most critically acclaimed titles in the collection, *Praying Drunk* is a set of twelve stories that defy easy classification. Minor’s work is noted for its structural innovation—often blurring the line between memoir and fiction—and its deep emotional resonance. It is frequently cited as a touchstone for the "innovative voices" Sarabande seeks to champion[7] .

#### *A Gram of Mars*** **by Becky Hagenston (1998)
As an early winner of the Mary McCarthy Prize (selected by A.M. Homes), this collection helped establish the press's fiction credentials. Hagenston’s stories portray the "modern family" not as a broken entity but as one that "refuses to be fashionably dysfunctional," finding resilience in hyphenated and step-parented relationships[5] . The collection includes stories like "Close Enough" and "Till Death Do Us Part," showcasing a realism that is both grounded and psychologically acute.

#### *Greetings from Below*** **by David Philip Mullins (2012)
This collection introduces the character Nick Danze, following him through a "sexual education" and life in the "neon-tinted" landscape of Las Vegas. It is described as a "vital debut" that explores the "perverse power of alienation and shame," demonstrating the press's willingness to engage with gritty, uncomfortable subject matter[22] .

#### *More Like Not Running Away*** **by Paul Shepherd (2005)
Though often marketed within the fiction series, this work functions as a novel. It details the life of Levi Revel, a boy grappling with a "majestic dreamer" father and a "violent, secret past." Critics like **Janet Burroway** have praised it for its "honesty" and "guileless voice," highlighting its exploration of childhood angst and the feeling of being an "outsider even in your own head"[23] .

#### *Lake Effect*** **by Hillary Behrman (Forthcoming 2026\)
Representing the future of the collection, this 2024 McCarthy Prize winner (selected by **Lauren Groff**) features characters navigating "wild places and hidden urban spaces." The stories move from ancient dry lakes to homeless encampments, exploring the "gravitational pull" of environments on the human psyche. This selection indicates that the press remains committed to discovering voices that interrogate the relationship between place and identity[15] .

#### *Lore*** **by Roy Kesey (Forthcoming 2025\)
Winner of the 2025 Mary McCarthy Prize, *Lore* tells the story of an isolated 76-year-old widow. Selected by judge **Ed Park**, the book is described as "powerful," continuing the press's tradition of highlighting narratives of solitude and resilience[26] .

### Poetry
#### *The Darker Fall*** **(2002) and *Want* (2008) by Rick Barot
**Rick Barot** is a central figure in the Sarabande poetry canon. *The Darker Fall*, which won the Kathryn A. Morton Prize, established his reputation for "lyric precision." His subsequent collection, *Want*, was a finalist for the **Lambda Literary Award** and won the **Grub Street Book Prize**.13 Barot’s trajectory illustrates Sarabande’s role in nurturing poets across multiple stages of their careers.

#### *Master*** **by Simon Shieh (2023)
A recent addition that has garnered significant acclaim, *Master* is a debut collection centered on themes of power, discipline, and the "formidable mystery of love." Selected by **Terrance Hayes**, the 90-page volume won the **Norma Farber First Book Award**. It is noted for its "lyric compression" and its ability to navigate complex emotional landscapes with minimal verbiage[11] .

#### *White Blood: A Lyric of Virginia*** **by Kiki Petrosino (2020)
This collection is a seminal work in the press's recent history. A "lyric of Virginia," it contemplates the "enduring, deeply personal legacies of enslavement and racial discrimination." The book uses poetry to perform a genealogical and historical excavation, opening with poems about silent ancestors. It has been widely reviewed and praised for its ambition and moral weight[8] .

#### *Hotel Almighty*** **by Sarah J. Sloat (2020)
*Hotel Almighty* represents the experimental edge of the Sarabande collection. It is a work of **visual poetry**, combining text with collage and erasure techniques. The book is described as a "wildly imaginative dream world," proving that the press is open to forms that challenge the traditional boundaries of the "poetry collection"[9] .

#### *Music Like Dirt*** **by Frank Bidart
One of the most prestigious titles in the press's history, this chapbook achieved the rare distinction of being a finalist for the **Pulitzer Prize**. This nomination validated the literary worth of the chapbook form and highlighted Sarabande’s ability to attract and publish giants of American letters alongside debut authors[4] .

#### *My Heresies*** **by Alina Stefanescu (2025)
A forthcoming collection described as a "feminist text," *My Heresies* guides readers through "liminal spaces where the sacred and the profane collide." The poems are characterized as "observant, angry, and questioning," reflecting the press's engagement with contemporary socio-political discourse and feminist poetics[30] .

#### *The Wrong End of the Rainbow*** **by Charles Wright (2005)
The inclusion of **Charles Wright**, a Pulitzer Prize winner and one of the most decorated poets of his generation, underscores the high standing of the Sarabande collection. This volume contributes to Wright's lifelong project of metaphysical investigation through landscape and memory[33] .

### Essays and Creative Nonfiction
#### *Bright: A Memoir*** **by Kiki Petrosino
Further establishing Petrosino as a core Sarabande author, *Bright* is a collection of "poetic, honest, philosophical vignettes." The book investigates the etymology of the term "bright" and its relationship to race, identity, and the "lightness" of skin. It received starred reviews from *Publishers Weekly* and was selected as a "New and Noteworthy" title by *Poets & Writers*, demonstrating the press's strength in the lyric essay and memoir genres[6] .

#### *Dear Damage*** **by Ashley Marie Farmer (2022)
Winner of the **2022 International Rubery Book Award**, this essay collection examines an act of violence through a lens of love rather than malice. It exemplifies the "hybrid" nature of Sarabande's nonfiction list, blending reportage with deep introspection[6] .

#### *Stealing Glimpses*** **by Molly McQuade
Subtitled "Of Poetry, Poets, and Things In Between," this essay collection reflects the press's dedication to literary criticism that is itself literary. It explores the intersections of poetry and daily life, reinforcing the press's mission to serve as an educational resource[5] .

## Cultural Impact and Critical Reception
The Sarabande collection occupies a vital niche in the American publishing ecosystem. It is widely regarded as "legit and very well respected in lit fic circles," effectively bridging the gap between small, experimental presses and the larger commercial houses[37] .

### Awards and Honors
The collection’s impact is quantifiable through the accolades its authors have received. Beyond the internal Morton and McCarthy prizes, Sarabande authors have garnered:

* **The Pulitzer Prize** (Finalist: Frank Bidart)[4] .  
* **The Whiting Award**.2  
* **The National Endowment for the Arts (NEA) Fellowships**.2  
* **The Pushcart Prize**: Multiple authors, including Whitney Collins, have been honored, confirming the press's status as a primary source for the "best" short fiction and poetry in the country[2] .  
* **The Norma Farber First Book Award**: Won by Simon Shieh, highlighting the press's role in discovering new talent[11] .  
* **Lambda Literary Awards**: Finalists such as **Jason K. Friedman** (*Fire Year*) and **Rick Barot** (*Want*) demonstrate the collection's engagement with LGBTQ+ themes and excellence[13] .

### Critical Review
Sarabande titles are regularly reviewed in major venues that often ignore small presses. *The New York Times*, *The San Francisco Chronicle*, *Publishers Weekly* (often with starred reviews), and *Kirkus Reviews* provide consistent coverage[2] . Reviewers frequently praise the press for its aesthetic consistency and its "bravery" in publishing formally inventive work[8] . For instance, the review of **Nona Caspers**'s *The Fifth Woman* noted that the "mundane becomes poetic," a sentiment that could apply to much of the collection[8] .

### Demographics and Diversity
The collection is notable for its inclusivity. The editorial mandate actively seeks "innovative voices," which translates into a roster rich in diversity:

* **Neurodivergent Perspectives**: A 2026 AWP panel featuring Sarabande authors (**Stine An**, **Lauren Haldeman**, **Alina Stefanescu**) highlights the press's support for neurodivergent poets and the exploration of "novelty-fueled nervous systems"[30] .  
* **Diasporic and International Voices**: Authors like **Stine An** (whose work *S\_MMER CR\_SH* explores diasporic poetics) and **Robert Yune** (author of *Impossible Children*, dealing with immigrant experiences in the US) demonstrate the collection's engagement with global narratives[11] .  
* **Racial and Social Justice**: Works like **Kiki Petrosino**'s *White Blood* directly confront the legacies of slavery and discrimination, positioning the press as a participant in the national conversation on race[10] .

## Educational Initiatives and Community Engagement
Sarabande defines itself not just as a publisher but as an educational resource[40] . This mission is realized through several key initiatives that extend the collection's impact beyond the bookstore:

* **Sarabande-in-Education**: A comprehensive web-based resource that provides study questions, writing prompts, and interviews related to the collection's titles. This initiative supports teachers and students of creative writing, integrating Sarabande books into curricula across the country[4] .  
* **The Flo Gault Student Poetry Prize**: This program honors poets studying at the college level, providing them with broadsides and cash prizes. It fosters the next generation of writers and connects them to the broader literary community[4] .  
* **Sarabande Writing Labs**: These are free arts programming initiatives designed to invest in emerging writers, both locally in Kentucky and nationally. They underscore the press's commitment to its nonprofit mission of public service[6] .

## Disambiguation: Other Collections and Works Titled "Sarabande"
While "Sarabande (collection)" is most prominently associated with the literary press, the term appears in other artistic contexts. A comprehensive report must distinguish these entities to ensure clarity.

### The Varèse Sarabande Collection
**Varèse Sarabande** is a prolific American record label specializing in film scores and original cast recordings[41] . While distinct from the book publisher, it produces "collections" that are culturally significant in their own right.

* **The 25th Anniversary Collection**: A major compilation released by the label, celebrating its history of soundtrack preservation. Snippets explicitly refer to "Varèse Sarabande 25th Anniversary Collection" (VSD4-6460) and "Volume Two" (VCL 0703-1021)[41] .  
* **The 40th Anniversary**: A later collection, "40 Years of Great Film Music 1978-2018," further cements the label's legacy[44] .  
* **Composers**: The label's catalog includes extensive works by composers like **John Scott** (e.g., *Mountbatten: The Last Viceroy*, *Man on Fire*, *King Kong Lives*), **Jerry Goldsmith**, and **Hans Zimmer**.41 While unrelated to the literary press, the shared name "Sarabande" and the shared involvement of a "John Scott" (composer vs. poet) often leads to bibliographic confusion.

### *Sarabande*** **(Novel, 2017\)
A singular novel titled *Sarabande* was published on January 10, 2017\. It is a "powerful love story for our digital age" featuring characters **Anna** and **Colin**. The narrative explores themes of intimacy, integrity, and physical distance in the modern world[46] . This work is a discrete publication and not a "collection" in the sense of an anthology or series.

### *Sarabande*** **(Poetry by John A. Scott)
The Australian poet **John A. Scott** published a poetry collection titled *Sarabande* in 1988 (University of Queensland Press). Snippets reference "Sarabande John A. Scott" in search queries, often conflating him with the composer John Scott or the Sarabande Books press[38] . This is a specific title by a specific author, distinct from the American publisher.

### Musical Origins
The term "Sarabande" itself refers to a dance in triple time, originating in Spain and popularized in the Baroque era by composers like **Handel** and **Bach**. **Handel's Sarabande** (from Suite No. 4 in D minor, HWV 437\) is a famous piece often found in classical music collections[48] . This musical form provides the metaphorical foundation for Sarabande Books, symbolizing the balance of elegance and passion.

## Conclusion
The **Sarabande** collection, under the stewardship of Sarabande Books, stands as a premier institution in American letters. By championing the short story, the poem, and the lyric essay during periods of commercial contraction, the press has curated a library of "lasting editions" that define the contemporary literary landscape. Through its rigorous **Kathryn A. Morton** and **Mary McCarthy** prizes, its dedication to regional voices via the **Linda Bruckheimer Series**, and its production of physically exquisite books, Sarabande has fulfilled its mission to honor the "gracious, elegant dance" of the creative spirit. While the name "Sarabande" also graces significant musical collections and singular novels, it is the literary press in Louisville, Kentucky, that has most fully embodied the term as a collective noun for artistic excellence and innovation.
