# Kaleigh Trace: A Comprehensive Biographical and Critical Study
## 1\. Introduction: The Intersection of Disability, Sexuality, and Radical Vulnerability
Kaleigh Trace serves as a singular and formidable voice within the landscape of Canadian non-fiction and disability justice. A writer, sex educator, and psychotherapist based in Toronto, Ontario, Trace has spent the last decade dismantling the societal desexualization of disabled bodies through a potent combination of memoir, cultural criticism, and clinical practice[1] . Her work, which began in the grassroots activism of the Halifax sex-positive community and evolved into award-winning literature, operates at the nexus of queer theory, Crip theory, and feminist praxis.

Born in 1986, Trace’s trajectory is defined by a refusal to compartmentalize her identity. She is not merely a "disabled writer" or a "sex educator," but a theorist of the body who argues that the messy, vulnerable realities of physical impairment are not obstacles to intimacy but rather generative sites of connection and pleasure[1] . Her seminal work, *Hot, Wet, and Shaking: How I Learned to Talk About Sex* (2014), remains a cornerstone text in the canon of sex-positive disability literature, recognized for its "candid discussions of sexuality and disability" and its refusal to sanitize the disabled experience for an able-bodied gaze[1] .

The significance of Trace’s contributions gained new urgency in 2024\. Following a diagnosis of terminal cancer, she republished an expanded edition of her memoir, extending her theoretical framework to encompass the end of life. In doing so, she drew a profound parallel between the vulnerability required for sexual intimacy and the vulnerability required to face mortality, asserting that both are acts of radical openness[3] . This report provides an exhaustive analysis of Trace’s biography, her literary and journalistic output, her theoretical contributions to the understanding of desire and disability, and the evolving legacy of her work in the face of terminal illness.

## 2\. Biography and Medical Narrative
Kaleigh Trace’s biography is inextricably linked to her medical history. Unlike many public figures who strive to keep their medical realities private, Trace centers her medicalization as a primary text, reading her own body to critique the "medical industrial complex" and the societal scripts regarding ability and desirability.

### 2.1. Early Childhood and the Dual Disability Narrative
Trace’s disability narrative is complex, characterized by the intersection of congenital condition and acquired injury—a distinction she often weaves into a singular experience of "complex embodiment."

Trace was born in 1986\. At the age of 15 months, she was diagnosed with cerebral palsy (CP) and spasticity[5] . Cerebral palsy serves as the baseline of her physical existence, affecting her muscle tone and movement patterns from infancy. Her mother, Julie, recounted that despite the physical limitations, Kaleigh possessed an "awesome personality" from a young age, suggesting an early resilience that would later characterize her public persona[5] .

The progression of her condition during early childhood was marked by significant mobility challenges. Reports indicate that as a young child, she walked on her tiptoes with an arched back due to severe spasticity[5] . This spasticity—a condition where muscles are continuously contracted—meant that physical exertion was taxing. Her mother frequently had to carry her when the fatigue of navigating the world became too great, establishing an early dynamic of interdependence that Trace would later reframe through the lens of disability justice[5] .

However, her physiological reality was compounded by a traumatic event at the age of eight. Trace was involved in a severe car accident that resulted in a spinal cord injury[6] . This accident is a pivotal moment in her biography, as it layered an acquired, traumatic injury atop her congenital condition. In her own words, this event left her "permanently disabled" in a manner that rendered her hyper-visible[6] . The accident cemented her status as a subject of medical intervention and public scrutiny, altering her trajectory from a child with CP to a person navigating the acute aftermath of spinal trauma.

### 2.2. Medical Interventions and the "Cyborg" Body
Trace’s childhood was dominated by interactions with medical technology and rehabilitation, experiences that informed her later critiques of the "cure" narrative. Following her car accident and the worsening of her spasticity, she underwent a year of intensive injection therapy and casting[5] . These treatments, designed to forcibly stretch tight muscles and improve range of motion, were physically demanding and ultimately yielded insufficient relief[5] .

This failure of conservative management led to a significant surgical intervention. When Trace was eight years old—coinciding with the period following her accident—her mother consulted doctors about Intrathecal Baclofen (ITB) Therapy[5] . This therapy involves the implantation of a pump that delivers a muscle relaxant (baclofen) directly into the spinal fluid, bypassing the systemic side effects of oral medication.

Trace has described the screening process for this device with visceral clarity. During the test dose, her muscles—historically tight and spastic—turned to what she described as "limp noodles"[5] . This sudden loss of tension was a moment of duality: it brought excitement to her mother and medical team, who saw the potential for normalized movement, but terror to the young Kaleigh, whose bodily schema was built around high muscle tone. She recalled being scared because "she had never been able to move that much without feeling pain," highlighting the gap between clinical success and the patient's subjective experience of their own body[5] .

In 2003, as a teenager, Trace proceeded with the surgery to implant the baclofen pump[5] . The procedure was successful, marking a shift in her physical capabilities. The pump allowed her to discard crutches for short distances, improved her gait and posture, and notably, granted her the fine motor dexterity required to play the piano[5] . This integration of technology into her body—becoming, in a theoretical sense, a "cyborg"—granted her a level of independence that was crucial for her development during her university years. It allowed her to walk "in stride with her friends and family," reducing the physical gap between her and her able-bodied peers, even as she remained culturally distinct[5] .

### 2.3. Adolescence: The Politics of Uncool
Trace’s adolescence in the late 1990s and 2000s was shaped by a profound sense of exclusion from the sexual marketplace. In her essays, she reflects on the cultural landscape of that era, dominated by pop icons like Britney Spears and Christina Aguilera[6] . These figures represented a specific, exclusionary "pinnacle of beauty and sex appeal": thin, white, and aggressively able-bodied[6] .

Navigating high school as a disabled girl, Trace felt "critically uncool"[7] . Her disability was impossible to hide; she described her gait as "swaying," with legs that "scissored in and out," often leading her to "tumble over completely"[6] . This hyper-visibility created a paradox: she was constantly looked at, yet rarely seen as a subject of desire. She internalized this gaze, feeling "inherently undesirable" and becoming "mostly indifferent to sex" as a protective mechanism[6] . She remained "in the closet" regarding her queerness for years, as the intersection of disability and queerness felt like an impossible navigation in a heteronormative, ableist environment[6] .

## 3\. Academic Foundations and Professional Evolution
Trace’s transition from a "critically uncool" teenager to a leading voice in sex education was mediated by her engagement with higher education and feminist theory. Her academic path provided the lexicon she needed to articulate her lived experience, transforming personal grievance into structural critique.

### 3.1. Higher Education
Trace attended **Dalhousie University** in Halifax, Nova Scotia, graduating with a **Bachelor of Arts (BA)** in 2009[1] . She has humorously noted that graduating with a BA in the middle of a global recession was "not a super useful degree," yet this period was intellectually foundational[6] . It was at Dalhousie that she likely first encountered the feminist critical theory that would later draw her to her work at Venus Envy.

Following her time in Halifax, Trace sought to professionalize her therapeutic instincts. She enrolled at the **University of Guelph**, a respected institution for family therapy training, where she earned a **Master of Science (MSc) in Couple and Family Therapy**.1 This degree bridged the gap between her work as a peer educator and her later career as a clinician. It provided her with the systemic frameworks necessary to understand relationships not just as interpersonal connections, but as structures influenced by power, privilege, and societal norms.

### 3.2. The Venus Envy Era (2009–2017)
The crucible of Trace’s career was **Venus Envy**, an education-based sex shop and bookstore in Halifax. Trace applied for a job there in 2009, not because of an interest in sex toys—she admitted to being "less interested" in the merchandise initially—but because the store was famous for its "incredible collection of feminist critical theory"[6] .

Hired for her retail experience, Trace worked at Venus Envy for eight years, from 2009 to 2017[8] . This environment acted as a second university. She describes the transition from being an inexperienced, closeted 23-year-old to a vocal sex educator as a radical transformation. Her role quickly expanded beyond retail; she became the **Education Coordinator**, a position that required her to facilitate workshops across Nova Scotia[9] .

#### The Workshops and "Grapefruiting"
Trace’s workshops were renowned for their blend of humor, explicit instruction, and inclusivity. She taught diverse groups, ranging from bachelorette parties to seniors' facilities, covering topics from consent to oral sex[6] .

A notable aspect of her tenure at Venus Envy was her willingness to engage with viral sex culture "for science." In a 2018 article in *The Coast*, her colleague Christine Ollier recounted how Trace and the staff experimented with "Grapefruiting"—a sexual technique made viral by "Auntie Angel"—to see if it was a viable topic for their fellatio workshops[10] . This anecdote illustrates Trace’s pedagogical style: a refusal to be embarrassed, a commitment to testing sexual scripts, and a belief that sex education should be fun, messy, and practical.

### 3.3. Digital Activism: The Fucking Facts
To document her growing expertise and the specificities of disabled sexuality, Trace launched the blog **"The Fucking Facts"** around 2009/2010.6 The blog was a groundbreaking platform in the Canadian digital landscape. Its mission was to "document the pleasure and joy of sex for people with disabilities," serving as a direct counter-narrative to the prevailing medical models that desexualized disabled bodies[6] .

The blog was characterized by the "resonance of a single human voice" and a "wicked sense of humour"[11] . It covered topics that were rarely discussed in mainstream media, such as the logistics of sex with mobility aids, the intersection of queer identity and disability, and the reclaiming of explicit language.

#### The "Blurred Lines" Parody
Trace’s digital activism extended to video. In 2013, at the height of the controversy surrounding Robin Thicke’s song "Blurred Lines"—which was widely criticized by feminists for minimizing consent—Trace co-wrote and appeared in a viral parody video[9] . Her version flipped the script to focus explicitly on the importance of consent, using humor and satire to engage with a massive cultural moment. This video gained her a measure of "internet celebrity" and demonstrated her ability to pivot from personal blogging to broader cultural critique[9] .

#### Audio Smut Collaboration
Trace’s storytelling also found a home in audio formats. She collaborated with **Audio Smut** (which later evolved into the podcast *The Heart*) to produce a story about her early sexual experiences[12] . This piece, part of an episode on "Coming of Age," explored the rituals of independence—shaving, driving, and sex—further establishing her voice as a key narrator of the disabled coming-of-age experience[12] .

## 4\. Literary Works: *Hot, Wet, and Shaking*
Kaleigh Trace’s literary reputation rests primarily on her memoir, *Hot, Wet, and Shaking: How I Learned to Talk About Sex*. First published in 2014, the book is a hybrid text that blends traditional memoir with sex education guide and feminist manifesto.

### 4.1. The 2014 Edition
**Genesis:** The book grew directly out of her blog, *The Fucking Facts*. Trace was approached at a party by Robbie MacGregor of **Invisible Publishing**, who suggested she write a book. Despite the casual nature of the offer, MacGregor followed up with a "very kind email," leading Trace to sign on for the project[6] .

**Content and Style:** The book is structured as a journey from "ignorance to bliss"[13] . It chronicles her evolution from a teenager who felt excluded from desire to a "sex-positive sex educator" who teaches blowjob workshops[14] .

* **Radical Language:** Trace insists on using words like "cunt," "come," and "fuck." She argues that avoiding these words allows "parameters to get set by somebody else," which is dangerous for marginalized bodies[11] .  
* **The Abortion Narrative:** A critical chapter details her experience having an abortion in her mid-20s. Trace describes herself as being "radicalized by having an abortion"[3] . Reviewers noted that this section was vital because it treated abortion not as an unspeakable trauma but as a routine medical procedure and a common part of sexual life, thereby destigmatizing it[11] .  
* **Disability as "Is":** Reviewers praised the book for its non-theoretical approach to disability. *Broken Pencil* noted that unlike academic texts that deconstruct disability, Trace’s narrative presents disability as something that "simply is"[15] . It depicts the disabled body as a site of embarrassment, humor, and triumph, akin to "sitting with your best friend recounting the embarrassingly hilarious date you had the night before"[15] .

**Reception:** The 2014 edition was a critical success in Canada.

* **Awards:** It won the **2015 Evelyn Richardson Memorial Non-Fiction Award** at the Atlantic Book Awards, the region's highest honor for non-fiction[1] .  
* **Critical Praise:** *The National Post* praised her lack of clinical distance, stating the book read like the "honest musings of a woman in a pair of yesterday's dirty jeans"[7] *The Coast* declared that Trace had "learned to talk about sex and she has learned to do it extremely well"[16] .

### 4.2. The 2024 10th Anniversary Edition
In 2024, Invisible Publishing released a revised and expanded edition of the memoir. This release was poignant, as it coincided with a major life event for Trace.

**Context:** Weeks after agreeing to the republication, Trace was diagnosed with terminal cancer[3] . Rather than canceling the project, she used the new edition to bridge the gap between sex and death.

**New Material:**

* **Essays on Mortality:** The updated edition includes new essays on grief, medicine, and the terminal diagnosis[7] . Trace explores the thematic continuity between her sex work and her death work: both require a surrender of control and a rejection of shame.  
* **Christa Couture’s Contribution:** The edition features an afterword by **Christa Couture**, a prominent disabled indigenous writer and broadcaster[7] . This collaboration signifies a dialogue between two of Canada’s most important voices on disability, creating a lineage of "crip wisdom."  
* **Updated Foreword:** Trace penned a new foreword contemplating her future and the "second coming" of the book (a pun she gleefully acknowledges)[3] .

## 5\. Journalism and Media Career
Parallel to her book writing, Trace maintained a prolific career as a journalist and columnist, primarily within the alternative press.

### 5.1. *The Coast* Columnist
Trace was a regular contributor to *The Coast*, Halifax's weekly alternative newspaper. Her writing here was essential in cementing her status as a local public intellectual.

* **Scope:** Her columns covered a wide array of topics, from local queer events to reviews of cultural phenomena. She was listed as a "Writer," "Sex Educator," and "Therapist" in their archives[1] .  
* **Tone:** Her journalistic voice mirrored her memoirist voice: cheeky, unapologetic, and deeply embedded in the Halifax community. She used this platform to normalize discussions of sex toys and queer relationships for a general readership.

### 5.2. Other Publications
Trace’s byline has appeared in major Canadian publications, indicating her crossover appeal from niche activist circles to mainstream media.

* **Toronto Life:** In August 2024, she published a harrowing and beautiful personal essay titled *"I wrote a book about learning to love my body. After finding out I have terminal cancer, I’ve decided to republish it"*.6 This piece brought her story to a national audience, detailing her journey from the car accident at age eight to her current terminal diagnosis.  
* **Shameless Magazine:** She contributed to this feminist magazine for young women and trans youth, aligning with her goal of providing the representation she lacked as a teenager[2] .  
* **CBC Radio:** Trace has been a featured guest on CBC’s flagship programs. She appeared on *Tapestry* in an episode titled "Sex educator shares her path to radical self-acceptance" (2018) and on *The Current* discussing disability and sex education (2017)[8] .

## 6\. Clinical Practice and Therapeutic Philosophy
Trace’s work as a psychotherapist is an extension of her activism. She is a **Registered Psychotherapist** with the College of Registered Psychotherapists of Ontario[18] .

### 6.1. "Justice-Doing" Therapy
Trace explicitly politicizes her therapeutic practice. She describes therapy as "justice-doing work," a framework that likely draws from Narrative Therapy and feminist psychology[18] . In this model, the therapist is not a neutral observer but a witness who "advocates for and resists alongside clients"[18] .

### 6.2. Specialization
Her practice focuses on **couples and sex therapy**. She operates from the belief that "intimate relationships are sites of healing"[14] . Her background allows her to serve clients who might feel alienated by traditional medical settings, particularly queer and disabled individuals. She brings a stance of "non-judgmental openness" to the therapy room, leveraging her own visibly disabled body to disrupt the traditional power dynamics between "healthy doctor" and "sick patient"[18] .

## 7\. The 2024 Diagnosis and Philosophy of Mortality
In mid-2024, at the age of 38, Kaleigh Trace’s life and work took a sudden, tragic, yet generative turn.

### 7.1. The Diagnosis
Trace was diagnosed with **terminal cancer**.3 In interviews, she has alluded to the cancer being metastatic, affecting her bones and lungs, and drastically reducing her energy levels[6] . The diagnosis was given with a prognosis of "2" (the unit of time—months or years—was cut off in the snippet, but the implication of "terminal" is clear)[3] .

### 7.2. "Holding On While Facing Death"
The diagnosis forced Trace to stop working full-time. A self-described "workaholic" who defined herself by her capacity to help others, she had to take a year off to manage her physical decline[6] . She has since returned to part-time work, but her lifestyle has shifted to accommodate her lower energy, including taking up "punch-needling" as a hobby[2] .

### 7.3. The Synthesis of Sex and Death
Trace’s response to her diagnosis has been to theorize it through the lens of her previous work. She argues that the skills she learned as a sex educator—vulnerability, communication, body awareness, and the rejection of shame—are the exact skills needed to die well.

* **Vulnerability:** In an interview with *The Tyee*, she stated, "If you want the best sex of your life, you're going to have to be vulnerable"[4] . She applies this same logic to death, arguing that pretending to be "strong" or "invincible" robs one of the connection that comes from shared vulnerability.  
* **Bodily Autonomy:** Just as she fought for the right to pleasure in a disabled body, she now advocates for the right to grieve and experience the reality of a dying body without the "shaming pressure of expectation" to be a "warrior"[7] .

## 8\. Theoretical Contributions and Legacy
Kaleigh Trace’s work constitutes a significant contribution to **Crip Theory**, a branch of disability studies that intersects with queer theory.

### 8.1. Crip Theory and the Rejection of Cure
Trace often uses the term "crip" (a reclaimed slur) to describe her community and politics[14] . Her work aligns with theorists like Eli Clare (whom she has appeared alongside on CBC) in rejecting the "cure" mentality[19] . Instead of viewing her cerebral palsy or spinal injury as tragedies to be fixed, she views them as integral parts of her embodiment that shape her unique access to pleasure.

### 8.2. Radical Self-Acceptance
Trace’s concept of "radical self-acceptance" is not a passive state but an active, political practice. It involves accepting the "leaky," "spastic," or "dying" body in a world that demands containment and health. As noted by reviewers, her work serves as a "lighthouse" for others navigating the "unchartered waters of sex and disability"[15] .

## 11\. Conclusion
Kaleigh Trace’s career has been defined by the persistent, joyful, and rigorous interrogation of what it means to live in a body that the world deems "imperfect." From her early days navigating the physical pain of spasticity and the social pain of exclusion, to her rise as a celebrated author and sex educator, Trace has consistently turned her personal experience into public pedagogy. Her 2024 diagnosis of terminal cancer has not silenced this voice but deepened it, allowing her to offer a final, profound lesson on the continuity between the vulnerability of orgasm and the vulnerability of the end of life. As she noted in her memoir, "Desire is holy, sex is church," and through her work, she has expanded that congregation to include the disabled, the queer, and the dying, offering them a benediction of radical pleasure and acceptance[7] .
