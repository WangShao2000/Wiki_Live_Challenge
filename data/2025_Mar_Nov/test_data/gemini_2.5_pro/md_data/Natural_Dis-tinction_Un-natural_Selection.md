# Natural Dis-tinction Un-natural Selection
**Natural Dis-tinction Un-natural Selection** is the thirty-third collection by the British fashion designer Alexander McQueen, presented for the Spring/Summer 2009 season. Staged on October 3, 2008, at Le Centquatre in Paris, the collection is widely recognized by fashion historians and critics as a pivotal moment in McQueen's oeuvre, marking a significant aesthetic and technological transition toward the digitally driven innovation that would define his final works. Drawing direct thematic inspiration from Charles Darwin’s seminal scientific text *On the Origin of Species*, the collection explored the tension between the natural world and the encroaching influence of industrialization, technology, and human intervention[1] .

Distinguished by its pioneering application of digitally engineered prints—a sophisticated textile technique where imagery is mapped precisely to the geometry of the garment rather than printed in a repeating pattern—the collection is frequently cited in fashion historiography as a precursor to McQueen’s magnum opus, *Plato’s Atlantis* (Spring/Summer 2010)[4] . The presentation featured a sharp dichotomy of aesthetics: the first half celebrated organic forms through soft tailoring and natural imagery, while the second half transitioned into structured, crystalline, and "un-natural" silhouettes that suggested a forced evolution or artificial interference[2] .

The collection was presented during a period of intense creativity for the designer, who was increasingly interested in the intersection of nature and technology. This interest was not merely aesthetic but technical; McQueen sought to utilize emerging textile technologies to realize his vision of a modern, perhaps dystopian, evolution. The collection's timing coincided with the 150th anniversary of the publication of Darwin's *On the Origin of Species* (1859), a connection explicitly acknowledged in the show's program notes[1] . Unlike the darker, more aggressive themes of his earlier work, such as *Highland Rape* (1995) or *Voss* (2001), this collection approached its subject with a sense of wonder and complexity, balancing the beauty of the natural world with the sharp, synthetic edges of modernity[2] .

## Background
By 2008, Alexander McQueen had established a reputation for staging elaborate, narrative-driven runway presentations that often bordered on performance art. Following the melancholic and autobiographical *The Girl Who Lived in the Tree* (Autumn/Winter 2008), which drew heavily on British heritage and royal iconography, *Natural Dis-tinction Un-natural Selection* represented a shift outward, focusing on global environmental themes and the history of science[2] . This shift marked a departure from the intensely personal narratives of his previous collections, moving towards a broader commentary on the state of the planet and humanity's role within it.

### The 150th Anniversary of Darwinism
The year 2009 marked the bicentenary of Charles Darwin's birth and the 150th anniversary of the publication of *On the Origin of Species*. This scientific milestone provided the intellectual framework for McQueen's collection. The designer, known for his deep engagement with history and nature, used Darwin's theories of natural selection as a springboard to explore the evolution of fashion itself[1] . The collection's title, a play on Darwin's terminology, suggested a disruption of the natural order—a "dis-tinction" rather than a selection—implying that the evolutionary process was now being driven by unnatural, human-made forces.

### Technological Context in Fashion
In the late 2000s, the fashion industry was on the cusp of a digital revolution. While digital printing had existed, it was often used for simple, repeating patterns. McQueen sought to push this technology further, employing "engineered prints" that were designed specifically for the three-dimensional form of the garment. This required a level of precision and collaboration between textile designers and pattern cutters that was unprecedented at the time[5] . The collection served as a testing ground for these techniques, which would later be perfected in *Plato's Atlantis*.

## Concept and Themes
The overarching narrative of *Natural Dis-tinction Un-natural Selection* centers on the concept of evolution, interpreted through both biological and industrial lenses. The collection is structured as a binary dialogue between the organic and the artificial, exploring the "interplay between the natural and the artificial".

### Darwinism and Evolution
McQueen used the runway to visualize the progression of life forms, albeit through a fashion lexicon. The designer stated that the collection was inspired by "evolution both in a conceptual and technical sense". This dual focus allowed for a thematic exploration of raw materials found in nature—such as wood, granite, and crystal—alongside the "dawn of the industrial age," represented by motifs of coiled metal and engineering feats like the Eiffel Tower. The garments themselves seemed to evolve on the runway, moving from soft, fluid shapes to rigid, structured forms.

### Nature vs. Industry
The collection is structured as a binary dialogue between the organic and the artificial. The initial looks presented a vision of "nature unspoiled by man," featuring bucolic embroideries, soft colors, and fluid lines[1] . As the show progressed, the aesthetic shifted toward the "technological or super-natural," characterized by sharper silhouettes, synthetic fabrics, and prints that morphed organic matter into geometric or skeletal forms[1] . This progression mirrored the narrative of the Anthropocene, suggesting that the "natural distinction" of species was being supplanted by an "un-natural selection" driven by human activity and technological advancement[8] .

### Environmentalism
While McQueen noted in the program that his intention was "not to preach," the collection is widely interpreted as a commentary on the environmental crisis and global warming[2] . The staging, which featured a backdrop of a rotating earth and taxidermy animals, evoked the fragility of the natural world. Contemporary reviews questioned the designer's new stance, with critic Sarah Mower asking, "Alexander McQueen, environmentalist?" in her review for *Vogue*, highlighting the unexpected directness of the ecological theme. The collection's visual narrative suggested a world where nature is preserved only in "specimen jars" or as taxidermy, a haunting prediction of a future where the natural world is entirely artificial.

### The Un-natural Selection
The "un-natural" aspect of the collection title referred to the artificial intervention in the evolutionary process. McQueen explored the idea that humanity, through technology and industrialization, has become the primary agent of selection, overriding natural processes. This was visually represented by the transition from organic prints to crystalline structures and metallic embellishments. The collection posed the question: what happens when evolution is no longer natural?

## Development and Techniques
The technical achievements of *Natural Dis-tinction Un-natural Selection* are significant within the history of 21st-century fashion, particularly regarding textile design and garment construction. McQueen's studio pushed the boundaries of what was possible with digital printing and fabric manipulation.

### Engineered Digital Prints
The most defining innovation of the collection was the extensive use of "engineered prints." Unlike traditional textile printing, where a pattern repeats continuously across a bolt of fabric, engineered prints are digitally designed to fit the specific dimensions and seams of a garment's pattern pieces[4] .

McQueen and his design team utilized high-resolution digital photography of natural textures—agate, crystal, wood grain, and granite—and manipulated these images to wrap around the body in a way that enhanced the silhouette[4] . For example, a print might be positioned so that the visual "grain" of the wood tapered at the waist to create an hourglass illusion, or a crystal geode pattern exploded from the shoulder to emphasize width[4] . This technique required a high degree of collaboration between the textile designers and the pattern cutters, as the print placement had to be finalized before the fabric was even cut. Sarah Burton, McQueen's right-hand and eventual successor, later noted that McQueen had such a precise eye that he could "drape an engineered print," a task usually requiring complex computer modeling.

### Materiality and Embellishment
In addition to the printed textiles, the collection utilized advanced construction techniques to create "artful, multidimensional effects".

* **Swarovski Crystals:** The "un-natural" segment of the show featured garments heavily encrusted with Swarovski crystals. These were not merely decorative but structural; in the case of the "Bell Jar" dress, the crystal mesh was so dense that it formed a rigid, sculptural carapace[6] . The crystals were used to mimic the texture of minerals and rocks, blurring the line between fabric and stone.  
* **Fabric Manipulation:** The collection included dresses where enamelled flowers were "trapped" between layers of tulle, mimicking the appearance of specimens preserved in jars[1] . Other pieces used bonded leather and "stretch synthetic" materials to achieve a futuristic, almost alien skin[6] .  
* **Tailoring:** True to McQueen’s Savile Row training, the tailoring remained rigorous. "Frock coats" and "skinny-leg pants" provided a structured counterpoint to the more experimental shapes, often rendered in the wood-grain or granite prints to merge the classic with the organic.

### The "Bell Jar" Technique
One of the most technically demanding aspects of the collection was the creation of the "Bell Jar" silhouette. These dresses were designed to stand away from the body, creating a rounded, protective shape reminiscent of a glass specimen jar. This was achieved through the use of specialized padding and support structures, particularly in the skirt, to maintain the volume and shape. The net base of the dress was then densely covered in Swarovski crystals, adding significant weight but also structural integrity to the garment.

## Runway Show
The runway presentation for *Natural Dis-tinction Un-natural Selection* was staged on October 3, 2008, at **Le Centquatre** (104) in Paris[3] . The choice of venue was significant; Le Centquatre was a massive 19th-century structure that had formerly served as a municipal undertaker’s complex and morgue. This added a layer of *memento mori* to the proceedings—a recurring motif in McQueen's work—reinforcing the themes of life, death, and preservation[3] .

### Set Design
The runway was designed to resemble a museum diorama or a "Noah's Ark" of endangered species. The catwalk was lined with a menagerie of high-quality antique taxidermy, including a tiger, zebra, giraffe, polar bear, and elephant[2] . These silent, preserved observers reinforced the theme of preservation and the stillness of the natural world in the face of human expansion. The juxtaposition of the models with the taxidermy created a visual dialogue between the living and the preserved, the natural and the artificial.

At the rear of the runway, a large circular screen projected elemental imagery: a "fiery golden sun," a "cool silver moon," and a rotating Earth. At one point, the projection shifted to an image of McQueen’s own eyeball, suggesting the designer’s watchful gaze over the "natural distinction" unfolding before the audience.

### Soundtrack
The auditory landscape of the show was divided into two distinct movements to mirror the collection's thematic split.

1. **Part One (Nature):** The show opened with a soundscape evoking a "beautiful jungle," utilizing steel drums and tribal chants. This accompanied the flow of the more organic, soft, and nature-inspired garments, creating an atmosphere of "nature unspoiled by man".  
2. **Part Two (Un-natural):** As the collection transitioned to the industrial and technological aesthetics, the music shifted to a darker, more electronic tone, though reviews noted it was "nowhere near as dark" as typical McQueen shows, maintaining a thread of optimism.

### Hair and Makeup
The styling for the show complemented the "alien" and "futuristic" evolution of the models.

* **Hair:** The hair was styled to look "plastered" around the face, creating a sleek, hydrodynamic, or "alien" appearance that removed the softness of human hair from the silhouette. This look contributed to the "un-natural" aesthetic of the models, making them appear as if they were evolving into a new species.  
* **Makeup:** The makeup often featured pale, alabaster skin, serving as a blank canvas that highlighted the vivid colors of the garments. The look was less aggressive than the "clown" makeup of the subsequent *Horn of Plenty* collection, fitting the "specimen" aesthetic.

## The Collection
The collection comprised forty-one looks, evolving from soft, organic naturalism to hard-edged, crystalline industrialism. This progression was not merely visual but narrative, telling the story of the Earth's transformation from a natural paradise to a technological construct.

### Phase I: Natural Distinction
The first half of the show presented a romanticized vision of nature.

* **Silhouettes:** Soft, flowing lines, draped chiffons, and "cocoon" shapes. The tailoring featured rounded shoulders and hips, exaggerating the female form in a manner reminiscent of the *Venus of Willendorf* or biological curves.  
* **Prints and Textures:** This section introduced the engineered prints in their most organic forms. Garments appeared to be hewn from wood or carved from granite. One standout look featured a "wood-print tailoring" ensemble where the grain of the wood emphasized the cut of the jacket.  
* **Details:** "Bucolic embroideries" and silk flowers were prominent. Some dresses featured "flowers trapped beneath nude chiffon," giving the impression of pressed flowers or specimens viewed through a soft focus.

### Phase II: Un-natural Selection
The second half marked the intrusion of the artificial.

* **The "Bell Jar" Dress:** One of the most iconic pieces from the collection is the "Bell Jar" dress (Look 18). Sculpted to resemble the shape of a Victorian glass specimen jar, the dress was constructed from a net base densely covered in Swarovski crystals[6] . The silhouette exaggerated the hips to an extreme degree, creating a shape that was simultaneously protective and restrictive.  
* **Crystal and Mineral Prints:** The prints evolved from matte wood and stone to sharp, refracting crystal and diamond patterns. One specific design featured a "diamond print which morphs into a human skeleton," directly visualizing the fusion of the organic body with mineral rigidity[1] .  
* **Industrial Motifs:** Images of the Eiffel Tower and coiled metal springs were engineered onto leggings and dresses, representing the "dawn of the industrial age".  
* **Color Palette:** The soft earth tones of the first half gave way to "virulent shades" of acid yellow, vivid pink, and sapphire blue, representing enamelled or synthetic interpretations of natural colors[1] .

### Footwear and Accessories
The footwear for *Natural Dis-tinction Un-natural Selection* featured platform heels, often described in retrospect as precursors to the "Armadillo" boots of *Plato's Atlantis*. However, the S/S 2009 shoes were distinct: they were often "moulded toe" leather heels or sandals with complex strapping that resembled "delicate wrappings"[8] . Some designs featured resin or pearl heels that mimicked animal claws or organic protrusions. The jewelry, created in collaboration with Shaun Leane, often incorporated natural materials like pheasant claws and pearls, blending the beautiful with the macabre.

## Collaborators
Alexander McQueen's creative process was highly collaborative, and *Natural Dis-tinction Un-natural Selection* relied on the expertise of his long-time creative partners.

### Shaun Leane
The jeweler Shaun Leane, a frequent collaborator with McQueen, created the jewelry for the collection. Leane's work for this season often combined precious materials with organic, sometimes "vulgar," elements. For example, necklaces featuring pheasant claws and Tahitian pearls were designed to challenge conventions of beauty and luxury. Leane's pieces reinforced the collection's theme of "natural distinction," blurring the line between adornment and biological specimen.

### Guido Palau
The hair for the show was styled by Guido Palau, another long-term collaborator. Palau created the "plastered" hair look, which removed the volume and softness of the models' hair, contributing to the streamlined, "alien" aesthetic. This styling choice was crucial in unifying the collection's narrative, presenting the models as a new, evolved species.

### Peter Philips
Makeup artist Peter Philips created the beauty look for the show. The makeup was characterized by "alabaster skin" and no eyebrows, giving the models a blank, almost robotic appearance. This underscored the "un-natural" theme, suggesting a departure from human norms.

## Reception
Critical reception to *Natural Dis-tinction Un-natural Selection* was generally positive, though marked by a degree of surprise at the collection's earnest environmental message and lack of overt "shock" tactics compared to McQueen's historical extravaganzas.

### Contemporary Reviews
* **Sarah Mower** (writing for *Vogue* and *Style.com*) praised the collection's technical ingenuity, specifically identifying the "engineered prints" as a major trend emanating from London designers. She noted the humor in McQueen's bow (he appeared in a rabbit suit) and questioned the depth of his sudden environmentalism, though she acknowledged the beauty of the execution[5] .  
* ***Women's Wear Daily*** **(WWD)** described the show as "powerful" and noted McQueen's "restraint," appreciating that the designer avoided "unleashing anger" despite the serious subject matter.  
* **Critical Consensus:** Reviewers often preferred the "more romantic first half" of the show, finding the natural imagery more accessible than the rigid, crystal-encrusted second half. The collection was seen as a "mature" work, balancing McQueen's theatrical instincts with a focus on wearable, albeit avant-garde, clothing.

### Retrospective Analysis
In the context of McQueen’s full body of work, this collection is viewed as a critical bridge. It introduced the **digital print revolution** that would culminate in *Plato's Atlantis* (S/S 2010), a collection that is often cited as changing the course of fashion history[5] .

* **Technological Legacy:** The collection is credited with popularizing the use of digital prints in high fashion. McQueen's ability to "drape an engineered print" set a new standard for textile design.  
* **Environmental Legacy:** The collection is seen as an early example of high fashion engaging with the **Anthropocene** and climate change, prefiguring the industry-wide shift toward sustainability narratives in the following decades.  
* **Museum Quality:** The "Bell Jar" dress and the engineered print dresses are now considered museum-quality examples of McQueen's ability to merge couture craftsmanship with industrial technology.

## Legacy and Museum Holdings
Garments from *Natural Dis-tinction Un-natural Selection* are held in major museum collections worldwide and have been featured in significant retrospective exhibitions.

### Alexander McQueen: Savage Beauty
The landmark retrospective *Alexander McQueen: Savage Beauty*, staged at the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York (2011) and the Victoria and Albert Museum in London (2015), featured key looks from this collection. The "Bell Jar" dress was a highlight of the "Nature" and "Natural Distinctions" sections of the exhibition, showcasing McQueen's fascination with the natural world[6] . The exhibition context emphasized the collection's role in McQueen's broader exploration of nature and technology.

### Lee Alexander McQueen: Mind, Mythos, Muse
The collection was also featured in the touring exhibition *Lee Alexander McQueen: Mind, Mythos, Muse* (2023), organized by the Los Angeles County Museum of Art (LACMA) and the National Gallery of Victoria (NGV). This exhibition contextualized McQueen's work alongside art history and historical artifacts, highlighting the technical precision of the engineered prints and their connection to geological forms[3] .

### Conservation
The "Bell Jar" dress presents unique challenges for textile conservators. Due to the extreme density of the Swarovski crystals, the dress is exceptionally heavy. When displayed, it requires custom-made padding and support structures to maintain its "Victorian bell jar" silhouette and prevent the weight of the crystals from distorting the net base. This highlights the sculptural nature of McQueen's designs, which often prioritized form over function.
