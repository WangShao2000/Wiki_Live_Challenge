# Second-Ponce de Leon Baptist Church: An Institutional, Architectural, and Theological History
## 1\. Introduction and Overview
Second-Ponce de Leon Baptist Church (SPdL), situated at 2715 Peachtree Road in the Buckhead district of Atlanta, Georgia, represents a seminal institution in the religious, architectural, and political history of the American South[1] . Established in its current form in 1932 through the merger of two historic congregations—Second Baptist Church (founded 1854\) and Ponce de Leon Baptist Church (founded 1904)—the institution has evolved from a downtown mission into a "Cathedral of Buckhead," wielding significant influence within the Georgia Baptist Convention and the broader socio-political fabric of Atlanta[1] .

As of late 2025, the church is characterized by its dual affiliation with the Southern Baptist Convention (SBC) and the Cooperative Baptist Fellowship (CBF), a stance that reflects its historical role as a bastion of moderate Baptist theology amidst the denomination's schisms of the late 20th century[1] . The church campus, dominated by a sanctuary designed by architect George Harwell Bond in the Georgian Colonial style, stands as a visual landmark at the intersection of Peachtree Road and East Wesley Road[1] . Beyond its architectural prominence, Second-Ponce is noted for its distinguished membership history, having served as the spiritual home for multiple Georgia governors, federal judges, and captains of industry, earning it a reputation as a nexus of civic and spiritual power[5] .

In the contemporary era, under the interim leadership of Rev. Ronnie Brewer, the church continues to adapt to the changing religious landscape of North America[8] . It has pioneered unique ecumenical partnerships, most notably with the neighboring Cathedral of Christ the King, and maintains extensive community outreach through its Community Wellness Center and music ministries[9] . This report provides an exhaustive analysis of the church's origins, architectural development, theological trajectory, and current operational status.

## 2\. Antebellum Origins: The History of Second Baptist Church (1854–1904)
The institutional lineage of Second-Ponce de Leon Baptist Church begins in the antebellum period with the establishment of Second Baptist Church. To understand the modern institution, one must first examine the distinct histories of its two predecessor bodies, the oldest of which traces its roots to the decade preceding the American Civil War.

### 2.1 Establishment and Early Mission
Second Baptist Church was founded in 1854[11] . It emerged as the second Baptist congregation in the growing railroad terminus of Atlanta, planted as a mission of the city's First Baptist Church, which had itself been established only six years prior in 1848[1] . Located in the heart of downtown Atlanta, near the site that would later house the Georgia State Capitol, Second Baptist was positioned to serve the commercial and residential core of the young city[1] .

The formation of Second Baptist reflected the rapid population growth of Atlanta in the 1850s. As the city expanded, the need for neighborhood-based congregations grew, prompting First Baptist to colonize new areas of the municipality. Second Baptist quickly established itself as a vibrant, independent congregation, drawing membership from the merchants and families residing in the city center.

### 2.2 The Civil War and the Intercession of Father O’Reilly (1864)
The most pivotal event in the early history of Second Baptist occurred during the American Civil War, specifically during the Siege of Atlanta in 1864\. As Union General William T. Sherman’s forces encircled the city, an order was given to bombard and subsequently burn the municipal infrastructure to cripple the Confederate war effort.

Historical records indicate that Second Baptist Church was spared from the conflagration that consumed much of Atlanta. This preservation is attributed to the extraordinary intercession of Father Thomas O’Reilly, the priest of the nearby Immaculate Conception Catholic Church[11] . O’Reilly, who ministered to both Confederate and Union soldiers (many of whom were Irish Catholics), leveraged his relationship with Union commanders to negotiate the protection of the city’s houses of worship.

O’Reilly warned the Union forces that if they destroyed the Catholic church, he would ensure that the Catholic soldiers in the Union ranks would mutiny or withdraw their support. He extended this protection to include the protestant churches in the vicinity, including Second Baptist, Central Presbyterian, St. Philip’s Episcopal, and Trinity Methodist[11] . Consequently, Second Baptist survived the burning of Atlanta, standing as one of the few antebellum structures remaining in the city post-1864. This event established an early, if informal, precedent for ecumenical cooperation—a theme that would resurface forcefully in the church's 21st-century history.

### 2.3 Post-War Growth and Urban Challenges
Following Reconstruction, Second Baptist continued to thrive in its downtown location. By the late 19th century, it was a central pillar of Atlanta's religious community. In 1939, a bell that had been installed in the original Second Baptist building was moved to the new Buckhead sanctuary, serving as a physical continuity between the antebellum congregation and the modern church[1] .

However, as the 20th century dawned, the urbanization of downtown Atlanta began to present challenges. The residential population began to migrate northward, fleeing the congestion and commercialization of the city center. This demographic shift—a precursor to the later "white flight" of the mid-century—began to erode the membership base of downtown churches, setting the stage for the eventual relocation and merger that would define the church's future[4] .

## 3\. The Expansion Era: Ponce de Leon Baptist Church (1904–1929)
While Second Baptist anchored the downtown religious landscape, the city's northward expansion along the streetcar lines prompted the formation of new congregations.

### 3.1 Formation and Location
Ponce de Leon Baptist Church was established in 1904, exactly fifty years after Second Baptist[1] . Like its predecessor, it began as a mission of First Baptist Church, designed to serve the affluent and growing neighborhoods developing along Ponce de Leon Avenue[4] .

The original sanctuary for this new congregation was erected at the intersection of Ponce de Leon Avenue and Piedmont Avenue[4] . This location placed the church at a cultural crossroads of early 20th-century Atlanta. Archival photographs from the Haralson Bleckley collection, dated 1909, depict the facade of this structure as a classical edifice featuring prominent columns and a grand staircase, reflecting the architectural tastes of the Edwardian era South[4] .

### 3.2 Relocation to Buckhead (1929)
By the late 1920s, the demographics of Atlanta were shifting once again. The congregation of Ponce de Leon Baptist found itself spread out as members moved further north into the developing suburb of Buckhead. In 1929, seeking to follow its membership and secure a location with greater longevity, the church made the strategic decision to relocate[4] .

The congregation sold its building at Ponce de Leon and Piedmont to the Central Congregational Church and purchased a tract of land at the corner of Peachtree Road and Wesley Avenue (now East Wesley Road)[4] . This move was prescient, positioning the church in what would become the wealthiest and most influential district of Atlanta. A new building was completed on this site in 1930, but the timing proved perilous[4] . The stock market crash of 1929 and the subsequent Great Depression immediately placed the relocated congregation under severe financial strain.

## 4\. The Great Depression and the Merger of 1932
The early 1930s presented an existential crisis for religious institutions across the United States, and Atlanta was no exception. The economic collapse decimated church budgets, while the shifting urban geography left older congregations isolated.

### 4.1 The Imperative for Union
By 1932, both Second Baptist Church and Ponce de Leon Baptist Church were facing decline.

* **Second Baptist** was struggling with a downtown location that had become largely commercial, losing its residential proximity to the membership[1] .  
* **Ponce de Leon Baptist**, despite its prime location in Buckhead, was burdened by debt from its 1929 relocation and building project, exacerbated by the Depression-era economy[1] .

Recognizing that separate survival was increasingly untenable, the leadership of both congregations opened negotiations for a merger. The logic was clear: Second Baptist possessed the historic endowment and the "mother church" prestige, while Ponce de Leon Baptist possessed the strategic real estate in the city's future growth corridor.

### 4.2 The Merger Agreement
The merger was formalized in November 1932\. Historical sources present a slight discrepancy regarding the exact date of the agreement:

* A 1954 church history states the merger document was signed on **November 9, 1932**.1  
* A 2003 history of Buckhead cites the date as **November 20, 1932**.1  
* Other sources place the "birth" of the new institution on **November 18, 1932**.11

Regardless of the specific day, the result was the creation of **Second-Ponce de Leon Baptist Church**. The merger combined the membership rolls and assets of both bodies. Additionally, snippets suggest a third entity, **Buckhead Baptist Church**, may have been involved in the earlier consolidation that formed the Ponce de Leon congregation’s presence in Buckhead in 1929, or was absorbed into the 1932 union, further consolidating the Baptist presence in the district[11] .

### 4.3 Ryland Knight and Stabilization
The first pastor of the united congregation was **Dr. Ryland Knight**. Knight had previously served as the pastor of Second Baptist Church and was retained to lead the merged body, providing continuity for the older congregation[1] . He served from the merger until 1945, guiding the church through the lean years of the Depression and the tumult of World War II. Under his leadership, the church stabilized its finances and began planning for a permanent sanctuary that would befit its growing stature[1] .

## 5\. Architectural History: The Cathedral of Buckhead
The physical campus of Second-Ponce de Leon Baptist Church is a testament to the architectural ambitions of the mid-20th century South. The church is frequently cited as a prime example of Georgian Colonial and Neocolonial religious architecture.

### 5.1 The 1937 Sanctuary
While the congregation initially occupied the 1930 structure built by Ponce de Leon Baptist, the rapid growth of the merged body necessitated a grander facility. The current sanctuary was dedicated on **October 10, 1937**.1

**Architect:** The building was designed by the renowned architect **George Harwell Bond**.1 Bond’s design drew inspiration from the colonial meeting houses of New England and the Anglican traditions of the American colonies, styles that projected stability, history, and civic order—values highly prized during the uncertainty of the 1930s.

**Design Characteristics:**

* **Style:** Georgian Colonial / Neocolonial[1] .  
* **Exterior:** The facade is dominated by a monumental portico supported by classical columns, approached by a wide flight of stairs. The use of red brick with white wood trim connects the building aesthetically to the residential architecture of the surrounding Buckhead neighborhoods[14] .  
* **The Spire:** The church features a towering steeple, a hallmark of the colonial style, which serves as a visual landmark on Peachtree Road[15] .  
* **Interior:** The sanctuary interior is designed to center on the pulpit and the choir, reflecting the Baptist emphasis on the preached Word and congregational song, yet the layout includes a nave and transepts, borrowing from more liturgical traditions.

**WPA Survey Data (1941):** A survey conducted by the Works Progress Administration (WPA) in 1941 provides a snapshot of the church shortly after the sanctuary's completion. At that time, the church property was valued at **$425,000** (equivalent to approximately $9.09 million in 2024 currency). The survey recorded a membership of 2,060 and a Sunday School enrollment of 1,607, indicating the church's rapid ascent to prominence[1] .

### 5.2 Renovations and Campus Expansion
Over the decades, the campus has expanded to meet the needs of a modern ministry. A significant master plan was executed by the architectural firm **TurnerBatson**, which included three phases of renovation and construction[16] .

* **Phase One:** Involved the renovation of the traditional four-story sanctuary building. This project modernized the worship center, the music suite, classrooms, and the youth department, ensuring the historic structure could support contemporary programming[16] .  
* **Phase Two:** Included the construction of a new addition designed to serve as a central entry point for the **Family Life Center** and the **Children’s Education Building**. This addition filled the gap between existing structures, creating a large lobby, check-in areas, and indoor play spaces, thereby improving security and circulation[16] .  
* **Phase Three:** Focused on the complete renovation of the separate four-story, 50,000-square-foot chapel building. This facility houses the chapel, a fellowship hall, a commercial kitchen, and administrative offices[16] .

## 6\. The Instrument of Worship: The Moller Pipe Organ
Central to the liturgical life of Second-Ponce de Leon is its massive pipe organ, an instrument of significant historical and musical value.

### 6.1 Origins and Installation
The organ, identified as **Opus 9185**, was built by the **M.P. Moller Pipe Organ Company** of Hagerstown, Maryland[17] . Installed in **1959**, it dates from the period when Moller was the largest pipe organ builder in the world[17] . The instrument was designed to fill the cavernous acoustic of the 1937 sanctuary and support the church's strong tradition of choral music.

### 6.2 Specifications and Divisions
The organ is a comprehensive instrument comprising several divisions, allowing for a wide range of dynamic and tonal expression. According to the Atlanta Chapter of the American Guild of Organists (AGO), the specifications are as follows 18:

| Division | Primary Stops/Ranks | Characteristics |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **I \- Choir** | Erzähler (16'), Holzgedeckt (8'), Koppelflöte (4') | An expressive division often used for accompaniment. Contains digital augmentations like Harp and Celesta. |
| **II \- Great** | Quintaton (16'), Principal (8'), Bourdon (8') | The backbone of the organ, providing the "Principal" chorus for congregational singing. Features mixtures (Fourniture IV, Cymbale III). |
| **III \- Swell** | Rohrgedeckt (16'), Viola Pomposa (8'), Trompette (8') | An expressive division housed in a box with shutters, containing solo reeds (Hautbois, Basson) and strings. |
| **IV \- Bombarde** | Trompette Harmonique (8'), Mounted Cornet | An unenclosed division designed for powerful solo lines and fanfare, including the "Festival Trumpet." |
| **Pedal** | Contra Principal (32'), Violone (16'), Bombarde (16') | Provides the deep bass foundation. Includes a massive 32' digital Contre Bombarde. |

### 6.3 Renovations and Digital Expansion
In recent years, the organ has been updated to ensure its reliability and expand its capabilities. These updates include the integration of digital voices (e.g., digital 32' stops in the Pedal division) to supplement the wind-blown pipes, a common practice in maintaining vintage instruments[18] . The church's music ministry, led by Minister of Music **Bill Gabbard** and Organist **Jack Mitchener**, continues to utilize this instrument as the centerpiece of Sunday worship and special events like the annual Christmas Cantata[19] .

## 7\. The Swilley Era: Education, Civil Rights, and Growth (1945–1969)
The post-World War II era marked the ascension of Second-Ponce de Leon to the pinnacle of Atlanta’s religious hierarchy, driven largely by the leadership of **Dr. Monroe F. Swilley Jr.**

### 7.1 Monroe Swilley: The Pastor-Educator
Monroe Swilley assumed the pastorate in 1945, succeeding Ryland Knight[1] . A native of El Dorado, Arkansas, Swilley was a figure of immense intellect and ambition. His tenure was defined by a dual commitment to evangelism and higher education.

Swilley was the primary architect of **Atlanta Baptist College**, an institution founded to provide Baptist higher education in the city. He advocated tirelessly for the college during its planning stages, and Second-Ponce de Leon Baptist Church served as a major financial patron for the new school[22] . In 1969, Swilley resigned his position as senior pastor to become the first president of Atlanta Baptist College[1] .

Under his guidance, the college merged with Mercer University in 1972, becoming **Mercer University Atlanta**. Swilley served as the vice president of the unified institution until his retirement in 1989[1] . His legacy is permanently enshrined on the campus, where the **Swilley Library** bears his name[22] . This connection established a lasting pipeline between the church and the university; numerous Mercer faculty, staff, and alumni—including later pastoral staff like Dr. Dock Hollingsworth and Rev. Jonathan Hall—have maintained deep ties to SPdL[22] .

### 7.2 The Challenge of Civil Rights
Like many prominent Southern congregations, Second-Ponce de Leon faced the moral and social reckoning of the Civil Rights Movement. While Atlanta marketed itself as "The City Too Busy to Hate," the reality within its white establishment churches was often one of resistance or slow adaptation.

In **1961**, the church's Board of Deacons instituted a racially segregated seating policy. This policy mandated that any African American visitors to the church were to be seated in a designated, roped-off area in the rear of the sanctuary[1] . This action reflected the prevailing tensions of the era, where the preservation of social order often took precedence over integration. While Swilley was considered a moderate denominational statesman, the church’s institutional stance during the height of the movement was conservative regarding racial integration. This history serves as a critical backdrop to the church's later efforts toward broader inclusivity and community service.

## 8\. The Dilday Era and the SBC Controversy (1969–1979)
Following Swilley’s departure for academia, the church called **Dr. Russell H. Dilday** as senior pastor. Dilday’s tenure would bridge the gap between the traditional era and the looming theological wars of the Southern Baptist Convention.

### 8.1 Russell Dilday: A Baptist Statesman
Russell Dilday served as pastor from 1969 to 1978\. Second-Ponce de Leon was his only pastorate outside the state of Texas, highlighting the church's national profile[23] . Dilday was known for his scholarly preaching and his focus on congregational unity.

In **1976**, Dilday delivered a prescient sermon to the SPdL congregation, warning against the dangers of theological factionalism. He urged the church not to waste its energy on "differences in theological positions" but to focus on "unity and usefulness"[24] . This sermon anticipated the "Conservative Resurgence" (or "Fundamentalist Takeover") that would erupt in the SBC beginning in 1979\.

### 8.2 The Conservative Resurgence
Dilday left Second-Ponce in 1978 to become the president of **Southwestern Baptist Theological Seminary** in Fort Worth, Texas[25] . There, he became a central figure in the resistance against the fundamentalist shift within the convention. His tenure at the seminary ended in 1994 when he was fired by a board of trustees dominated by the new conservative faction—a seminal event in modern Baptist history[25] .

Because of Dilday’s influence, Second-Ponce de Leon remained a stronghold for moderate Baptist theology. Even after his departure, the church continued to identify with the principles of local church autonomy and priesthood of the believer, resisting the increasingly authoritarian trends of the national denomination[24] .

## 9\. The Political Cathedral: "The Governors' Church"
During the mid-to-late 20th century, Second-Ponce de Leon Baptist Church acquired a reputation as the "church of governors" and the spiritual home of Atlanta's business and political elite. This membership profile granted the church immense informal power within the state.

### 9.1 Notable Members
* Carl Sanders (Governor 1963–1967):  
  Sanders, a progressive Democrat known for modernizing Georgia's government and infrastructure, was a devoted member of Second-Ponce. He taught Sunday School and was active in church leadership[5] . His memorial service was held at the church in November 2014, attended by six living governors (Jimmy Carter, Joe Frank Harris, Zell Miller, Roy Barnes, Sonny Perdue, and Nathan Deal), underscoring the church's centrality to Georgia's political establishment[5] .  
* George Busbee (Governor 1975–1983):  
  Busbee, who served two terms as governor and was known for his "workhorse" style, was also a member of Second-Ponce de Leon during his time in Atlanta[6] . His memorial service was conducted at the church following his death[28] .  
* Griffin Bell (U.S. Attorney General):  
  Griffin Bell, a federal judge and the U.S. Attorney General under President Jimmy Carter, was a long-time deacon at Second-Ponce[29] . Bell was a legendary figure in legal circles, known for his integrity and wit. His memorial service in January 2009 brought national dignitaries to the sanctuary, with eulogies reflecting on his role in restoring confidence in the Justice Department post-Watergate[7] .  
* J. Mack Robinson (Business Titan):  
  J. Mack Robinson, one of Atlanta’s most successful entrepreneurs (insurance, media, banking, and fashion) and the namesake of the Georgia State University College of Business, was a member[32] . Robinson was also a prominent racehorse owner. His funeral was held at SPdL in February 2014, celebrating his philanthropy and his "gentle" demeanor despite his immense wealth[33] .

### 9.2 Institutional Significance
The concentration of such high-profile figures meant that Second-Ponce functioned as a venue where the sacred and the secular frequently intersected. The hallways of the church were places where state policy and business deals were often discussed informally, cementing its status as a pillar of the "New South" establishment.

## 10\. Theological Identity and Denominational Affiliation (1990s–2020s)
As the Southern Baptist Convention solidified its conservative shift in the 1990s, Second-Ponce de Leon Baptist Church navigated a complex theological path.

### 10.1 Dual Affiliation
In 1991, disenfranchised moderate Baptists formed the **Cooperative Baptist Fellowship (CBF)**. Second-Ponce de Leon, reflecting its moderate heritage (and the legacy of pastors like Dilday), adopted a policy of **dual affiliation**.1 This allowed the church to remain part of the SBC (and the Georgia Baptist Convention) while also partnering with the CBF. This "middle way" allowed the church to accommodate a diverse membership that included both traditional conservatives and progressive moderates.

### 10.2 The Atlanta Baptist Association Controversy
In the early 2000s, the **Atlanta Baptist Association (ABA)** became a battleground over the issue of homosexuality. Two member churches, Oakhurst Baptist and Virginia-Highland Baptist, were targeted for expulsion due to their affirming stance on LGBTQ+ membership and leadership[35] .

During the ABA meetings, delegates from Second-Ponce de Leon, such as Gray Lambert, expressed strong opposition to the affirming churches, with Lambert stating, "Whoever would have thought that we would be associated with homosexuals?"[35] . This indicates that while SPdL was moderate regarding biblical interpretation and women in ministry, the lay leadership at the time remained conservative on issues of sexuality. Second-Ponce voted to distance itself from the affirming churches, preserving its standing within the ABA while other congregations were expelled or withdrew[35] .

### 10.3 Current Stance
Today, the church is generally described as "welcoming," emphasizing hospitality to all, though it stops short of the fully "affirming" label used by progressive activists. Its affiliation remains listed with both the SBC and CBF, a rarity in an increasingly polarized religious landscape[1] .

## 11\. Modern Ministries and Community Engagement
In the 21st century, Second-Ponce de Leon has diversified its ministry portfolio, moving beyond traditional Sunday services to engage the community through health, education, and the arts.

### 11.1 The Community Wellness Center (CWC)
The church operates the **Community Wellness Center**, a comprehensive fitness facility located on the campus[37] . Far from a simple church basement gym, the CWC is a professional-grade facility open to the public (membership required).

* **Amenities:** It features a cardio center with Precor equipment, aerobics studios, and—most notably in the 2020s—three indoor wooden **pickleball courts**.38  
* **Mission:** The CWC aims to promote "complete fitness" of body, mind, and spirit, serving as a "third place" for Buckhead residents to gather and interact with the church community in a non-religious setting[10] .

### 11.2 The Christ the King Preschool Partnership
One of the most innovative developments in the church’s recent history is its ecumenical partnership with the **Cathedral of Christ the King**, the Roman Catholic cathedral located directly across Peachtree Road.

* **Origins:** The partnership was forged through the friendship of SPdL Senior Pastor **Dr. Dock Hollingsworth** and Cathedral Rector **Monsignor Francis McNamee**.9  
* **Establishment:** In August 2022, the **Christ the King Preschool at Second-Ponce** opened its doors[9] .  
* **Operation:** The preschool is a Catholic institution operating within the facilities of the Baptist church. This arrangement maximizes the use of SPdL’s education space while alleviating the overcrowding at the Cathedral. It serves children from 12 months to Pre-K[40] .  
* **Significance:** This collaboration is highly unusual in the American South, where Baptist and Catholic relations have historically been distant. It stands as a testament to the church’s pragmatic and ecumenical spirit in the modern era.

### 11.3 Music and Worship
The music ministry remains a cornerstone of the church’s identity.

* **Leadership:** Minister of Music **Bill Gabbard** leads the program, which includes a chancel choir and a church orchestra[19] .  
* **Style:** The worship style is traditional and liturgical, featuring hymns, choral anthems, and organ masterworks. This distinguishes SPdL from the contemporary, rock-band-driven worship styles of many modern evangelical churches.  
* **Events:** The annual **Christmas Cantata** is a major cultural event in Atlanta, drawing large crowds for a performance of sacred classical music followed by a community reception[21] .

### 11.4 Children and Youth (The Kids Club)
The church invests heavily in the next generation through **The Kids Club** and the **Circle Up** family ministry[42] .

* **Curriculum:** The ministry focuses on three pillars: "Curious" (learning), "Compassionate" (service), and "Cozy" (safety)[42] .  
* **Leadership:** Rev. Allie Osborne (Minister of Missions & Children's Discipleship) and Rev. Jonathan Hall (Minister of Community & Student Discipleship) oversee these programs, both bringing training from the McAfee School of Theology[19] .

## 12\. Current Status and Leadership (2024–2025)
As of December 17, 2025, Second-Ponce de Leon Baptist Church is navigating a season of transition.

### 12.1 Interim Leadership
Following the tenure of Dr. Dock Hollingsworth, the church entered a search process for a new senior pastor. Currently, **Rev. Ronnie Brewer** serves as the **Interim Senior Pastor**.8

* **Preaching:** Rev. Brewer has led the congregation through sermon series such as "Virtue" (exploring wisdom and moral formation) and "Dark Corners" (addressing sins like anger)[8] . His leadership focuses on maintaining spiritual vitality and stability during the search for a permanent successor.

### 12.2 Staffing
The 2025 staff roster reflects a blend of experienced ministers and younger leaders:

* **Rev. Jonathan Hall:** Minister of Community & Student Discipleship[20] .  
* **Rev. Allie Osborne:** Minister of Missions & Children's Discipleship[20] .  
* **Bill Gabbard:** Minister of Music[20] .  
* **Jack Mitchener:** Organist[20] .  
* **Tiffany Baumstark:** Director of Events[20] .

### 12.3 Demographics and Outlook
While membership numbers have stabilized from their peak in the late 1990s (approx. 3,600 members), the church maintains a robust core of affluent, educated, and civically engaged members[1] . The active youth programs and the busy Community Wellness Center suggest a strategy of engaging young families in the Buckhead area to ensure the institution's longevity.

## 13\. Conclusion
Second-Ponce de Leon Baptist Church stands as a unique institution in the landscape of American Christianity. Born from the ashes of the Civil War and the Great Depression, it rose to become a "Cathedral of Buckhead," hosting the state's most powerful leaders and shaping the educational and theological trajectory of the region.

Its architecture speaks to a desire for permanence and order; its organ and music ministry speak to a commitment to high culture; and its dual affiliation speaks to a theological nuance that refuses to be categorized by the polarities of the culture wars. By partnering with a Catholic cathedral and opening its doors to the community through wellness and recreation, Second-Ponce demonstrates a capacity for adaptation that belies its traditional exterior. As it moves through the mid-2020s, it remains a vital witness to a thoughtful, historic, and civic-minded expression of the Baptist faith.
