# 2025 Pennsylvania Supreme Court election
## 1\. Introduction
The **2025 Pennsylvania Supreme Court election** was a statewide judicial retention election held on Tuesday, November 4, 2025, in the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania[1] . The election determined the tenure of three incumbent justices of the Supreme Court of Pennsylvania: Justice Christine Donohue, Justice Kevin Dougherty, and Justice David Wecht. All three jurists, who were originally elected in a historic Democratic sweep in 2015, sought retention for a second ten-year term[1] .

This election cycle was notable for the unusually high level of politicization, campaign spending, and strategic attention directed toward what is typically a low-profile judicial retention process[3] . While retention elections in Pennsylvania are nonpartisan in format—voters are presented with a simple "Yes" or "No" question regarding each justice—the 2025 contest functioned effectively as a partisan proxy war[1] . The retention of the three justices was critical for the Democratic Party to maintain its 5–2 majority on the state's highest court, a dominance established during the 2015 election[1] . Conversely, Republican-aligned interest groups and conservative donors viewed the election as a rare opportunity to alter the ideological trajectory of the court, which had issued pivotal rulings on congressional redistricting, voting rights, and reproductive healthcare in the preceding decade[1] .

The campaign was characterized by unprecedented financial expenditures, estimated to exceed $15 million, driven largely by independent expenditure groups and Political Action Committees (PACs)[1] . Pro-retention forces, organizing under banners such as "Pennsylvanians for Judicial Fairness," framed the election as a referendum on the independence of the judiciary and the protection of civil liberties, specifically abortion access following the federal overturning of *Roe v. Wade*.5 Opposition groups, including the "Commonwealth Leaders Fund" and the "Republican State Leadership Committee," mounted a "Vote No" campaign that criticized the justices as "activist judges" responsible for controversial decisions regarding the COVID-19 pandemic and election administration[4] .

On election night, all three justices were retained by decisive margins. Official results certified by the Secretary of the Commonwealth indicated that each justice received approximately 61% to 62% of the affirmative vote[2] . The results were uniform across the three incumbents, suggesting that the electorate largely viewed them as a unified slate[10] . The successful retention ensured the continuation of the Democratic majority on the court until at least 2027, when Justice Donohue is scheduled to retire due to the state's mandatory judicial retirement age[11] .

The election also served as a significant bellwether for the shifting political geography of Pennsylvania. Democratic incumbents performed strongly in traditional swing counties such as Erie and Northampton, outperforming the top-of-ticket metrics from the previous year's presidential election[10] . This divergence highlighted the distinct dynamics of judicial retention elections, where the power of incumbency and the specific framing of judicial independence can often transcend the stark partisan polarization seen in executive and legislative races[3] .

## 2\. Background
### 2.1. The Supreme Court of Pennsylvania
The Supreme Court of Pennsylvania is the court of last resort for the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania. It claims the distinction of being the oldest appellate court in the United States, predating the United States Supreme Court by several decades[3] . The court consists of seven justices who are elected to ten-year terms. The court exercises plenary jurisdiction over the state's legal system and has the final authority on matters of Pennsylvania law and the state constitution[3] .

Because the court has the power to interpret the state constitution, its composition is of immense political consequence. In the years leading up to the 2025 election, the court played a central role in resolving disputes between the Democratic executive branch (under Governors Tom Wolf and Josh Shapiro) and the Republican-controlled General Assembly[1] . These disputes frequently involved high-stakes issues such as the drawing of congressional and legislative district maps, the legality of mail-in voting procedures, and the scope of the governor's emergency powers during the COVID-19 pandemic[1] .

### 2.2. Judicial Selection and Retention
Pennsylvania utilizes a hybrid system for judicial selection that combines partisan elections with merit retention.

* **Initial Election:** Justices are initially selected in partisan statewide elections. Candidates run in party primaries (Democratic and Republican) and then face off in a general election. The winners serve an initial ten-year term[3] .  
* **Retention Election:** At the conclusion of their ten-year term, justices do not face a challenger. Instead, they stand for "retention." Their name appears on the general election ballot with a nonpartisan question: "Shall \[Justice Name\] be retained for an additional term as Justice of the Supreme Court of the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania?" Voters must choose "Yes" or "No"[1] .

If a justice receives more than 50% "Yes" votes, they are retained for another ten-year term. If they receive more "No" votes than "Yes" votes, their seat is declared vacant. The Governor must then appoint a replacement, subject to confirmation by two-thirds of the State Senate, to serve until the next municipal election, at which point a partisan election is held to fill the seat permanently[2] .

Historically, retention elections in Pennsylvania have been low-maintenance affairs for incumbents. Since the modern retention system was established by the Constitutional Convention of 1968, only one Supreme Court justice has ever lost a retention vote: Justice Russell Nigro in 2005[13] . Nigro's defeat was the result of a unique public backlash against a legislative pay raise that the court had allowed to stand, an event that galvanized a bipartisan "Vote No" movement[4] . Apart from that singular event, retention rates for appellate judges in Pennsylvania hover near 98%, with most justices securing retention with little campaign activity[13] .

However, the political climate of the 2020s introduced new volatility to this system. The increasing nationalization of state supreme court races, driven by the U.S. Supreme Court's devolution of major policy questions (such as abortion and gerrymandering) to the state level, transformed retention elections into potential battlegrounds[1] .

### 2.3. The 2015 "Democratic Sweep"
The political stakes of the 2025 election were directly rooted in the outcome of the 2015 Pennsylvania Supreme Court election. In 2015, due to a series of resignations and retirements, three seats on the seven-member court were open simultaneously—an unprecedented occurrence in modern state history[15] . The Democratic Party nominated three candidates: Superior Court Judges Christine Donohue and David Wecht, and Philadelphia Court of Common Pleas Judge Kevin Dougherty.

The Democrats won all three seats in a "sweep," flipping the partisan control of the court from Republican to Democratic[15] . This victory established the 5–2 Democratic majority that would define the court's jurisprudence for the next decade. The 2025 retention election was the first time these three justices faced voters since that pivotal victory. For Republicans, 2025 represented the first major opportunity to chip away at the majority that had been established ten years prior[3] .

## 3\. The Incumbents
The three justices seeking retention in 2025—Christine Donohue, Kevin Dougherty, and David Wecht—shared a party background but possessed distinct judicial records and personal narratives.

### 3.1. Justice Christine Donohue
**Christine Donohue** served on the Superior Court of Pennsylvania for eight years prior to her election to the Supreme Court in 2015[16] . A native of Pennsylvania with a background in private practice, she was often viewed as a pragmatic voice on the bench.

* **Judicial Tenure:** During her first term, Donohue authored several significant opinions and was part of the majority in key civil rights cases. Her supporters highlighted her role in protecting reproductive rights; although the court had not yet issued a definitive ruling on the state constitution's protection of abortion rights by 2025, Donohue was featured in advertisements linking her retention to the defense of such rights[1] .  
* **Evaluation:** The Pennsylvania Bar Association (PBA) Judicial Evaluation Commission recommended her for retention, describing her as "thorough and well-prepared for oral arguments," "smart and fair," and "attentive and engaged"[11] .  
* **Term Limitations:** A unique factor in Donohue's retention was her age. Under Pennsylvania law, judges must retire at the end of the calendar year in which they turn 75\. Donohue was expected to reach this mandatory retirement age in 2027, meaning that her retention vote in 2025 was effectively for a two-year extension rather than a full decade. This fact was noted by political observers but did not become a central theme of the opposition campaign[12] .

### 3.2. Justice Kevin Dougherty
**Kevin M. Dougherty** brought a different profile to the high court. Before 2015, he was the Administrative Judge of the Family Division of the Philadelphia Court of Common Pleas[16] . He was widely known in state politics not only for his judicial service but also for his family; he is the brother of John "Johnny Doc" Dougherty, a prominent and powerful Philadelphia labor leader who was convicted on federal corruption charges in the years preceding the 2025 election[11] .

* **Separation from Family Controversy:** Despite his brother's legal troubles, Justice Dougherty successfully maintained a distinct professional identity. He was not implicated in his brother's activities, and he continued to receive broad support from the legal community and labor unions[11] . His son, Sean Dougherty, also served as a member of the Pennsylvania House of Representatives, further cementing the family's political footprint[11] .  
* **Judicial Style:** The PBA recommended Dougherty for retention, noting that attorneys described him as "attentive, smart, well prepared" and "open to consideration of differing perspectives"[11] . His background in family court often informed his perspective on cases involving juvenile justice and social services.  
* **Campaign Performance:** Dougherty was a robust fundraiser, raising over $2.6 million for his retention committee—significantly more than his colleagues[2] .

### 3.3. Justice David Wecht
**David N. Wecht** served on the Pennsylvania Superior Court before joining the Supreme Court. He is the son of the famous forensic pathologist Cyril Wecht, a connection that gave him high name recognition in Western Pennsylvania[11] .

* **Jurisprudence:** Wecht is known for his scholarly, lengthy, and often forceful opinions. He frequently writes concurrences or dissents that explore the historical and theoretical underpinnings of the law[16] . One of his most notable contributions was in the realm of education funding; he authored a majority opinion asserting that petitioners had the right to challenge the state's education funding formula under the constitution, a ruling that paved the way for the landmark *William Penn* decision which declared the state's school funding system unconstitutional[11] .  
* **Target of Opposition:** Because of his outspoken nature and his authorship of key opinions regarding election administration (such as the extension of mail-in ballot deadlines in 2020), Wecht was a primary target for conservative opposition groups. The "Vote No" campaigns frequently featured his image and cited his rulings as examples of "judicial overreach"[1] .  
* **Evaluation:** Despite the partisan attacks, the PBA highly recommended him, stating his opinions were "well written and logical" and his judicial temperament "above reproach"[11] .

## 4\. Campaign Dynamics and Issues
The 2025 retention election transformed into a high-stakes political battleground, deviating from the norm of quiet judicial confirmations. The campaign environment was shaped by the broader national polarization surrounding the judiciary and specific grievances regarding Pennsylvania state policy.

### 4.1. The "Politicization" of Retention
Observers noted that the 2025 cycle was one of the most politicized retention elections in memory[1] . While the ballot itself remained nonpartisan, the external campaign environment was deeply partisan. The structural reality of the court—a 5-2 Democratic majority—meant that the retention election was the only mechanism available to Republicans to disrupt Democratic control before the next scheduled open-seat election[3] .

### 4.2. The "Vote No" Campaign (Opposition)
Conservative groups and the Republican Party infrastructure coalesced around a "Vote No" message. This campaign was driven by several key themes:

* **"Activist Judges":** The core argument was that Donohue, Dougherty, and Wecht had acted as "super-legislators" rather than impartial jurists. Opponents cited the court's 2018 decision to strike down the GOP-drawn congressional map as an unconstitutional gerrymander, arguing that the court had usurped the legislature's role[1] .  
* **COVID-19 Policies:** The opposition highlighted the court's rulings that upheld Governor Tom Wolf's emergency powers during the pandemic, including business closures and mask mandates. These decisions were framed as "lockdowns" that harmed the state's economy and personal freedoms[3] .  
* **Election Administration:** The court's decisions during the 2020 election, particularly those regarding mail-in ballots and drop boxes, were characterized by Republicans as creating "election chaos" and undermining election integrity[4] .  
* **Soft on Crime:** Some opposition ads attempted to portray the justices as lenient on criminal defendants, a standard tactic in judicial elections, although this was secondary to the structural arguments about power and overreach[4] .

The **Republican State Leadership Committee (RSLC)** and the **Commonwealth Leaders Fund** (associated with conservative mega-donor Jeff Yass) were the primary financial engines behind this message[4] . They utilized digital advertising, direct mail, and text messaging to urge a "No" vote[4] .

### 4.3. The "Vote Yes" Campaign (Pro-Retention)
The incumbents and their allies countered with a campaign focused on judicial independence and rights protection.

* **Reproductive Rights:** In the wake of the U.S. Supreme Court's *Dobbs* decision, Democrats heavily emphasized the state court's role as the final protector of abortion access in Pennsylvania. The "Vote Yes" campaign framed the retention of the three Democratic justices as essential to preventing an abortion ban[1] . TV ads explicitly stated, "We protected access to abortion... even when the powerful came after it"[18] .  
* **Democracy and Voting Rights:** Supporters argued that the court had protected the right to vote against partisan attempts to disenfranchise Pennsylvanians. They framed the court's redistricting and mail-in ballot rulings as victories for fairness and democratic participation[1] .  
* **Labor Support:** Organized labor was a massive pillar of the "Vote Yes" coalition. The **Pennsylvania AFL-CIO**, **Philadelphia Federation of Teachers**, and **SEIU** mobilized their memberships, endorsing the justices as defenders of workers' rights, collective bargaining, and fair wages[19] .  
* **Judicial Integrity:** The campaign also leveraged the "Recommended" ratings from the Bar Association to argue that the opposition was politically motivated and that the justices were highly qualified professionals doing their jobs[16] .

## 5\. Campaign Finance
The 2025 retention election set new records for spending in a Pennsylvania judicial retention contest. Total spending was estimated to exceed **$15 million**, a staggering sum for a race with no named opponents[1] .

### 5.1. Spending Disparity
Democratic and pro-retention groups significantly outspent their conservative counterparts, with estimates suggesting a **4-to-1** advantage for the "Vote Yes" side[1] . This financial dominance allowed the incumbents to define the narrative early and maintain a constant presence on television and digital platforms throughout the fall.

### 5.2. Independent Expenditure Groups
The majority of the spending did not flow through the candidates' own campaign committees but through independent Political Action Committees (PACs).

* **Pennsylvanians for Judicial Fairness (PJF):** This PAC was the primary vehicle for pro-retention spending. Funded heavily by trial lawyers (via the "Committee for a Better Tomorrow") and labor unions (such as the Carpenter's Union and various teacher's unions), PJF spent over **$3.4 million** on ads supporting the justices[17] . Their ads were slickly produced and focused on the themes of abortion rights and independence[6] .  
* **Vote Yes For Fair And Independent Courts:** Another major pro-retention group, spending approximately **$3.58 million**.17  
* **Commonwealth Leaders Fund:** On the opposition side, this PAC, largely funded by billionaire Jeff Yass, spent heavily to attack the justices. While direct spending on retention was reported around **$356,000** in some filings, the broader network's investment in the "Vote No" narrative was substantial[21] .  
* **Citizens for Term Limits:** A conservative group that spent approximately **$1.4 million** opposing the justices[17] .

### 5.3. Candidate Fundraising
While independent groups dominated the airwaves, the justices also raised funds for their own retention committees.

* **Kevin Dougherty:** Was the most prolific fundraiser among the justices, raising over **$2.6 million** and spending nearly the same amount[2] .  
* **Christine Donohue:** Raised approximately **$891,000** and spent roughly **$820,000**.2  
* **David Wecht:** Reported significantly lower direct spending (approx. **$58,000** in some reports), likely relying on the coordinated spend from the other justices and outside groups to carry his message[17] .

The sheer volume of money poured into the race drew criticism from good-government groups like the **Brennan Center for Justice**, which noted that the influx of outside money threatened to erode public confidence in the impartiality of the courts[22] .

## 6\. Election Results
On November 4, 2025, Pennsylvania voters delivered a decisive verdict, voting to retain all three justices by wide margins. The Associated Press called the races shortly before midnight, confirming that the Democratic majority on the court would remain intact[8] .

### 6.1. Statewide Totals
The official results certified by the Department of State showed a remarkable consistency in the vote totals for all three justices.

| **Christine Donohue** | Democrat | 2,231,527 | **61.80%** | 1,379,599 | 38.20% | **Retained** |
| **Kevin M. Dougherty** | Democrat | 2,227,880 | **61.79%** | 1,377,687 | 38.21% | **Retained** |

The tight clustering of the results—with Donohue and Dougherty separated by fewer than 4,000 votes out of 3.6 million cast—indicated that the electorate treated the retention questions as a slate. Voters largely voted "Yes" to all three or "No" to all three, with very little ticket-splitting or individual differentiation[2] .

### 6.2. Voter Turnout
Turnout for the election was approximately **42.45%** of registered voters, a significant increase from the 36.77% turnout observed in the 2023 municipal election[9] . This boost in participation was attributed to the high visibility of the Supreme Court race, as well as competitive local contests in various jurisdictions[9] . Secretary of the Commonwealth Al Schmidt praised the administration of the election, noting that audits confirmed the accuracy of the results[9] .

## 7\. Geographic and Demographic Analysis
A granular analysis of the results reveals the evolving political coalition that supported the court's retention. The "Vote Yes" coalition successfully combined the Democratic base in urban centers with a crucial slice of suburban and swing-county voters who may have been alienated by the "Vote No" campaign's perceived extremism or the attack on judicial independence.

### 7.1. Key Swing Counties
The performance of the justices in Pennsylvania's traditional "bellwether" counties was a critical factor in their comfortable victory.

* **Erie County:** Often cited as the state's most important swing county, Erie voted decisively for retention. Justice Donohue received **62.24%** of the vote (45,160 votes), slightly outperforming her statewide average[24] . This result was significant because Erie had been a highly contested battleground in the previous year's presidential election. The strong retention vote suggested that the Democratic judicial brand remained resilient in working-class areas[10] .  
* **Northampton County:** In the Lehigh Valley, another pivotal swing region, the justices also secured robust victories. Justice Donohue received **54,111 "Yes" votes** versus 32,643 "No" votes (approx. 62%)[25] . This mirrored the success of local Democratic candidates in the county, indicating a strong localized turnout operation or a regional shift toward the Democratic coalition in 2025[10] .

### 7.2. The Urban-Rural Divide
The election results underscored the stark polarization between Pennsylvania's metropolitan areas and its rural interior, a dynamic often referred to as the "T" (the central and northern rural regions).

* **Support Base:** The "Yes" vote was overwhelming in Philadelphia and Allegheny County (Pittsburgh), the state's two largest population centers. It also carried the influential "collar counties" around Philadelphia (Montgomery, Delaware, Chester, and Bucks), where the message of abortion rights and democracy likely resonated with suburban voters[3] .  
* **Opposition Strongholds:** Conversely, the "No" vote dominated in the rural counties.  
  * **Armstrong County:** Voted **64% No** to 36% Yes[26] .  
  * **Bedford County:** Voted **70% No** to 30% Yes[26] .  
  * **Adams County:** Was more competitive but still leaned against retention, with the "No" vote hovering around **51-52%**.26  
* **The "Blue Wall" in the West:** Interestingly, **Beaver County**, a working-class county in Western Pennsylvania that has trended Republican in recent presidential cycles, voted to *retain* the justices with **53-54%** of the vote[26] . This deviation from the presidential trendline suggests that labor union endorsements (strong in Beaver County) may have played a decisive role in peeling off working-class voters from the GOP's "Vote No" coalition.

### 7.3. Comparison to Top-of-Ticket Trends
Analysts noted that the justices performed better in several areas than the 2024 Democratic presidential nominee, Kamala Harris, had performed the previous year[10] . For instance, in Northampton County, the margin for the justices exceeded the presidential margin, suggesting that the "nonpartisan" nature of the retention ballot, combined with the specific issues of judicial independence and labor rights, allowed the justices to cultivate a broader coalition than a typical partisan candidate[10] .

## 8\. Implications
The retention of Justices Donohue, Dougherty, and Wecht had profound legal and political consequences for the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania.

### 8.1. Preservation of the Democratic Majority
The most immediate effect was the preservation of the **5–2 Democratic majority** on the Supreme Court. Had any of the justices been defeated, the governor would have been forced to make a temporary appointment, likely leading to a high-stakes confirmation battle in the State Senate and a precarious balance of power on the court[1] . By securing another ten-year term for Dougherty and Wecht (and a two-year extension for Donohue), the Democratic Party ensured that the court would remain a friendly venue for its policy priorities through the end of the decade[3] .

### 8.2. Future Jurisprudence
With the majority secured, the court was expected to continue its trajectory on key issues:

* **Abortion:** The court is anticipated to rule on the challenge to the state's ban on Medicaid funding for abortion, a case highlighted in the retention campaign ads. The retention of the liberal bloc makes a ruling expanding abortion access under the state's Equal Rights Amendment more likely[1] .  
* **Voting Rights:** The court remains the final arbiter on election disputes. With the 2026 gubernatorial and 2028 presidential elections on the horizon, the court's composition ensures that the expansive interpretation of voting access (regarding mail-in ballots and cure procedures) established in 2020 will likely be upheld[1] .  
* **Education Funding:** Following the *William Penn* decision, the court will likely oversee the implementation of the remedy, potentially forcing the legislature to allocate billions in additional funding to poorer school districts—a stance Justice Wecht explicitly supported in his earlier opinions[11] .

### 8.3. Political Aftermath
For the **Pennsylvania Democratic Party** and **Governor Josh Shapiro**, the 2025 election was a significant victory. It demonstrated the party's ability to mobilize its base in an off-year election and to win the messaging war on judicial issues[10] . The results were interpreted as a vote of confidence in the state's Democratic leadership and a rejection of the "culture war" attacks launched by the opposition[3] .

For the **Republican Party**, the failure to unseat even the most controversial justices highlighted the limitations of the "Vote No" strategy. Without a specific scandal to galvinize voter anger (like the 2005 pay raise), displacing an incumbent judge proved to be an insurmountable challenge, even with significant financial investment[1] .

### 8.4. Upcoming Vacancies
Despite the victories, the composition of the court will not remain static. **Justice Christine Donohue** will face mandatory retirement on December 31, 2027, upon reaching the age of 75[12] . This will create an open seat that will be contested in the partisan election of 2027\. Both parties are expected to view the 2027 race as the next critical battlefront in the war for the court[11] .
