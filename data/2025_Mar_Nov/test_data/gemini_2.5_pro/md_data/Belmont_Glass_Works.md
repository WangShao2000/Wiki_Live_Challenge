# The Rise and Fall of the Belmont Glass Works: An Industrial and Artistic History of Bellaire’s Pioneer Glasshouse (1866–1890)
## 1\. Introduction: The Geopolitical and Industrial Crucible of the Upper Ohio Valley
The history of the American glass industry in the latter half of the 19th century is a narrative defined by the interplay of geology, transportation, and skilled migration. While New England and Pittsburgh had established themselves as the antebellum centers of glass production, the post-Civil War era witnessed a dramatic westward shift of industrial capital. At the heart of this transformation was the Upper Ohio Valley, a region that offered a unique convergence of logistical and resource-based advantages. Within this burgeoning industrial corridor, the **Belmont Glass Works** (1866–1890) emerged not merely as a factory, but as a foundational institution that would catalyze the transformation of Bellaire, Ohio, into the renowned "Glass City"[1] .

This report provides an exhaustive analysis of the Belmont Glass Works, tracing its evolution from a cooperative experiment known as **Barnes, Faupel & Co.** to its zenith as a premier manufacturer of Early American Pattern Glass (EAPG). Through a detailed examination of corporate records, patent filings, and material culture, we explore the technological innovations of its mold shop, the artistic merit of its patterns—most notably the "Royal" and "Daisy & Button" lines—and the macroeconomic forces of the "Gas Boom" that ultimately precipitated its dissolution. Furthermore, this study addresses and corrects persistent historical inaccuracies regarding pattern attribution, specifically the conflation of Belmont’s output with the "Racine" moniker, and documents the diaspora of its workforce, whose expertise would go on to seed the glass industries of Northwest Ohio and Indiana[2] .

### 1.1 The Strategic Advantage of Bellaire
To understand the significance of Belmont Glass Works, one must first appreciate the strategic geography of Bellaire. Situated in Belmont County on the western bank of the Ohio River, directly opposite the industrial hub of Wheeling, West Virginia, Bellaire possessed the three critical ingredients for 19th-century heavy industry: fuel, sand, and transport.

1. **Bituminous Coal:** In the era prior to the widespread exploitation of natural gas, coal was the lifeblood of the glass furnace. Bellaire sat atop massive coal seams, allowing factories to mine fuel virtually on-site. This abundance of cheap thermal energy gave Bellaire a distinct cost advantage over eastern competitors who faced higher fuel transport costs[3] .  
2. **The River and the Rail:** The Ohio River had long served as the primary artery for moving goods to the expanding markets of the South and West. However, it was the arrival of the railroads—specifically the Baltimore & Ohio (B\&O) and the Central Ohio Railroad—that integrated Bellaire into the national grid. The convergence of these lines allowed Belmont Glass Works to ship fragile tableware efficiently to growing urban centers like Chicago, St. Louis, and Cincinnati[4] .  
3. **Human Capital:** The proximity to Wheeling, an established glass center, facilitated a cross-river exchange of talent. Skilled gaffers, blowers, and mold makers moved freely between the two cities, creating a dense network of technical expertise. It was from this rich labor pool that Belmont Glass Works would draw its founding cooperators[2] .

### 1.2 The Genesis of an Industry
When Barnes, Faupel & Co. broke ground in 1866, they were not just building a factory; they were inaugurating an era. Belmont Glass Works was the first glasshouse in Bellaire and only the second in the entire county. Its success served as a proof of concept for the region. Following Belmont's establishment, capital flooded into the area, and by 1880, Belmont County had risen to rank sixth in the nation for glass manufacturing, with Ohio ranking fourth. By 1890, the year of Belmont's closure, the state had climbed to second place nationally in production value, a testament to the momentum initiated by this pioneer firm[2] .

**2\. The Formative Years: From Cooperative Roots to Corporate Stability (1866–1870)**

### 2.1 Barnes, Faupel & Co.: The Cooperative Model
The enterprise was organized in August 1865 and commenced production in 1866 under the name **Barnes, Faupel & Co.**.2 The organizational structure of this early firm reflects a broader trend in the post-Civil War glass industry: the cooperative. In a capital-intensive industry where the gap between management and labor was often stark, the cooperative model allowed skilled artisans to pool their resources and labor to become owner-operators.

The founding group was a coalition of businessmen and craftsmen. **Henry Faupel** and **George Barnes** provided the titular leadership, but the roster of incorporators reveals the depth of practical glassmaking experience involved:

* **James S. Gill:** An English immigrant born in 1820, Gill had settled in Wheeling in the 1840s before crossing the river to help organize the Bellaire works. His technical knowledge was instrumental in the plant's design[5] .  
* **Charles Henry Over, John Robinson, and John Crimmel:** These men were listed as "glass-blowers" in early directories, yet they were also incorporators. This dual status of worker-owner ensured that the production floor had a direct voice in corporate governance, a feature that likely contributed to the high quality of the company's early output[2] .

### 2.2 The Trial by Fire: September 1867
The resilience of the fledgling company was tested early. In September 1867, barely a year after production began, the factory was consumed by fire[7] . Fire was the scourge of the 19th-century glass industry. The combination of molten glass (2,400°F), coal-fired furnaces, wooden building frames, and packing materials like straw and hay created a perpetually high-risk environment.

The destruction of the physical plant could have easily bankrupted the young partnership. However, the partners' commitment to the venture was absolute. They mobilized capital and labor to rebuild immediately, and the works were back in operation by the spring of 1868[7] . This rapid recovery demonstrates both the financial solvency of the partners and the strong market demand for their products.

### 2.3 Incorporation and Restructuring (1868)
Following the reconstruction, the partnership was formalized into a corporation. In December 1868, the entity was chartered as the **Belmont Glass Works**.2 The slate of incorporators for this new legal entity included Henry Faupel, Levi Cassell, James S. Gill, Charles Henry Over, John Robinson, John Crimmel, and Thomas J. Fleming.

The leadership hierarchy in 1871 was established as follows:

* **President:** Henry Faupel  
* **Treasurer:** William Gorby  
* **Manager:** Levi Cassell  
* **Directors/Blowers:** Over, Robinson, and Crimmel[2] .

This structure remained relatively stable through the 1870s, providing the continuity necessary for the company to expand its product lines and market share. While the company is frequently referred to as "Belmont Glass Company" in secondary literature and some period advertisements, "Belmont Glass Works" became the dominant trade name, particularly after 1888[8] .


**3\. Production Dynamics: From Chimneys to Crystal**

### 3.1 The Staple Trade: Lamp Chimneys
In its initial phase (1866–1870), the factory’s primary output was **lamp chimneys**.8 In the decades before rural electrification, the kerosene lamp was the standard for domestic lighting. The glass chimney, which protected the flame and induced the draft necessary for a bright, smokeless burn, was a consumable commodity. They were fragile, subject to thermal shock, and required frequent replacement. For a new glasshouse, chimneys provided a steady, high-volume baseline of revenue that allowed the firm to refine its furnace operations and train its workforce.

### 3.2 The Pivot to Pressed Tableware
As the company matured, it pivoted toward higher-margin goods. By 1877, trade publications and company letterheads listed a diversified product mix: "table ware, lamps, \[and\] stationers glass ware"[8] . This transition marked Belmont's entry into the competitive world of **Early American Pattern Glass (EAPG)**.

EAPG was a phenomenon of the industrial revolution. It was "pressed" glass, formed by plunging a metal plunger into a mold filled with molten glass. This process allowed for the mass production of intricate designs that mimicked the look of expensive cut crystal. Belmont Glass Works excelled in this medium, leveraging its proprietary mold-making capabilities to produce patterns of exceptional complexity.

### 3.3 The Role of Chemistry: Manganese and "Sun-Purple" Glass
Belmont, like many of its contemporaries, utilized a specific chemical formula for its clear glass. The sand available in the Ohio Valley often contained iron impurities, which naturally tinted the glass a "coke-bottle" green. To counteract this, glassmakers added **Manganese Dioxide** to the batch. The manganese acted as a decolorizer, neutralizing the green hue and rendering the glass crystal clear.

However, this chemical solution came with a delayed reaction. When glass containing manganese is exposed to ultraviolet (UV) radiation from sunlight over nearly a century, a photochemical reaction occurs. The manganese oxidizes, slowly turning the glass a faint amethyst or purple color. Today, collectors prize this "sun-purple" tint as a hallmark of authenticity, distinguishing original Belmont production from modern reproductions that use selenium or other decolorizers[9] .


**4\. Center of Excellence: The Belmont Mold Shop**

### 4.1 Vertical Integration
One of the most distinguishing features of the Belmont Glass Works was its vertical integration. While many glass factories outsourced the production of their iron molds to independent foundries, Belmont operated its own foundry and mold shop as early as 1870[9] .

The mold is the most critical capital asset in pressed glass production. It determines the shape, the pattern, and the surface finish of the final product. By controlling this process in-house, Belmont could:

1. **Protect Intellectual Property:** New designs could be kept secret until launch.  
2. **Ensure Quality Control:** The fit and finish of the mold parts (the "joint lines") could be maintained to higher standards.  
3. **Innovate Rapidly:** Modifications to patterns (such as adding the "skirt" to the Royal pattern to create Venus) could be executed quickly.

### 4.2 The OEM Strategy
Remarkably, Belmont’s mold shop was so proficient that it functioned as an Original Equipment Manufacturer (OEM) for the broader industry. Historical records confirm that Belmont "chipped" (the term for carving the design into the iron) molds for some of the largest names in American glass, including:

* **McKee & Brothers** (Pittsburgh)  
* **Crystal Glass Company** (Bridgeport, OH)  
* **Central Glass Company** (Wheeling, WV)  
* **Elson Glass Company**  
* **Gillinder Brothers** (Philadelphia)  
* **Fostoria Glass Company**.9

This activity implies that Belmont’s machinists were among the elite of the trade. Producing molds for competitors was a double-edged sword: it generated immediate revenue but also equipped rival firms with the tools to compete. However, it also suggests that Belmont viewed itself as a hub of the Bellaire industrial ecosystem, deeply integrated into the supply chains of its neighbors.


**5\. Artistic Output: A Definitive Catalog of Belmont Patterns**

The artistic legacy of Belmont Glass Works is preserved in its patterns. Unlike the utilitarian chimneys of its early years, these designs were intended to grace the Victorian dining table, serving as affordable luxuries for the American middle class.

### 5.1 The "Royal" and "Venus" Line (c. 1881–1883)
The **"Royal"** pattern (also known as Belmont’s No. 1 or Royal Lady) is perhaps the company's most iconic design. Introduced around 1881, the pattern is characterized by its elegance and distinctive **ball finials** and feet[10] .

* **Design Evolution:** The popularity of the Royal pattern led to a variation introduced in 1883 known as **"Venus"** (No. 2). This iteration retained the core aesthetic but added a "skirted base" and utilized a more conventional finial, likely to differentiate the line or address durability issues with the original ball feet[10] .  
* **Intellectual Property:** The design was significant enough to warrant a patent, filed by **William Fox**.8 This legal protection underscores the commercial value attached to these proprietary designs.

### 5.2 Pattern No. 100: "Daisy & Button" (1886)
While the "Daisy & Button" motif was a generic industry standard produced by dozens of factories, Belmont’s interpretation (Pattern No. 100\) is widely regarded by collectors as one of the most ornate and technically ambitious versions ever produced[9] .

The Technical Marvel of the Covered Compote:  
The covered compote in the Daisy & Button line serves as a masterclass in 19th-century mold engineering. The piece was too complex to be pressed in a single operation. Instead, it was constructed as a composite:

* **The Bowl and Pedestal:** These were pressed separately and then joined while still hot using a "wafer" of molten glass—a technique requiring precise temperature control to ensure a permanent bond without cracking.  
* **The Rim:** Applied separately to the top of the bowl.  
* **The Lid:** Unique to Belmont, the pattern on the lid was pressed on the *inside* surface. This created a smooth exterior that was easier to clean while maintaining the optical brilliance of the pattern through the glass. The finial (a round knob with a bar) was applied separately[9] .

Color Palette:  
Belmont produced the Daisy & Button line in a vibrant array of colors, capitalizing on the Victorian craze for colored glass.

* **Canary (Vaseline):** This yellow-green glass contains uranium oxide, which causes it to fluoresce bright green under ultraviolet light.  
* **Amber and Blue:** Rich, deep tones that complemented the heavy relief of the pattern[9] .

### 5.3 Novelties and Commemoratives
Belmont also catered to the whimsy of the market through the production of "novelties"—small, often humorous or commemorative items.

* **Emperor William Beer Mug:** Following the unification of Germany in 1871, Belmont produced a beer mug featuring the likeness of Emperor William I of Prussia. This item likely appealed to the large German-American population in the Ohio Valley and the Midwest[2] .  
* **Figural Salt Shakers:** The company produced salt shakers in various figural shapes, a popular collectible category[11] .

### 5.4 Other Notable Patterns
* **Dewberry (c. 1870):** A naturalistic pattern patented by **Stephen Hipkins Jr.**.8  
* **Swan (c. 1873/1884):** Reflecting the Victorian fascination with nature, this pattern featured swan motifs, likely on covered dishes[8] .  
* **Giant Bull's Eye (No. 151):** Also known as "Excelsior," this was a heavy, geometric pattern often used for barware due to its durability and optical qualities[2] .  
* **Aida (c. 1880s):** A crystal pattern that showcased the clarity of the company’s lead-free glass[8] .


**6\. The "Racine" Misattribution: A Case Study in Identification**

In the field of Early American Pattern Glass, attribution is often complicated by lost records and the recycling of pattern names. A persistent error in modern database queries and collector circles is the association of the name **"Racine"** with Belmont Glass Works. Extensive analysis of the available evidence confirms that **no such pattern exists** in the Belmont catalog. This misattribution stems from three distinct sources of confusion:

### 6.1 The Metalware Confusion
The **Racine Silver Plate Company** was a manufacturer of silver-plated tablewares, including caster sets (wire or metal frames holding glass cruets for oil, vinegar, and spices). Belmont Glass Works likely supplied the glass bottles for these sets. Collectors finding a set marked "Racine" on the metal base often erroneously attribute the glass pattern to a "Racine" line by Belmont. In reality, the glass is often a standard Belmont pattern (like Daisy & Button) sitting in a holder made by the Racine Silver Plate Co..12

### 6.2 The Horlick's Jar Connection
The city of **Racine, Wisconsin**, was home to the **Horlick's Malted Milk** company. Horlick's used high-quality glass jars, often embossed with "Racine, Wis," to package their powder. These jars are frequently collected alongside EAPG. The proximity of "Racine" jars and Belmont glass in antique listings has led to a conflation of the city of origin with a pattern name[13] .

### 6.3 The Bavarian Porcelain Factor
"Racine" is a documented pattern name for Bavarian china produced by **Hutschenreuther** (often marked "Racine Bavaria"). These ceramics are often sold by the same dealers who sell EAPG, leading to keyword cross-contamination in auction listings[15] .

**Conclusion on Racine:** There is no evidence in primary source material (catalogs, patents, or trade journals) to support the existence of a Belmont "Racine" pattern. The correct attribution for the company's premier line is "Royal" or "Venus."


**7\. Biographical Profiles: The Human Element**

The history of Belmont Glass Works is inextricably linked to the biographies of the men who built it. Their lives offer a window into the social history of the 19th-century industrial workforce.

### 7.1 Henry Crimmel: Soldier, Craftsman, Manager
**Henry Crimmel (1844–1917)** stands as a titan in the history of Ohio glass. Born in Hesse, Germany, Crimmel emigrated to the United States as a child. He served in the American Civil War, a defining experience for his generation. Upon returning to civilian life, he entered the glass trade, rising from a floor worker to a master craftsman and eventually management.

Crimmel served as the plant manager for Belmont Glass Works during its final operational years. He was not merely an administrator; he was an inventor, securing a patent in 1892 for a resealable bottle stopper[11] . His expertise was such that when Belmont closed, he did not retire. Instead, he became a founding figure in the **Novelty Glass Company** of Fostoria and later the **Sneath Glass Company**, following the industry as it migrated west to the gas fields[11] . His brother, **Jacob Crimmel**, also worked at Belmont and became a seminal figure in labor history as a founder of the **American Flint Glass Workers' Union (AFGWU)**, writing extensively for the union journal *American Flint*.2

### 7.2 James S. Gill and the Ancillary Industries
**James S. Gill** represents the technical entrepreneur. One of the original 1868 incorporators, Gill remained a director until 1884\. His departure was significant: he left to establish the **J. S. Gill Glass Pot Company**.5

Glass pots are the large clay crucibles in which the glass batch is melted inside the furnace. They are consumable items with a short lifespan, requiring specialized clay and a months-long drying and firing process. By establishing a dedicated pot factory, Gill signaled the maturation of the Bellaire glass economy; the industry had grown large enough to support specialized supplier firms. His son, **William X. Gill**, followed him into the trade, managing the pot factory after James's death in 1889[5] .


**8\. The Energy Crisis and the Gas Boom (1888–1890)**

The demise of Belmont Glass Works was not caused by product failure or labor strife, but by a seismic shift in the economics of energy.

### 8.1 Coal vs. Gas
For its first two decades, Belmont relied on the coal mined in the surrounding hills. Coal provided a steady, hot flame, but it was dirty (leaving soot on the glass) and required manual stoking. In the late 1880s, massive reservoirs of **natural gas** were discovered in Northwest Ohio (around Findlay and Toledo).

Natural gas was a miracle fuel for glassmakers. It was clean, burned hotter than coal, and, most importantly, it was incredibly cheap—often offered for free or at flat rates by municipalities eager to attract industry.

### 8.2 The Economic Squeeze
Factories located in the "Gas Belt" (Northwest Ohio and Indiana) could produce glass at a fraction of the cost of the coal-dependent river factories in Bellaire. By January 1890, the strain on Belmont was evident. Trade reports noted that while Belmont produced "one of the finest lines of tableware in the country," it was forced to slash prices to compete with the gas-fueled plants. As a result, the company paid no dividends for the 1889 fiscal year[2] .

The board of directors faced a stark choice: invest massive capital to drill for local gas (which was uncertain) or liquidate while the assets still held value.


**9\. Dissolution and Diaspora (1890–1893)**

### 9.1 The Closure (June 1890\)
In June 1890, the shareholders voted to cease operations. The closure was an orderly liquidation. The machinery and inventory were sold off, and the workforce began to scatter[2] .

### 9.2 The Dispersal of the Molds
The most valuable assets—the molds—were purchased by former competitors. This dispersal explains why Belmont patterns appear in the catalogs of other companies after 1890:

* **Crystal Glass Company** (Bridgeport, OH) acquired a significant portion of the mold inventory.  
* **Central Glass Company** also purchased molds.

This transfer of intellectual property ensured the survival of the designs, even as the company that created them vanished[9] .

### 9.3 From Glass to Steel
The physical plant did not survive. The buildings were demolished, and by 1893, the site at 34th and Hamilton Streets was redeveloped by the **Novelty Stamping Company**, a metalworking firm. This transition mirrored the broader industrial trend of the region, as the steel and metal fabrication industries began to eclipse glass manufacturing in the Ohio Valley[2] .


**10\. Conclusion**

The Belmont Glass Works stands as a microcosm of the American industrial experience in the Gilded Age. Born from the cooperative spirit of returning Civil War veterans, it helped transform Bellaire into the "Glass City," setting standards of excellence in mold making and design that rippled throughout the industry. Its "Royal" and "Daisy & Button" patterns remain enduring icons of Victorian taste, technically sophisticated and artistically vibrant.

However, Belmont’s history is also a cautionary tale of technological disruption. The company survived fire and flood, but it could not survive the economic geography of the Gas Boom. When the center of gravity for glass manufacturing shifted to the natural gas fields of Northwest Ohio, Belmont, tethered to the coal economy of the river, was left behind. Yet, through the migration of men like Henry Crimmel and the preservation of its molds by rival firms, the DNA of Belmont Glass Works survived, woven into the fabric of the American glass tradition.
