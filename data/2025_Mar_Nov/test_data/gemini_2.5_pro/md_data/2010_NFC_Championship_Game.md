# 2010 NFC Championship Game: The Battle for the George Halas Trophy
## Introduction
The 2010 NFC Championship Game, played on January 23, 2011, remains one of the most significant contests in the history of the National Football League (NFL). It marked only the second postseason meeting between the league's oldest and most storied rivals, the Green Bay Packers and the Chicago Bears, in their 90-year shared history[1] . The game, held at Soldier Field in Chicago, Illinois, determined the National Football Conference (NFC) representative for Super Bowl XLV.

The contest was a collision of contrasting identities and paths. The Chicago Bears, champions of the NFC North, entered as the second seed with an 11–5 record, relying on a stout defense and opportunistic special teams play[3] . The Green Bay Packers, the sixth seed with a 10–6 record, arrived as resilient road warriors, having overcome significant injuries to battle through the Wild Card and Divisional rounds[4] .

The game is etched in NFL lore not only for the final score—a 21–14 Packers victory—but for the dramatic narratives that unfolded within the sixty minutes of play. These included the early dominance of Packers quarterback Aaron Rodgers, the controversial exit of Bears quarterback Jay Cutler due to a knee injury, the unexpected and nearly heroic performance of third-string quarterback Caleb Hanie, and the decisive interception return for a touchdown by Packers defensive tackle B.J. Raji[5] . The victory propelled the Packers to Super Bowl XLV, where they would secure their fourth Super Bowl title, while the loss marked a turning point for the Lovie Smith era in Chicago[8] .

## Background and Context
### The Historic Rivalry
To understand the magnitude of the 2010 NFC Championship Game, one must examine the depth of the Bears–Packers rivalry. Dating back to 1921, it is the longest-running rivalry in NFL history. By the end of the 2010 season, the two franchises had met over 180 times in the regular season. However, their paths had crossed in the postseason only once before: the 1941 NFL Western Division Playoff, where the Bears defeated the Packers 33–14 en route to an NFL championship[9] .

For nearly seven decades, despite the geographic proximity and frequency of their meetings, the two teams rarely peaked simultaneously. The 2010 season broke this trend, setting the stage for a matchup that fans and media dubbed the biggest game in the history of the rivalry[10] . The winner would not only advance to the Super Bowl but would also claim the George Halas Trophy—named after the Bears' legendary founder—on the loser's home field, adding a layer of symbolic weight to the contest[11] .

### 2010 Green Bay Packers Season
The 2010 Green Bay Packers season was defined by adversity. The team faced an avalanche of injuries, placing 16 players on Injured Reserve, including key starters like running back Ryan Grant and tight end Jermichael Finley[4] . Despite a depleted roster, the Packers never lost a game by more than four points and never trailed by more than seven points at any time during the regular season, a testament to their competitiveness[4] .

Quarterback Aaron Rodgers continued his ascent to the elite tier of NFL passers, throwing for 3,922 yards and 28 touchdowns[12] . However, the defense, coordinated by Dom Capers, was the team's stabilizing force. The unit ranked second in the league in scoring defense, allowing just 15.0 points per game[12] . Pro Bowl selections from the defense included linebacker Clay Matthews, who recorded 13.5 sacks, and defensive backs Charles Woodson, Tramon Williams, and Nick Collins[12] .

Entering the playoffs as the sixth seed, the Packers embraced a "road warrior" mentality. They defeated the Philadelphia Eagles 21–16 in the Wild Card Round and then dismantled the top-seeded Atlanta Falcons 48–21 in the Divisional Round, a game in which Rodgers delivered a near-perfect performance[4] .

### 2010 Chicago Bears Season
The Chicago Bears, led by head coach Lovie Smith, rebounded from consecutive mediocre seasons to reclaim the NFC North title. Their 11–5 record secured the conference's second seed and a first-round bye[3] . The Bears' identity was forged by their defense, which ranked fourth in the NFL in points allowed[15] . The offseason signing of defensive end Julius Peppers provided the pass rush necessary to complement the linebacker corps of Brian Urlacher and Lance Briggs[15] .

Offensively, the Bears were inconsistent. Quarterback Jay Cutler, acquired in a blockbuster trade the previous year, showed flashes of brilliance but struggled with turnovers and protection. He was sacked a league-high 52 times and threw 16 interceptions during the regular season[15] . Offensive coordinator Mike Martz, known for his "Greatest Show on Turf" schemes, often clashed with the personnel limitations of the Bears' offensive line. Despite these offensive hurdles, the Bears defeated the Seattle Seahawks 35–24 in the Divisional Round to set up the championship clash[3] .

### Regular Season Meetings
The two regular-season meetings in 2010 split the series 1-1, fueling the intrigue for the rubber match:

* **Week 3 (September 27, 2010):** The Bears defeated the Packers 20–17 at Soldier Field. The game was notable for the Bears committing 18 penalties yet still winning, thanks largely to a Devin Hester punt return touchdown and the Packers' inability to capitalize on mistakes[1] .  
* **Week 17 (January 2, 2011):** In a "win-and-in" scenario for Green Bay, the Packers defeated the Bears 10–3 at Lambeau Field. The Bears, having already clinched their playoff seed, played their starters but could not muster an offensive touchdown against the Packers' suffocating defense. This victory secured the Packers' Wild Card berth[5] .

## Pre-Game Logistics and Atmosphere
### Venue and Weather
The game was hosted at Soldier Field on the lakefront of Chicago. Known for its difficult playing conditions late in the season, the stadium lived up to its reputation. The game-time temperature was recorded at 20°F (-7°C), with winds blowing at 14 miles per hour, creating a biting wind chill that affected the kicking game and deep passing accuracy[5] . The playing surface, often criticized for its poor quality late in the year, was hard and slick, influencing footing for players on both sides[16] .

### Officiating Crew
The game was officiated by referee Terry McAulay and his crew[5] . McAulay, a veteran official, presided over a game that would feature several controversial moments and replay reviews. The officiating crew's tendency to let players play physically in the secondary was a talking point leading up to the game, given the aggressive nature of both defensive backfields[17] .

### Media and Broadcast
The game was broadcast nationally in the United States on FOX. The announcing team consisted of Joe Buck (play-by-play) and Troy Aikman (color commentary), with Pam Oliver and Chris Myers reporting from the sidelines[5] . The broadcast drew massive ratings, with a 28.1 household rating and approximately 51.9 million viewers tuning in, making it one of the most-watched conference championship games in history at the time[18] .

## Game Summary: Play-by-Play Analysis
### First Quarter: Packers Strike First
The Bears won the coin toss but deferred, giving Aaron Rodgers and the Packers the ball to start the game. Green Bay wasted no time establishing a rhythm.

* **Opening Drive:** Rodgers orchestrated a masterful 7-play, 84-yard drive. He found Greg Jennings for a 22-yard gain and then immediately hit him again for a 26-yard completion, exploiting the seams in the Bears' zone coverage[5] .  
* **The Score:** The drive culminated with Rodgers rolling out on a bootleg to his right. Finding no receivers open, he scrambled into the end zone for a 1-yard touchdown run. Mason Crosby's extra point made it **7–0 Packers** with 10:50 remaining in the first quarter[5] .

The Bears' offense, conversely, started sluggishly. Jay Cutler and the offensive line struggled against the Packers' disguised blitzes and coverage rotations. The Bears managed only short gains and were forced to punt repeatedly. The first quarter ended with the Packers maintaining their 7–0 lead and dominating the field position battle[5] .

### Second Quarter: Missed Opportunities and Extending the Lead
The second quarter saw the Packers extend their lead while the Bears' offense continued to flounder.

* **Starks' Touchdown:** Following a stalled drive near midfield, the Packers mounted another scoring threat. Rookie running back James Starks, who had emerged as a postseason workhorse, carried the load. Starks capped a 5-play, 44-yard drive with a 4-yard touchdown run, extending the lead to **14–0** with 11:13 left in the half[5] .

The Bears' offense appeared disjointed. Jay Cutler missed several throws, and the running game with Matt Forte found little traction against B.J. Raji and the Packers' defensive front.

* **Turnovers:** The quarter was marred by turnovers. Rodgers threw an interception to Bears linebacker Lance Briggs, giving Chicago life. However, the Bears immediately squandered the opportunity when Cutler launched a deep pass intended for Johnny Knox that was intercepted by Packers rookie cornerback Sam Shields[5] . This marked the beginning of a remarkable day for Shields.  
* **Halftime Score:** The Packers took a **14–0** lead into the locker room. The stats were lopsided, with Green Bay outgaining Chicago significantly in total yardage[20] .

### Third Quarter: The Turning Point
The third quarter was a defensive struggle that featured no scoring but arguably the most consequential moment of the game.

* **Cutler's Exit:** Early in the quarter, Jay Cutler suffered a knee injury. He attempted to play through it for a series but was visibly limited in his mobility and ability to plant his leg for throws. With the Bears trailing 14–0 and the offense stagnant, Lovie Smith made the decision to pull Cutler from the game[5] .  
* **Todd Collins Enters:** Cutler was replaced by veteran backup Todd Collins. Collins' performance was disastrous. He went 0-for-4 passing and looked immobile in the pocket, taking sacks and failing to move the chains[20] . The Bears went three-and-out on consecutive possessions, and the Soldier Field crowd began to rain boos down on the home team.  
* **Packers Stagnation:** Despite the Bears' offensive ineptitude, the Packers failed to put the game away. Rodgers struggled to find a rhythm in the third quarter, as the Bears' defense tightened. Rodgers threw a second interception, this time to Brian Urlacher near the goal line, preventing a potential knockout blow[5] .

### Fourth Quarter: The Caleb Hanie Show
With the game slipping away and Collins ineffective, Lovie Smith turned to third-string quarterback Caleb Hanie to start the fourth quarter. Hanie, largely unknown to the general public, injected immediate energy into the game.

Bears First Scoring Drive:  
Hanie led an impressive 8-play, 67-yard drive. He displayed mobility and a willingness to throw downfield that had been missing all game. The drive ended with a 1-yard touchdown run by Chester Taylor, cutting the Packers' lead to 14–7 with 12:02 remaining[5] .  
The B.J. Raji Moment:  
After the Bears' defense forced a Packers punt, Hanie had the ball with a chance to tie the game. He drove the Bears deep into their own territory. Facing a critical 3rd-and-short situation from his own 15-yard line, Hanie dropped back to pass.

* **The Play:** Packers defensive coordinator Dom Capers called a zone blitz, dropping nose tackle B.J. Raji into coverage—a tactic designed to confuse the quarterback. Hanie, failing to see the 337-pound lineman, threw a short pass intended for the flat.  
* **The Return:** Raji intercepted the pass and rumbled 18 yards into the end zone for a touchdown. He celebrated with a hip-shaking dance that became an instant viral sensation. The score made it **21–7** with 6:04 left[7] .

Bears Response:  
Refusing to yield, Hanie led another quick scoring drive. In just four plays covering 60 yards, he connected with receiver Earl Bennett for a 35-yard touchdown pass. The score was now 21–14 with 4:43 remaining[5] .  
The Final Stand:  
The Bears' defense forced a three-and-out from the Packers, giving Hanie the ball back with one last chance to tie the game. The Bears drove to the Packers' 29-yard line inside the two-minute warning.

* **The Interception:** On a decisive fourth down (or late down sequence), Hanie threw a pass intended for Johnny Knox. Sam Shields jumped the route and intercepted the ball—his second pick of the game—effectively sealing the victory for Green Bay[5] .  
* **Kneel Down:** Aaron Rodgers kneeled out the remaining clock. The Green Bay Packers were NFC Champions.

## The Jay Cutler Controversy
The most enduring and contentious narrative to emerge from the game was the reaction to Jay Cutler's injury. Unlike modern NFL protocols where injury decisions are strictly regulated and communicated, the ambiguity of Cutler's exit in 2011 created a firestorm of criticism.

### The Injury
Cutler suffered a Grade II MCL sprain in his left knee[26] . While not a season-ending injury in a regular context, the instability it caused made it impossible for him to plant his leg and throw effectively. However, Cutler remained on the sideline for the rest of the game, wearing a heavy parka and standing with his teammates, rather than receiving treatment in the locker room or appearing on crutches. This visual fueled speculation that he had "quit" on his team[21] .

### Social Media Backlash
Twitter, then a burgeoning platform for real-time sports commentary, exploded with vitriol from current and former NFL players.

* **Maurice Jones-Drew (Jaguars RB):** "All I'm saying is that he can finish the game on a hurt knee I played the whole season on one." 6  
* **Deion Sanders (NFL Analyst):** "Im telling u in the playoffs u must drag me off the field... folks i never question a players injury but i do question a players heart." 6  
* **Darnell Dockett (Cardinals DL):** "If I'm on Chicago team Jay Cutler has to wait till me and the team shower get dressed and leave before he comes in the locker room\!" 6

### Defense and Fallout
The Bears organization fiercely defended their quarterback. Linebacker Brian Urlacher stated post-game, "Jay was hurt. I don't question his toughness, he's tough as hell."[26] . Head coach Lovie Smith emphasized that the decision to pull Cutler was made by the medical staff, not the player[26] .

Despite the medical validity of the injury, the "quitter" narrative dogged Cutler for the remainder of his career. It became a defining aspect of his legacy in Chicago, overshadowing his statistical achievements as the franchise's all-time leading passer[21] .

## Caleb Hanie's Moment in the Sun
Caleb Hanie's performance stands as one of the great "what ifs" in Chicago sports history. Entering the NFC Championship Game in the fourth quarter having thrown only 14 passes in the regular season, Hanie nearly engineered a historic comeback.

His stat line—13 of 20 for 153 yards and a touchdown—was impressive given the circumstances. He showed a fearlessness that the offense lacked under Cutler and Collins. However, his inexperience proved fatal on the two interceptions. The first, to B.J. Raji, was a result of failing to identify the zone drop by the defensive lineman. The second, to Sam Shields, was a forced throw into coverage in a desperate situation.

Hanie's performance earned him temporary acclaim, but he was unable to replicate this success in subsequent seasons, struggling when given starting opportunities the following year[28] .

## Aftermath and Legacy
### Green Bay Packers
The victory secured the Packers' place in Super Bowl XLV, where they faced the AFC Champion Pittsburgh Steelers. On February 6, 2011, the Packers defeated the Steelers 31–25. Aaron Rodgers was named Super Bowl MVP[8] . The 2010 Packers became the first NFC sixth seed to win the Super Bowl and only the second sixth seed in NFL history to do so (following the 2005 Steelers)[4] . The championship cemented Mike McCarthy's status as a winning coach and validated General Manager Ted Thompson's draft-and-develop philosophy.

### Chicago Bears
The loss marked the beginning of the end for the Lovie Smith era. While the Bears remained competitive, finishing 8–8 in 2011 and 10–6 in 2012, they failed to make the playoffs in either season. Smith was fired following the 2012 season[3] . The team would not return to the playoffs until 2018, entering a period of rebuilding and instability[3] .

### Impact on the Rivalry
The 2010 NFC Championship Game fundamentally shifted the balance of the Bears–Packers rivalry. In the years following this game, Green Bay dominated the series, winning 12 of the next 14 matchups between 2011 and 2016[10] . The image of the Packers celebrating the Halas Trophy presentation on the Soldier Field turf remains a defining memory for both fanbases—a moment of ultimate triumph for Green Bay and bitter disappointment for Chicago[1] .
