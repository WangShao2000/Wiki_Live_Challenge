# Robert Jacomb-Hood
## Lead Section
**Robert Jacomb-Hood** MICE (25 January 1822 – 10 May 1900\) was a distinguished British civil engineer and railway administrator who played a pivotal role in the development of the railway infrastructure of southern England during the Victorian era[1] . Serving as the Chief Resident Engineer of the London, Brighton and South Coast Railway (LB\&SCR) from 1846 to 1860, he was responsible for the design and execution of some of the company's most significant works, including the construction of the original LB\&SCR terminus at London Victoria station, the extensive enlargement of London Bridge station, and the engineering of a comprehensive network of branch lines that integrated the rural districts of Sussex and Surrey into the national rail system[1] . His career, which spanned nearly six decades, coincided with the "Golden Age" of railway expansion, bridging the gap between the pioneering "Railway Mania" of the 1840s and the mature, consolidated industrial networks of the late 19th century[1] .

Born into a family of the landed gentry in Bedfordshire, he was originally named Robert Jacomb. He adopted the additional surname Hood in his youth following his father's inheritance of the Bardon Park estate in Leicestershire, a bequest contingent upon the assumption of the Hood name[5] . Educated at the prestigious Christ's Hospital and briefly at Trinity College, Cambridge, Jacomb-Hood abandoned a planned career in the law to pursue engineering, apprenticing under the renowned George Watson Buck[1] . His rise in the profession was meteoric; he was appointed Resident Engineer of the newly amalgamated LB\&SCR at the age of just twenty-four, a testament to the fluidity and opportunity present in the burgeoning engineering profession of the mid-19th century[1] .

Beyond his work as a practitioner of railway construction, Jacomb-Hood was a significant figure in the intellectual life of the engineering community. He was elected a Member of the Institution of Civil Engineers (ICE) in 1847 and remained active within the organization for fifty-three years[2] . He was a recognized authority on the mechanics of movable bridges and the architectural theory of railway stations. His contributions to the field were formally recognized with two of the Institution's highest accolades: the Council Premium of Books in 1850 for his innovative design of a hydraulic vertical lift bridge over the Grand Surrey Canal, and the prestigious Telford Medal in 1858 for his seminal paper *On the Arrangement and Construction of Railway Stations*.1 This latter work is considered a foundational text in the discipline, advocating for the primacy of engineering functionality over purely decorative architectural styles in industrial design[8] .

In his later career, Jacomb-Hood transitioned from direct engineering oversight to corporate governance. Following his resignation as Resident Engineer in 1860, he served as a director for a diverse portfolio of companies, including the LB\&SCR, the Anglo-Maltese Hydraulic Dock Company, and the Danish Gas Company[9] . He remained a central figure in the administration of the London, Brighton and South Coast Railway until his death in 1900[2] . He was the patriarch of a notable family; his children included the celebrated Victorian artist George Percy Jacomb-Hood and the civil engineer John Wykeham Jacomb-Hood, who served as Chief Engineer of the London and South Western Railway[1] . Robert Jacomb-Hood's legacy remains imprinted on the physical landscape of London and the South East, where the alignments of the railways he laid out continue to serve as arterial transport routes in the 21st century.

## Ancestry, Early Life, and Education
### The Hood Lineage and Bardon Park
The family history of Robert Jacomb-Hood is deeply entwined with the history of the Bardon Park estate in Leicestershire, a connection that provided the social and financial foundation for his career. The Hood family had been established at Bardon Park since at least the late 16th century. Historical records indicate that a Thomas Hood married Elizabeth in 1574, marking the beginning of the family's recorded tenure on the estate[5] . The estate itself, which included the prominent Bardon Hill—the highest point in Leicestershire—and Bardon Hall, was a significant landholding that conferred the status of gentry upon its owners.

The lineage remained unbroken at Bardon for over two centuries. The family navigated the turbulent periods of English history, including the English Civil War and the Restoration, maintaining their position as local notables. By the early 19th century, the estate was in the possession of William Hood. William Hood was a figure of considerable longevity and local standing, living to nearly ninety years of age. However, he lacked a direct male heir to continue the Hood name at Bardon Park. Upon his death on 16 May 1833, the direct male line of the Hoods of Bardon became extinct[5] .

### The Jacomb Connection and the 1833 Inheritance
The transmission of the Bardon Park estate to the Jacomb family was the result of a complex network of professional and marital alliances formed in London during the 18th century. The connection originated with Robert's grandfather, William Jacomb, a London solicitor. William Jacomb was in professional partnership with John Hood, the father of the William Hood mentioned above. This professional bond was cemented by a double marriage: the business partners married two sisters. William Jacomb married Mary Snell, while John Hood married Cecelia Snell; both women were granddaughters of Sir Edmund Harrison[5] .

This genealogical web meant that Robert Jacomb (the father of the subject of this article) was the first cousin once removed of William Hood. Additionally, there was a further link through Robert Jacomb's father, the Reverend Robert Jacomb, whose first wife, Sarah Danvers, was a cousin of William Hood[5] . When William Hood drafted his will, he selected his cousin Robert Jacomb as the heir to the Bardon Park estate. However, consistent with the common practice of the time for preserving ancient family names, the bequest was conditional. To inherit the estate, Robert Jacomb was required to assume the surname Hood[12] .

Following William Hood's death in 1833, Robert Jacomb (Senior) and his wife Susan complied with the terms of the will, adopting the hyphenated surname **Jacomb-Hood**. The inheritance transformed the family's fortunes. Previously of respectable but modest professional background—Robert Jacomb-Hood (the subject) was born in Riseley, Bedfordshire, on 25 January 1822 1—the family ascended to the ranks of the landed gentry.

In 1834, the family relocated to Bardon Park. The existing manor house, an ancient hall situated on a moated island in the valley, was found to be in a state of severe dilapidation. Robert Jacomb-Hood (Senior) undertook a significant program of modernization. In 1836, he commissioned the construction of a "grand new hall" on the hillside, a location that offered better drainage and commanding views of the estate. The family took residence in this new mansion in 1837, and the medieval moated hall was demolished around 1840, leaving only the moat as a testament to its existence[5] . This period of architectural renewal and social elevation formed the backdrop of young Robert Jacomb-Hood's adolescence.

### Education at Christ's Hospital
Robert Jacomb-Hood received his formal secondary education at **Christ's Hospital**, one of England's most historic public schools[4] . Founded in 1552 by King Edward VI, the school is famous for its distinctive Tudor-style uniform of long blue coats and yellow socks. During the 1830s, when Jacomb-Hood attended (he was a student there for six years 13), the school was located in Newgate Street in the City of London, before its later move to Horsham.

The education at Christ's Hospital in the early 19th century was rigorous and classical, but it also had a strong tradition of mathematical instruction, particularly in the Royal Mathematical School (a subset of the institution founded by Charles II to train navigators). While it is not recorded if Jacomb-Hood was a "Mathemats" boy, the school's curriculum would have provided him with a solid grounding in the disciplined thinking and mathematical principles that were prerequisites for a career in engineering.

### Trinity College, Cambridge, and the Rejection of Law
Upon completing his schooling, Jacomb-Hood faced the expectations of his father, who desired him to pursue a career in the law. A legal career was viewed as a prestigious and stable profession, suitable for the eldest son of a landed family. Deferring to paternal authority, Jacomb-Hood was admitted to **Trinity College, Cambridge**, on 25 May 1840[1] . He is listed in the university records (*Alumni Cantabrigienses*) as a "pensioner" (a student who paid for his own commons and tuition, distinct from scholars or sizars) and was described as being "of Bardon Park, Leics"[1] .

Jacomb-Hood kept several terms at Cambridge during 1840 and 1841\. However, his time at the university coincided with the early phases of the "Railway Mania," a period of intense speculation and industrial development in Britain. Through his social circles, he was introduced to young men who were engaged in railway construction projects in the Midlands. These peers, who were working on the front lines of the industrial revolution, imbued him with a fascination for engineering[4] .

The contrast between the abstract, historical study of law at Cambridge and the tangible, dynamic world of railway engineering proved decisive. The construction of railways was the "Silicon Valley" of the 1840s—the sector where the most exciting technological and economic progress was happening. Jacomb-Hood found himself unable to reconcile his growing passion for mechanics and construction with the dry pursuit of legal statutes. In April 1841, after what has been described as a "couple of unsuccessful terms" in terms of his engagement with the subject matter, he made the bold decision to drop out of the University of Cambridge[1] . He successfully persuaded his father to allow him to switch vocations, marking the beginning of his journey as a civil engineer.

## Apprenticeship and Early Career (1841–1846)
### Articled Pupil to George Watson Buck
In 1841, having secured his father's reluctant blessing, Robert Jacomb-Hood entered into an articled pupilage with **George Watson Buck**.4 This placement was a coup for an aspiring engineer. Buck was one of the premier engineers of the day, a man who had authored authoritative texts on the construction of oblique arches (skew bridges) and was serving as the Chief Engineer of the **Manchester and Birmingham Railway (M\&BR)** in conjunction with the legendary Robert Stephenson[4] .

The apprenticeship system was the primary method of training for engineers in the Victorian era, as university degrees in engineering were not yet commonplace. As a pupil, Jacomb-Hood would have been immersed in the day-to-day realities of railway construction: surveying routes, calculating earthwork volumes, drafting plans for bridges and culverts, and supervising contractors.

### The Manchester and Birmingham Railway
Jacomb-Hood's training took place on the Manchester and Birmingham Railway, a line designed to provide a direct link between Manchester and the south via Crewe.

* **Holmes Chapel Section:** His first assignment was as an Assistant on the Holmes Chapel length of the line. Here, he worked under **William Baker**, a rising star who would later become the Chief Engineer of the London and North Western Railway (LNWR)[2] . Working under Baker provided Jacomb-Hood with a mentor who exemplified the highest standards of the profession.  
* **Crewe Section:** He subsequently moved to the Crewe length of the line, working under **W. H. Barlow**.2 Barlow was another giant of the industry, who would go on to design the train shed at St Pancras and serve as President of the Institution of Civil Engineers.

The exposure to these figures—Buck, Baker, and Barlow—meant that Jacomb-Hood's "university" was the muddy construction sites of Cheshire, and his "professors" were the men inventing the discipline of railway engineering in real-time.

### Professional Advancement
When the M\&BR opened its line to Crewe in 1842, George Watson Buck resigned from the company to establish a private consulting practice in Manchester. Recognizing Jacomb-Hood's talent and reliability, Buck hired his former pupil as his **Chief Assistant**.2 In this capacity, Jacomb-Hood took on greater responsibility, managing projects such as the rebuilding of the **New Bailey Bridge** across the River Irwell in Salford[2] . This project would have given him his first independent experience with bridge engineering, a sub-discipline in which he would later excel.

He later returned to the staff of the railway company (now transitioning into the London and North Western Railway orbit) to work again with William Baker. He was charged with the construction of the **Macclesfield Branch**, a project that required navigating the hilly terrain on the edge of the Peak District[2] .

### Resident Engineer: The Altrincham Line (1844–1846)
In 1844, at the remarkably young age of twenty-two, Jacomb-Hood was appointed **Resident Engineer** of the **Manchester, South Junction and Altrincham Railway (MSJ\&AR)**.1 The MSJ\&AR was a pioneering line, often cited as one of the first true suburban railways designed to commute passengers from the leafy outskirts of Altrincham into the industrial heart of Manchester.

Jacomb-Hood held this post for two years. During this time, he was responsible for the entire physical plant of the railway, overseeing track maintenance, station construction, and signaling. He also found time to assist William Baker on surveys for the **Shropshire Union Canal** and the **Wilmslow & Knutsford Railway**, broadening his experience to include hydraulic engineering and further railway surveying[1] .

By 1846, Jacomb-Hood had matured from a raw apprentice into a competent and experienced engineer. His reputation in the North was solid, but the rapidly expanding networks in the South of England beckoned. He turned down an offer to work on the Leeds Northern Railway and declined a partnership with William Baker—a decision that speaks to his ambition and desire for autonomy—to apply for a new and challenging role in London[1] .

## The London, Brighton and South Coast Railway (1846–1860)
### Appointment as Resident Engineer
The year 1846 was a watershed moment in British railway history, marked by a frenzy of amalgamations. On 27 July 1846, the London and Brighton Railway merged with the London and Croydon Railway to form the **London, Brighton and South Coast Railway (LB\&SCR)**.7 This new entity controlled a monopoly on rail traffic between London and the Sussex coast, but it inherited a disjointed network and a chaotic management structure.

Robert Jacomb-Hood was appointed as the first **Resident Engineer** of the LB\&SCR immediately following the amalgamation[1] . He was twenty-four years old. The appointment placed him in technical command of a mainline network that included difficult engineering features such as the Ouse Valley Viaduct and the Clayton Tunnel, as well as the busy commuter lines of South London.

The early years of the LB\&SCR were fraught with difficulty. The company's first chairman, C. P. Grenfell, and the first manager, Peter Clarke, struggled to stabilize the company's finances. In 1848, Clarke resigned amidst accusations of inefficiency and strong complaints[7] . Jacomb-Hood navigated this corporate turbulence with skill. While the company's finances were "tumultuous"—oscillating between strong gains and near-insolvency—Jacomb-Hood focused on the technical efficiency of the line. There were moments where his position was precarious, and he "narrowly escaped being fired" during cost-cutting purges, but his competence made him indispensable[1] .

### Expansion of the Network (1850–1860)
As the 1850s dawned, the LB\&SCR moved from a period of retrenchment to one of aggressive expansion. The company sought to secure its territory against incursions from the rival South Eastern Railway (SER) and the London and South Western Railway (LSWR). Jacomb-Hood was the general who executed this strategy on the ground, surveying and building a web of branch lines that filled in the "gaps" on the map of Sussex and Surrey.

**Key Infrastructure Projects (1850–1860):**

| Project Name | Significance & Function |
| :---- | :---- |
| **Crystal Palace Branch** | Built to serve the relocated Crystal Palace at Sydenham (1854). This line was a massive revenue generator, carrying millions of excursionists to the exhibition grounds. It required heavy earthworks to reach the high ground of Sydenham Hill. 2 |
| **East Grinstead Branch** | Connected the market town of East Grinstead to the main line at Three Bridges. This was the first stage of what would become a tangled network of lines in the High Weald. 2 |
| **Midhurst Branch** | Penetrated the rural western Weald, serving the market town of Midhurst. This line was strategic, blocking LSWR advances from the west. 2 |
| **Lewes and Uckfield Line** | Improved connectivity in East Sussex, linking the county town of Lewes with the agricultural hinterland of Uckfield. 2 |
| **Shoreham and Henfield Line** | Also known as the "Steyning Line," this route followed the Adur Valley, connecting the port of Shoreham with the main line at Horsham. 2 |
| **Newhaven Branch** | Developed to serve the port of Newhaven, which the LB\&SCR was developing as a packet port for ferries to Dieppe, establishing the "shorter" route to Paris. 2 |
| **Hailsham & Eastbourne** | Completed the coastal network, facilitating the growth of Eastbourne from a quiet hamlet into a major Victorian seaside resort. 2 |
| **Croydon & Wandsworth** | Strengthened the suburban commuter network in South London, an area of rapid residential growth. 2 |

In addition to these new lines, Jacomb-Hood oversaw major capital works on existing infrastructure. He managed the enlargement of **Brighton Station**, adapting the original Mocatta design to handle increased traffic. He also constructed the **Bricklayers Arms Goods Station**, a massive freight depot in Bermondsey that served as the primary goods terminal for the railway, relieving congestion at London Bridge[2] .

### The Construction of London Victoria Station
The defining achievement of Jacomb-Hood's career as Resident Engineer was the creation of **London Victoria Station**. By the late 1850s, the LB\&SCR's terminus at London Bridge was becoming increasingly inadequate. It was located in the City, far from the centers of political and social power in Westminster and the West End. To capture the lucrative traffic of the upper classes and the government elite, the LB\&SCR needed a terminus in the West End[3] .

#### The Victoria Station & Pimlico Railway
The solution was the Victoria Station & Pimlico Railway, a legally separate company authorized to build a line from the south bank of the Thames across the river to a site near Buckingham Palace Road. The project involved the construction of the **Grosvenor Bridge**, the first railway bridge to cross the Thames in central London[3] .

The station site itself, covering 14 acres, was developed as a joint terminus for two competing railway companies: the LB\&SCR (occupying the western side) and the London, Chatham and Dover Railway (LCDR) (occupying the eastern side). While they shared a name and a location, they were effectively two distinct stations built side-by-side[3] .

#### Jacomb-Hood’s Design
Robert Jacomb-Hood was the engineer responsible for the LB\&SCR's half of Victoria Station. His design philosophy was driven by a combination of structural pragmatism and economic discipline.

* **Layout:** His station comprised six platforms and ten tracks, covering an area of 8.5 acres (3.4 hectares). It measured 800 feet in length and 230 feet in width[14] .  
* **Roof Structure:** In contrast to the heavy, monumental roofs popular at the time (such as the one designed by Sir John Fowler for the LCDR side, or the later St Pancras), Jacomb-Hood utilized a lighter, more efficient system. He employed a **single-truss design** supported by wrought iron girders. This design allowed for a spacious, column-free interior that maximized operational flexibility.  
* **Cost Efficiency:** The genius of Jacomb-Hood's design lay in its economy. By applying his principles of "judicious arrangement," he succeeded in constructing the roof for just **£17 per 100 square feet** (9.3 m²). This translated to a total cost that was only two-thirds of the expenditure incurred by the LCDR for their adjacent roof[1] .

The LB\&SCR station opened to passengers on **1 October 1860**, nearly two years before the LCDR station was completed[3] . The design included thoughtful touches for the station's prestigious neighbors; the tracks leading from Eccleston Bridge into the station were roofed over to protect the residents of Pimlico and Belgravia from steam and smoke, a concession to the political power of the local constituency[3] .

Although Jacomb-Hood's original roof was replaced in 1908 during the station's Edwardian rebuilding, his layout established Victoria as the premier gateway to the South Coast and the Continent, a status it retains today[3] .

## Contributions to Engineering Science
Robert Jacomb-Hood was not merely a builder; he was a thinker who contributed to the theoretical advancement of civil engineering. He was a devoted member of the **Institution of Civil Engineers (ICE)**, the world's first professional engineering body. Elected a Member on 2 March 1847, he remained a part of the institution for over half a century[2] .

### The Grand Surrey Canal Vertical Lift Bridge (1850)
One of his earliest contributions to engineering literature arose from a challenging problem on the Thames Junction Branch of the LB\&SCR. The line needed to cross the **Grand Surrey Canal** near New Cross. The canal act required a clear navigation width of 54 feet and a specific headway for barges, but the railway's gradient was too low to allow for a fixed bridge at the required height[15] .

Jacomb-Hood devised a novel solution: a **vertical lift bridge**. Unlike a swing bridge (which rotates horizontally) or a bascule bridge (which hinges upward like a drawbridge), a vertical lift bridge consists of a deck that remains horizontal while being raised vertically by counterweights and machinery.

* **Specifications:** The bridge deck weighed approximately 12 tons. In its lowered position, the rails were only 4 feet 2½ inches above the water. The mechanism allowed the bridge to be lifted to provide a clearance of 9 feet 6 inches[15] .  
* **Execution:** The bridge was constructed between October 1848 and June 1849 at a cost of £1,300 (approximately £165,000 in 2023 terms)[1] .

On 2 April 1850, Jacomb-Hood presented a paper titled *Description of a Vertical Lift Bridge erected over the Grand Surrey Canal* to the ICE. The paper detailed the mechanical linkages and the hydraulic considerations of the design. The Institution awarded him a **Council Premium of Books** on 7 November 1850, recognizing the ingenuity of the solution for crossing navigable waterways with low-level railways[1] .

### The Theory of Station Design (1858)
In 1858, Jacomb-Hood delivered his most influential paper, *On the Arrangement and Construction of Railway Stations*. This work addressed a growing tension in the Victorian industrial world: the conflict between the **Civil Engineer** and the **Architect**.

In the mid-19th century, railway stations were often designed by architects who prioritized external grandeur and classical ornamentation, sometimes at the expense of operational flow. Jacomb-Hood argued that the "correct and judicious arrangement" of a station was fundamentally an engineering problem. He regretted that station design was often "divided with directors, managers, and architects," rather than being led by engineers who understood the dynamics of train movements and passenger flows[8] .

**Key Concepts in the Paper:**

1. **Functionalism:** He advocated that the form of the station should follow its function. The roof, platforms, and concourse should be designed to facilitate the rapid movement of trains and people, not merely to impress observers.  
2. **Safety and Platform Layout:** He discussed the use of **staggered platforms** in stations where footbridges were not provided. By offsetting the platforms, passengers were forced to cross the tracks at a specific point, often behind the train, reducing the risk of accidents—a safety innovation born of hard experience[16] .  
3. **Roofing Technology:** Drawing on the lessons of the Crystal Palace and his own work, he discussed the benefits of "ridge-and-furrow" glazing and large span iron roofs to create bright, airy, and column-free interiors[17] .

This paper was awarded the **Telford Medal**, the highest honor bestowed by the ICE. The award solidified Jacomb-Hood's reputation as a leading intellectual in the field of transport infrastructure[1] .

## Later Career: The Boardroom and International Ventures (1860–1900)
By 1860, the crushing workload of the Resident Engineer's post began to take its toll. The dual burden of managing massive construction projects like Victoria Station while simultaneously handling the "parliamentary work"—the preparation of plans and testimony for railway bills in Parliament—was unsustainable. In September 1860, Jacomb-Hood resigned as Resident Engineer[2] .

However, the LB\&SCR was unwilling to lose his expertise. He was retained as a **Consulting Engineer** and was subsequently invited to join the **Board of Directors**. This marked a transition from employee to governor, a rare trajectory for an engineer of that era[2] .

### International Business Interests
Free from the daily grind of railway maintenance, Jacomb-Hood applied his engineering acumen to international business ventures.

* **The Anglo-Maltese Hydraulic Dock Company:** In 1870, he joined the board of this London-based company, which was constructing a state-of-the-art naval facility in Malta. The project involved a hydraulic lift dock capable of hoisting ironclad warships out of the water for repairs. The dock was completed in 1873 at a cost of £143,000. Jacomb-Hood traveled to Malta to supervise the company's affairs, ensuring the successful implementation of this complex hydraulic machinery in a strategic imperial outpost[9] .  
* **The Danish Gas Company:** He served for several years as the Chairman of the Danish Gas Company. In this role, he applied his knowledge of piping and pressure systems to the production and distribution of coal gas, becoming an authority on municipal gas lighting infrastructure[10] .  
* **Other Works:** He also acted as a consultant for the civil engineering aspects of the **National Gallery** building in London and the **Crystal Palace Aquarium**, demonstrating his versatility beyond heavy rail[9] .

Despite these diversions, his heart remained with the **London, Brighton and South Coast Railway**. He served on the board for the rest of his life, devoting his final seventeen years almost exclusively to the company's affairs. His colleagues on the board valued his "keen intellect, fine memory and tactful discrimination," relying on his deep institutional memory to guide the railway through the competitive pressures of the late Victorian era[2] .

## Personal Life and Family
Robert Jacomb-Hood was a family man whose domestic life was marked by both tragedy and dynastic success. He married his first wife, **Jane Stothard Littlewood**, in 1852\. She was the daughter of George Littlewood, a printer. The marriage produced several children before Jane's premature death in 1869[1] . He remarried in 1871 to **Elizabeth Thornton**, but she too died shortly after, in 1875[1] .

### Notable Descendants
Jacomb-Hood's children achieved significant prominence in their own right, continuing the family's tradition of professional excellence.

* **George Percy Jacomb-Hood MVO (1857–1929):** His son George Percy became a celebrated painter, etcher, and illustrator. A founding member of the New English Art Club, he was famous for his work with *The Graphic* magazine, which sent him on assignments around the world. He accompanied the Prince and Princess of Wales (later King George V and Queen Mary) on their 1905 tour of India and served on the King's personal staff during the 1911 Delhi Durbar. His artistic career brought a different kind of fame to the Jacomb-Hood name[11] .  
* **John Wykeham Jacomb-Hood (1859–1914):** Another son, John Wykeham, followed in his father's footsteps. He became a civil engineer and rose to the position of Chief Resident Engineer of the **London and South Western Railway (LSWR)**. In a poetic parallel to his father's career, John was responsible for the major reconstruction of **Waterloo Station** (the rival to Victoria) in the early 20th century. Like his father, he was a Member of the ICE[9] .  
* **Philanthropy:** The family maintained a tradition of charitable giving. In 1885, Robert's sister, **Eliza Jacomb-Hood**, donated a 37-foot lifeboat to the Royal National Lifeboat Institution (RNLI) station at Newbiggin-by-Sea. The boat, named the *Robert and Susan* in memory of their parents, served until 1906 and saved 79 lives[5] .

## Death and Legacy
Robert Jacomb-Hood died suddenly on **10 May 1900** at his residence in Royal Tunbridge Wells, Kent[1] . He was 78 years old. His death was unexpected; he had been in "almost full vigour of health" and had been transacting business for the Brighton Company on the very day of his passing[2] .

He was buried in Tunbridge Wells. His gravestone is inscribed with the verse from 2 Timothy 4:7: *"I have fought a good fight, I have finished my course, I have kept the faith"*—an epitaph that reflected his tenacious and principled approach to his life and work[1] .

His obituary in the *Minutes of the Proceedings of the Institution of Civil Engineers* paid tribute to him as a link to the heroic age of engineering. It noted that he was one of the oldest members of the Institution and praised his unflagging dedication to the LB\&SCR.

Historical Assessment:  
Robert Jacomb-Hood's legacy is built into the fabric of the South East of England. The railway lines he surveyed and built—from the busy commuter arteries of South London to the scenic coastal routes of Sussex—remain in heavy use today. His work at Victoria Station facilitated the westward shift of London's transport gravity, while his theoretical writings helped to professionalize the role of the railway engineer, asserting the dignity and necessity of functional design in an age often obsessed with ornamentation. He stands as a prime example of the Victorian engineer-administrator: practical, inventive, and deeply committed to the infrastructure of progress.
